What they ask

The instruction is the Canvas page "PC-lab · Sampling — instruction" of module M2 (the task sheet and the supporting sheet are handed out for the session). No hand-in: nothing is collected and nothing is graded, but the spreadsheet you build is the calculation behind every sample mass quoted in the rest of the course, and the project will ask for it. Purpose, in the page's words: to turn Gy's equation from something you can read into something you can use; by the end you should take a lot of a given mass and top size, a target sampling error and the crushers available, and design a staged sample-preparation scheme that reaches the target, and explain why the same scheme fails for a coarse-gold ore and succeeds for a disseminated iron ore.

Before the session: a laptop with spreadsheet software (the whole session is spreadsheet work); the main sampling deck from the material page open at the slides "Gy's Theory of Sampling" and "Factors in Gy's equation", where the working expressions of the form factor, the size-distribution coefficient, the liberation factor and the mineral factor are; the task sheet read once, not solved; a mineral density reference for gold, magnetite, molybdenite and the gangue; Warm-up Q2 done. Recommended: the skeleton of the spreadsheet built before you arrive, with inputs for lot mass M, sample mass ms, top size d95, grade, mineral grain size and densities, and outputs the sampling constant C and the relative standard deviation.

The session, task by task (the page's times):

  1. Warm up on minerals (15 min). Find minerals from an element list, look up the compositional range of a few ore minerals, identify a mineral from an energy-dispersive analysis: a sampling calculation needs a mineral, a grade and a density before it can start.
  2. Build and test the spreadsheet (20 min). Implement s2=Cd3(1ms−1M) and the expression for C from the slides. Test on the single-stage problem: a 100 kg drum of −1 mm material grading 8 ppm Au, largest gold grains 1 mm, ore density 2.8 g/cm³, maximum acceptable error 10 %: how large a sample? Then gold grains at 100 µm and at 10 µm.
  3. Grains, not grams (20 min). A 3 cm resin mount with a 0.1 mm exposed layer, gold grains of 50 µm, ore at 5 g/t: how many gold grains will you actually see? Then at 10 µm. Then how many grains you need for a 5 % error.
  4. Multi-stage design, coarse gold (30 min). Five tonnes at 8 g/t blasted to below 100 mm, average gold grain 200 µm; deliver 1 kg bags at −3 mm; two jaw crushers with settings adjustable over 5 to 50 mm and 0.5 to 5 mm; target error 5 %. First the naive scheme (everything to 50 mm, take 100 kg, crush to −3 mm, bag 1 kg) and how bad it is; then the sample masses that reach 5 % at 50 mm and at 3 mm; then relax to 10 % with 500 kg and 10 kg stages and find which particle sizes that forces.
  5. Multi-stage design, iron ore (20 min). The same five tonnes, crushers, 1 kg at −3 mm and 5 % target, but magnetite with a 600 µm grain size; design the scheme, put the two side by side and explain the difference from the terms in C.
  6. Audit an existing scheme (20 min). Twenty kilograms of a molybdenum ore at 0.167 % molybdenite prepared through the four stages of the table on the sheet (masses in g, particle sizes in mm): compute the error of the scheme as written, decide whether it is acceptable, revise it to reach 10 %.
  7. Wrap up (15 min). Compare answers across the room; where groups disagree it is almost always the liberation factor or the choice of d95.

What you should have by the end: a working spreadsheet returning a sampling error from lot mass, sample mass, top size, grade, grain size and densities; two staged schemes as tables (the gold case and the iron case), each row with top size, sample mass and the error of that stage, plus the combined error; the audited molybdenum scheme with its error and your revision; two or three written sentences on why the two ores need different schemes.

How the work flows

The session is one formula, wired once, then turned on six situations of increasing size. Nothing is measured: the inputs are on the task sheet, the arithmetic is Gy's equation, and what you read is a relative error. Four things have different roles:

  • Fixed for every run: Gy's equation and the factor expressions of the slides; the data of each task (lot mass, grade, grain size, densities, top sizes, the crushers' setting ranges, the target error).
  • Chosen once, in the skeleton: the units (masses in grams, sizes in centimetres inside the formula, grade as a mass fraction), the densities of gold, magnetite, molybdenite and the gangue, and one cell for each factor of C so that a wrong factor can be found later.
  • Turned in the design tasks: the sample mass and the top size of each stage, one at a time, until the error of the stage and the combined error of the scheme meet the target with the crushers you actually have.
  • Read after every change: the sampling constant, the error of the stage, the combined error, and, in the grains task, the number of grains the sample contains.
  • Compared at the end: the gold scheme against the iron scheme (the same lot, the same crushers, a different C), the naive scheme against the designed one, and the molybdenum scheme as written against your revision.

In the page's words, "turn Gy's equation into something you can use" is the spreadsheet, "design a staged scheme" is the loop on masses and top sizes, and "explain why the same scheme fails for coarse gold and succeeds for disseminated iron" is the sentence you write at the end from the terms in C: grain size and grade.

How to check a sampling error

  • Units first. Inside Gy's equation d is in centimetres and the masses in grams, so C comes out in g/cm³; a millimetre where a centimetre belongs changes the answer a thousandfold. The grade a is a mass fraction (8 g/t is 8×10−6).
  • The liberation factor cannot exceed 1. It compares the grain size of the valuable mineral with the top size; when the grains are as large as the particles it is 1, and the error is at its worst. Check it before anything else when two groups disagree.
  • Stages add as variances. The combined relative error of a staged scheme is the square root of the sum of the squared errors of its stages, not their sum; the stage with the largest top size and the smallest sample usually dominates.
  • Read the consequences off the formula. The error rises with a large top size, a small sample, a large grain size and a low grade; it falls when you crush before you split, because halving the particle size multiplies the number of particles by about eight. That is why the first stage is the expensive one and why a crusher sits before every split.
  • When M is much larger than ms, s2≈Cd3/ms: the lot mass drops out, which is why a five-tonne lot and a hundred-kilogram drum are designed the same way.

Before the session: numbers by hand

The drum, with the textbook factors. The slides give the course's expressions for the four factors of C; the usual textbook forms, used here only to see the size of the answer, are a form factor of 0.5, a size-distribution coefficient of 0.25 for a broad distribution, a liberation factor dl/d (1 when the grains are as large as the particles) and a mineral factor 1−aa[(1−a)ρm+aρg] with a the grade as a mass fraction. For the 100 kg drum at 8 ppm gold, gold at 19.3 g/cm³ in a gangue of 2.8, the mineral factor is about 2.4×106 g/cm³; with gold grains of 1 mm in −1 mm material the liberation factor is 1, so C≈0.5×0.25×1×2.4×106=3.0×105 g/cm³. With d = 0.1 cm, Cd3=300 g, and a 10 % error (s2=0.01) needs

1ms=0.01300+1100000≈4.3×10−5g−1,ms≈23kg

of a 100 kg drum. With gold grains of 100 µm the liberation factor falls to 0.32 and the sample to about 9 kg; at 10 µm to 0.1 and about 3 kg. That is the violent movement the task wants you to see, and it comes entirely from the grain size. The slides' exact expressions replace the textbook ones in the session; the shape of the answer will not change.

Grains, not grams. A 3 cm mount with a 0.1 mm layer holds about 0.7cm3 of rock, about 2 g at 2.8 g/cm³; at 5 g/t that is 10−5 g of gold, and a 50 µm grain of gold weighs about 1.3×10−6 g (a sphere of that diameter at 19.3 g/cm³), so the mount carries about eight gold grains; at 10 µm about a thousand. The relative error of a count of N grains is about 1/N, so 5 % needs about 400 grains. Do the arithmetic yourself before the session; it is the single most useful intuition the page names.

Step by step in the session

1

Warm up on minerals: a mineral, a grade, a density

ClickWork the first tasks of the sheet quickly: minerals from an element list, the compositional range of a few ore minerals, a mineral from an energy-dispersive analysis. Write the densities of gold, magnetite, molybdenite and the gangue where the spreadsheet will read them.
ExpectThe inputs a sampling calculation needs before it can start.
WhyThe page says these tasks lead straight into M3; they also stop the spreadsheet from being fed a grade without a mineral behind it.
2

Build the skeleton: inputs, the four factors, C, the error

ClickInputs: lot mass M (g), sample mass m_s (g), top size d95 (cm), grade a (mass fraction), grain size d_l (cm), densities. One cell per factor of C from the slides "Factors in Gy's equation"; C as their product; s² = C d³ (1/m_s − 1/M); the relative error as its square root.
ExpectA sheet that returns an error from six inputs, with every factor visible.
WhyA wrong factor is found in seconds when it has its own cell, and never when it is buried in one formula.
Centimetres and grams inside the formula; a mass fraction for the grade; a liberation factor capped at 1.
3

Test on the drum, then move the grain size

Click100 kg, −1 mm, 8 ppm Au, gold grains 1 mm, ore density 2.8, error 10 %: solve for m_s. Then grains of 100 µm, then 10 µm.
ExpectA sample of tens of kilograms at 1 mm grains, falling by an order of magnitude as the grains shrink; the hand numbers above show the shape.
WhyThe test both checks the wiring and shows what the liberation factor does.
If the answer is off by a factor of a thousand in either direction, a millimetre has been typed where a centimetre belongs.
4

Grains, not grams

ClickThe resin mount: 3 cm, 0.1 mm layer, 50 µm gold grains, 5 g/t: count the grains you would see; then at 10 µm; then the number of grains for a 5 % error.
ExpectA handful of grains at 50 µm, hundreds at 10 µm, and about 400 needed for 5 %.
WhySampling error is about the number of grains captured, not the mass weighed.
5

Coarse gold: quantify the naive scheme, then design

ClickFive tonnes at 8 g/t below 100 mm, grains 200 µm, bags of 1 kg at −3 mm, crushers 5 to 50 mm and 0.5 to 5 mm, target 5 %. Naive scheme first (to 50 mm, 100 kg, to −3 mm, 1 kg): compute the error of each stage and combine them. Then find the sample masses that reach 5 % at 50 mm and at 3 mm. Then relax to 10 % with 500 kg and 10 kg stages and find the particle sizes that forces.
ExpectA table with one row per stage (top size, sample mass, error) and the combined error; the naive scheme far above 5 %.
WhyThis is the design the whole session builds towards.
Combine as variances; the coarse stage dominates.
6

Iron ore: the same scheme problem with magnetite at 600 µm

ClickSame lot, crushers, bags and target; magnetite grain 600 µm and the iron ore's grade and densities. Design the scheme and put it beside the gold scheme.
ExpectA far lighter scheme, and a sentence on which terms of C changed: grain size and grade.
WhyThe comparison is the point of the session.
7

Audit the molybdenum scheme

Click20 kg at 0.167 % molybdenite through the four stages of the sheet's table (masses in g, sizes in mm): compute the error of the scheme as written, decide whether it is acceptable, revise it to 10 %.
ExpectAn error per stage, a combined error, a verdict, and a revised table.
WhyAuditing a scheme somebody else wrote is what the project will ask of a drill-hole dataset.
Convert the sheet's millimetres to centimetres before they enter the formula.
8

Wrap up: compare across the room and write the sentences

ClickCompare answers with the other groups; where they differ, find whether it is the liberation factor or the choice of d95, and say which. Write two or three sentences on why gold and iron need different schemes.
ExpectThe sentences the page says are the module.
WhyIf you can write them, you have M2.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the first drum answer as final instead of moving the grain size: the movement is the lesson.
  • Millimetres inside the formula, grade in per cent instead of a fraction, a liberation factor above 1.
  • Adding the errors of the stages instead of their variances.
  • Choosing d95 by the crusher's nominal setting without saying so; it is where groups disagree at the wrap-up.
  • Leaving the spreadsheet on the PC-lab machine: keep it, the project needs it.
