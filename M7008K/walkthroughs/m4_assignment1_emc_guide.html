Assignment 1, step by step: element-to-mineral conversion of borehole MUJ1

What they ask

The sheet is MP_Assignment 1 - Element to Mineral conversion.pdf (Mehdi Parian, 2023-09-11, one page); the Canvas assignment page of module M4 repeats it and adds what a pass requires. It is an individual hand-in, one Excel file, due Sunday 27 September at 23:59 as Canvas reads it on 9 September (the course said assignment dates may still be updated; the Canvas date is the one that counts). Point by point:

1. Purpose (section 1). To train calculating and visualising the modal mineralogy of a borehole against depth, and to make justified assumptions when the chemistry alone does not determine the mineralogy. The Canvas page says it in one line: the assumptions "are the part that is actually being examined".

2. The data (section 3). MUJ1.csv: borehole samples analysed by XRF for the oxides and by titration for FeO. Thirty columns: sample id, hole id, depth from and to, then Fe, FeO, P (twice: a calculated and a "best" column), Al2O3, As, Ba, CaO, Cl, Co, Cr2O3, Cu, K2O, MgO, Mn, Na2O, Ni, Pb, SiO2, Sn, Sr, S, TiO2, V, Zn, Zr and LOI, all in per cent by mass. The expected minerals, in the sheet's order: magnetite, hematite, apatite, albite, biotite, actinolite, titanite, barite, quartz, diopside.

3. What to do (Canvas page). Organise and format the raw assays (units, missing values, totals). Choose the minerals: take each one's chemical composition and density from the HSC Geo database and record them. Set up the rounds: which minerals are solved from which assays, in what order; where an element is shared between minerals or a mineral has variable composition, assume something, write it down, say why it is reasonable. Solve, round by round, the system Ax=b (b the element grades, A the mineral compositions, x the mineral abundances): least squares where over-determined, an added constraint or assumption where under-determined, never a silent pick. Check the calculation: residuals element by element and sample by sample. Visualise: modal mineralogy against depth as a stacked bar chart.

4. What to hand in (section 2): seven sheets, in this order. (1) names, date and assignment title; (2) organised and formatted raw data of the chemical assays; (3) the minerals selected from HSC Geo with composition and density, and the assumptions; (4) the rounds used (minerals and assays in each round); (5) the results, modal mineralogy from the conversion; (6) a graph sheet, modal mineral abundance against depth as a stacked bar chart; (7) the goodness of the calculation, including residuals.

5. What a pass requires (Canvas page). All seven sheets present, in order, readable by someone who did not build the workbook; compositions and densities recorded with their source; the rounds explicitly documented so a reader sees which assay solved which mineral; assumptions written down where the chemistry does not determine the answer, with a justification; residuals reported and commented on; a stacked bar chart that can be read (axes labelled, minerals distinguishable, depth increasing in a sensible direction).

Note

This is an individual hand-in and the assumptions are what is examined, so this guide explains the method, the data and the checks, and stops there: which assumption you make about the shared elements, and how you justify it, is your work. Check the course's FAQ for its rule on outside help before you submit.

How the work flows

The sheet lists seven sheets, so it reads like a report to fill in. The work is a calculation loop inside a bookkeeping frame: everything you record before the first solve stays fixed, the rounds are turned until the residuals are small and no abundance is negative, and the seven sheets are the record of that loop. Four things have different roles:

  • Fixed for every run: the assays of MUJ1 as you organised them once (which columns are oxides, which are elements, which two are the same phosphorus, what LOI is), the ten expected minerals, and the requirement that the mineral grades of a sample sum to about 100 %.
  • Chosen once, and recorded with their source: the chemical composition and density of each mineral from HSC Geo, on one basis (element or oxide) for the whole matrix; and the assumptions where the chemistry does not decide: how ferrous iron (the FeO column) is shared between magnetite and the iron-bearing silicates, and therefore where the magnetite/hematite split falls; how calcium is shared between apatite, actinolite, diopside and titanite; how magnesium between actinolite, diopside and biotite; how aluminium between albite and biotite.
  • Turned in the rounds: the order of the rounds, which assay solves which mineral in each, and least squares against non-negative least squares. You turn one thing per pass and read the residuals again.
  • Read after every pass: negative abundances, the residual of every element in every sample, the totals, and whether a residual is random across samples or systematic (the sign of a wrong composition).
  • Compared at the end: the measured assays against the assays back-calculated from your minerals, and the modal mineralogy against depth in the chart, where a mineral that jumps between neighbouring samples for no geological reason is a sign of an unstable round.

In the sheet's words, "justified assumptions" is the examined deliverable, "rounds" is the method (solve first what one element determines almost alone, subtract, pass the remainder on), and "goodness of calculations including residuals" is the proof that you checked. A conversion with no residual is not a good conversion; it is an unchecked one.

How to check the conversion

  • The residual is the check. For each sample, back-calculate the assays from your minerals (a=Cm) and subtract the measured ones; sheet 7 holds that table. A residual within the analytical uncertainty is fine; a large one-sided residual on one element says a host is missing or a composition is wrong; a residual that is systematic across many samples says the composition of a mineral is off, not the fit. Comment on the poor fits: the Canvas page asks for it.
  • Ferrous iron is the hinge. MUJ1 gives total iron and, from titration, FeO. Iron in magnetite is two-thirds ferric and one-third ferrous; iron in hematite is all ferric; biotite, actinolite and diopside carry ferrous iron of their own. The FeO column is therefore the only information that splits magnetite from hematite, and it is shared with the silicates, so what you assume about ferrous iron in the silicates decides the magnetite/hematite split. Write that assumption first, in sheet 3, with a reason.
  • Shared elements need a stated rule. Phosphorus, barium, titanium, potassium and sodium each point at one mineral of the list (apatite, barite, titanite, biotite, albite) and can open the rounds; calcium, magnesium, aluminium and silicon are shared by several. An under-determined element cannot be solved by arithmetic: impose closure, assume a ratio between two minerals (approximate, local, said so), or leave one mineral as the residual phase. Any of these is acceptable if it is written down; none is acceptable in silence.
  • Sanity checks that cost nothing. Totals near 100 % per sample; no negative abundance in the final round; a mineral that appears only in samples where its diagnostic element is above the detection limit; LOI treated as loss or gain on ignition, not as a mineral (negative values in the file are gains, typical of ferrous iron oxidising on heating); the two phosphorus columns used once, not twice.

Key points

  1. The assumptions are the examined part. Every place where the chemistry does not decide (ferrous iron, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, the residual phase) is a sentence in sheet 3 that says what you assumed and why it is reasonable for this ore; the calculation is graded on those sentences as much as on the numbers.
  2. One basis for the whole matrix. The XRF assays are oxides, the titration gives FeO, HSC Geo can give minerals on an element or an oxide basis: mixing bases inside one matrix is the classic error (the PC-lab's Exercise 3 exists to make you meet it before the assignment). Convert once, say which basis, and keep it.
  3. Rounds, not one big solve. Solve first the minerals that one element determines almost alone, subtract what they consume, pass the remainder on; finish with a residual phase or with closure. Negative abundances in the one-round solve are the signal that tells you where the rounds are needed.
  4. Sources, recorded. The composition and density of every mineral come from HSC Geo (or from a deposit-specific analysis if you have one) and sheet 3 says so, mineral by mineral. A stoichiometric end member is a reasonable start for magnetite or quartz, and a poor one for the silicates, which are solid solutions.
  5. Readable by a stranger. Seven sheets in the sheet's order, a chart with labelled axes, distinguishable minerals and depth running the sensible way, a rounds sheet from which a reader sees which assay solved which mineral. Individual work: your name, your reasoning.
  6. What it feeds. The same conversion returns in the GeoMet game and in the project, where a block model that carries only grades carries the wrong variable.

How to start, step by step

The assays and the minerals, and which element points at which mineral, read from stoichiometry. Solid lines: an element that one mineral of the list carries almost alone, and can open a round. Dashed: an element shared by several minerals, where a rule of yours is needed. Your own reading of HSC Geo's compositions replaces this sketch.

2

Organise MUJ1 into sheet 2, and decide the basis of every column

ClickOpen MUJ1.csv. Keep sample id, hole, depth from and to, and the assays you will use; mark each column as oxide (Al2O3, CaO, K2O, MgO, Na2O, SiO2, TiO2, Cr2O3) or element (Fe, P, Ba, S, Mn, V, Cu, Zn, ...); note that P_Calc_D and P_pct_BEST carry the same phosphorus and use one; note that FeO comes from titration and is ferrous iron in oxide form; treat LOI as a column to explain, not a mineral (negative values are gains on ignition). Fix units, missing values and totals; format the sheet.
ExpectA clean assay table with a basis written above every column and the depth pair per sample.
WhySheet 2 is graded on being organised and formatted; every later sheet reads from it.
Mixing oxide and element bases inside one matrix is the classic error; decide the basis here, once.
3

Take the ten minerals from HSC Geo and record composition, density and source in sheet 3

ClickIn HSC Geo's mineral database find magnetite, hematite, apatite, albite, biotite, actinolite, titanite, barite, quartz and diopside; copy each composition on the basis you chose in step 2 (convert oxide to element or the reverse, once) and the density; write the source next to each. Start the assumption list on the same sheet: which minerals are end members, which are solid solutions and what composition you took for them.
ExpectSheet 3 with ten rows of composition and density, the source stated, and the first assumptions written.
WhyThe whole calculation rests on this matrix; its source is a pass criterion.
The silicates (biotite, actinolite, diopside, albite) are solid solutions; an end-member composition biases every sample the same way, and that bias shows up later as a systematic residual.
4

Write the element-to-mineral map and plan the rounds in sheet 4

ClickFrom the sketch above and your sheet 3, write for each assay which minerals carry it. Elements carried by one mineral open the first rounds (phosphorus, barium, titanium, potassium, sodium); then the ferrous-iron decision (magnetite against hematite, with the silicates' share of FeO assumed and written); then the shared calcium, magnesium and aluminium with your rule; silicon closes with quartz by difference or with closure. Write the plan as a table: round, minerals solved, assays used, method (least squares or non-negative).
ExpectSheet 4 in draft: a reader can see which assay is meant to solve which mineral before any number exists.
Why"The rounds explicitly documented" is a pass criterion; planning them on paper is where the assumptions become visible.
An assumption made in the software and never written down is the failure mode the assignment is built to catch.
5

First pass: one round, all minerals, plain least squares, and read what comes back

ClickRun the conversion once with all ten minerals against all useful assays. Look at the abundances and the residuals per element.
ExpectSome negative abundances and some large residuals. That is information: the negatives sit where the chemistry does not determine the answer.
WhyThe first pass shows you where the rounds and the assumptions are needed; it is the starting point, not a result.
Do not fix a negative by deleting a mineral that microscopy or XRD says is there; fix it with a round or an assumption, and record which.
6

Build the rounds and turn one thing per pass

ClickSolve round by round as planned in step 4; subtract what each solved mineral consumes; pass the remainder on; use non-negative least squares where a plain fit goes negative; make the ferrous-iron decision explicit; leave quartz (or closure) for the end. After each change, re-read the residuals per element and per sample.
ExpectAbundances all non-negative, totals near 100 %, residuals of the size of the analytical uncertainty, and a log of what you changed between passes.
WhyThis is the loop of the assignment; the log becomes the text of sheets 4 and 7.
One change per pass, or you will not know which one fixed (or broke) the fit.
7

Check: residuals, totals, and the shape against depth

ClickBuild sheet 7: measured assay, back-calculated assay and the difference, for every element and every sample; add a line per element saying whether its residual is random or systematic, and why. Plot a first stacked bar of the minerals against depth and look for jumps between neighbouring samples that no geology explains.
ExpectA residual table with comments, and a depth profile that makes geological sense or a note on where it does not.
Why"Residuals reported and commented on" is a pass criterion; the depth profile is your own quickest sanity check.
A residual that is large on one element in every sample points at a composition; a residual large in one sample points at that sample's assays.
8

Results and the chart, sheets 5 and 6

ClickSheet 5: modal mineralogy per sample, with the depth pair. Sheet 6: a stacked bar chart of the ten minerals against depth: depth on the axis in a sensible direction (downhole), one distinguishable colour per mineral, axes labelled with units, a legend, a title with the hole name.
ExpectA chart a stranger can read without opening the other sheets.
WhyThe chart is graded on readability, not on beauty.
Samples of unequal length (SAMPFROM to SAMPTO differ) are honest as bars of unequal height, or as equal bars with the interval labelled; say which you did.
9

Write the assumptions where they will be read

ClickReturn to sheet 3 and make the assumption list complete: the ferrous-iron rule and its reason, the rule for each shared element, the compositions taken for the solid-solution silicates, what LOI and the trace columns were taken to mean, and for each assumption what measurement would remove it.
ExpectThe examined part of the assignment, in full sentences, next to the data it applies to.
WhyThe Canvas page: "assumptions written down where the chemistry does not determine the answer, with a justification. This is the part that is actually being examined."
"Assumed" without "because" does not meet the criterion.
10

Assemble, read as a stranger, submit

ClickSheet 1 with your name, the date and the assignment title; sheets in the sheet's order; a last read-through of each sheet against its pass criterion; one Excel file uploaded in Canvas by the due date. Keep the file: the same conversion returns in the game and in the project.
ExpectA workbook where a reader sees the data, the minerals, the rounds, the results, the chart and the residuals, in that order, and can follow every number.
WhyThe pass criteria are the marking scheme.

Deliverables checklist

Common mistakes

  • Treating the one-round solve as the result: the negatives and the residuals of the first pass are where the assignment actually starts.
  • Mixing oxide and element bases inside the matrix, or counting phosphorus twice because the file carries it in two columns.
  • Ignoring the FeO column, so that the magnetite/hematite split is decided by the software's default rather than by a stated assumption.
  • End-member compositions for the solid-solution silicates without saying so, and then a systematic residual nobody comments on.
  • Negative abundances left in the final round, or a mineral deleted from the list to make them disappear.
  • No residual table, or a residual table without a comment on where the fit is poor and why.
  • A chart with depth running upwards, unlabelled axes, or ten shades of one colour.
  • Assumptions made inside the software and never written in sheet 3.
