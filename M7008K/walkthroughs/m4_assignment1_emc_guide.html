What they ask

The sheet is MP_Assignment 1 - Element to Mineral conversion.pdf, a one-page document written by Mehdi Parian and dated 2023-09-11. The Canvas assignment page of module M4 repeats it and adds what a pass requires. It is an individual hand-in of one Excel file, due on Sunday 27 September at 23:59 as Canvas showed it on 9 September. The course has said that assignment dates may still be updated, so the date in Canvas is the one that counts. Element to mineral conversion, EMC for short, turns chemical assays into modal mineralogy. An assay says how much of each element or oxide a sample holds, while the modal mineralogy says how much of each mineral it holds, in per cent by mass. The task is described below point by point.

1. Purpose (section 1). The assignment trains you to calculate the modal mineralogy of a borehole and to show it against depth. It also asks for justified assumptions wherever the chemistry alone does not decide the mineralogy. The Canvas page puts it in one line, because it says that the assumptions "are the part that is actually being examined".

2. The data (section 3). The file MUJ1.csv holds 100 samples from borehole MUJ1, from 0.06 to 266.8 m. The oxides were analysed by XRF (X-ray fluorescence, which measures the elements and reports most of them as oxides), and FeO was measured by titration. The titration gives ferrous iron, which is iron in the Fe2+ form found in FeO, as opposed to ferric iron, the Fe3+ form found in Fe2O3. The file has 31 columns. They are the sample id, the hole id, the depth from and to, and then Fe, FeO, P twice (a calculated column and a "best" column), Al2O3, As, Ba, CaO, Cl, Co, Cr2O3, Cu, K2O, MgO, Mn, Na2O, Ni, Pb, SiO2, Sn, Sr, S, TiO2, V, Zn, Zr and LOI, all in per cent by mass. LOI, the loss on ignition, is the mass a sample loses when the laboratory heats it strongly, mostly as water and other volatiles. The expected minerals, in the sheet's order, are magnetite, hematite, apatite, albite, biotite, actinolite, titanite, barite, quartz and diopside.

3. What to do (Canvas page). First you organise and format the raw assays, which means their units, their missing values and their totals. Then you choose the minerals, take the chemical composition and the density of each one from the HSC Geo database, and record them. Next you set up the rounds, deciding which minerals are solved from which assays and in what order. Wherever an element is shared between minerals, or a mineral has a variable composition, you assume something, write it down and say why it is reasonable. The rounds solve the mass balance Ax=b, in which b holds the element grades of the sample, A holds the mineral compositions and x holds the mineral amounts. When a round has more assays than minerals, it is over-determined and you solve it by least squares, which picks the mineral amounts that make the sum of the squared differences between the measured and the calculated assays as small as possible. When it has fewer assays than minerals, it is under-determined and you add a constraint or an assumption, so the software never picks an answer silently. After that you check the calculation with residuals, element by element and sample by sample. A residual is the measured assay minus the assay calculated back from your minerals. Finally you show the modal mineralogy against depth as a stacked bar chart.

4. What to hand in (section 2): seven sheets, in this order. Sheet 1 holds the names, the date and the assignment title. Sheet 2 holds the chemical assays, organised and formatted. Sheet 3 holds the minerals selected from HSC Geo with their composition and density, together with the assumptions. Sheet 4 holds the rounds, with the minerals and the assays of each round. Sheet 5 holds the results, which is the modal mineralogy that the conversion gives. Sheet 6 is a graph sheet with the modal mineralogy against depth as a stacked bar chart. Sheet 7 holds the goodness of the calculation, including the residuals.

5. What a pass requires (Canvas page). All seven sheets are present, in order, and readable by someone who did not build the workbook. The compositions and densities are recorded with their source. The rounds are documented explicitly, so that a reader sees which assay solved which mineral. The assumptions are written down wherever the chemistry does not determine the answer, each with a justification. The residuals are reported and commented on. Finally, the stacked bar chart can be read, which means labelled axes, minerals that can be told apart and depth increasing in a sensible direction.

How the work flows

The sheet lists seven sheets, so it reads like a report to fill in. The work itself is a calculation loop inside a bookkeeping frame. Everything you record before the first solve stays fixed, the rounds are turned until the residuals are small and no amount is negative, and the seven sheets are the record of that loop. Five kinds of things play different roles in it.

  • Fixed for every run. The assays of MUJ1, as you organised them once, stay fixed. That includes which columns are oxides and which are elements, which two columns carry the same phosphorus and what LOI means. The ten expected minerals are fixed too, and so is the requirement that the minerals of a sample add up to about 100 %.
  • Chosen once, and recorded with their source. You choose the composition and density of each mineral, on one basis (element or oxide) for the whole balance. You also choose the assumptions where the chemistry does not decide. The first one is how the ferrous iron of the FeO column is shared between magnetite and the iron-bearing silicates, because that decides where the split between magnetite and hematite falls. The others are how calcium is shared between apatite, actinolite, diopside and titanite, how magnesium is shared between actinolite, diopside and biotite, and how aluminium is shared between albite and biotite.
  • Turned in the rounds. You turn the order of the rounds, the assays that solve each mineral, and the choice between plain and non-negative least squares. You change one thing per pass, and then you read the residuals again.
  • Read after every pass. After every pass you read the negative amounts, the residual of every element in every sample and the totals. You also ask whether a residual is random across the samples or systematic, because a systematic residual is the sign of a wrong composition.
  • Compared at the end. At the end you compare the measured assays with the assays calculated back from your minerals, and you look at the modal mineralogy against depth in the chart. A mineral that jumps between neighbouring samples for no geological reason is a sign of an unstable round.

In the sheet's own words, "justified assumptions" is the examined deliverable, "rounds" is the method and "goodness of calculations inc. residuals" is the proof that you checked. The method of the rounds is to solve first what one element determines almost alone, to subtract what those minerals use, and to pass the remainder on to the next round. A conversion without residuals is therefore not a good conversion, because nobody has checked it.

How to check the conversion

  • The residual is the check. For each sample you calculate the assays back from your minerals and subtract them from the measured ones, and sheet 7 holds that table. The M4 core concepts page writes the same balance as a=Cm, with C for the compositions and m for the mineral amounts. A residual within the analytical uncertainty is fine. A large residual with the same sign on one element says that a host mineral is missing or that a composition is wrong, and a residual that is systematic across many samples points at the composition of a mineral rather than at the fit. The Canvas page asks you to comment on the poor fits, and the folds under step 7 show how to read them.
  • Ferrous iron is the hinge. MUJ1 gives the total iron and, from the titration, the FeO. The iron of magnetite is two thirds ferric and one third ferrous, the iron of hematite is all ferric, and biotite, actinolite and diopside carry ferrous iron of their own. The FeO column is therefore the only information that splits magnetite from hematite. Because the silicates share that ferrous iron, what you assume about their iron decides the split between magnetite and hematite, so write that assumption first, in sheet 3, with its reason.
  • Shared elements need a stated rule. Phosphorus, barium and titanium each point at one mineral of the list (apatite, barite and titanite), so they open the rounds. Potassium and sodium point mostly at biotite and albite, but step 4 explains why in MUJ1 they are better fitted together with the other silicates than used alone. Calcium, magnesium, aluminium and silicon are shared by several minerals. An under-determined element cannot be solved by arithmetic, so you impose closure (the minerals add up to 100 %), assume a ratio between two minerals (approximate, local and said so), fit several minerals together, or leave one mineral as the residual phase. Any of these is acceptable when it is written down, and none of them is acceptable in silence.
  • Sanity checks that cost nothing. The totals should be near 100 % in every sample, and no amount should be negative in the final round. A mineral should only appear in samples where its diagnostic element is above the detection limit. LOI is treated as a loss or a gain on ignition and not as a mineral, and a negative LOI in the file is a gain, typical of ferrous iron taking up oxygen as the sample is heated. Finally, the two phosphorus columns are used once, not twice.

Key points

  1. The assumptions are the examined part. Every place where the chemistry does not decide (ferrous iron, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, potassium and the residual phase) becomes a sentence in sheet 3 that says what you assumed and why it is reasonable for this ore. The calculation is graded on those sentences as much as on the numbers.
  2. One basis for the whole balance. The XRF assays are mostly oxides, the titration gives FeO, and a mineral table can be written on an element basis or an oxide basis. Mixing the two bases inside one balance is the classic error, and Exercise 3 of the PC-lab exists to make you meet it before the assignment. Convert once, say which basis you used, and keep it.
  3. Rounds, not one big solve. Solve first the minerals that one element determines almost alone, subtract what they use, and pass the remainder on. Minerals that share elements are fitted together, and the last mineral closes the balance by difference. Negative amounts in a one-round solve are the signal that tells you where the rounds are needed.
  4. Sources, recorded. The composition and density of every mineral come from HSC Geo or from a deposit-specific analysis, and sheet 3 says which one, mineral by mineral. The course's Malmberget table is such an analysis, and two of its entries must be corrected before use, as the folds under step 3 explain. A stoichiometric end member, which is the ideal formula of a mineral, is a reasonable start for magnetite or quartz. It is a poor start for the silicates, because they are solid solutions whose composition varies from deposit to deposit.
  5. Readable by a stranger. The seven sheets come in the sheet's order. The chart has labelled axes, minerals that can be told apart and depth running the sensible way, and the rounds sheet lets a reader see which assay solved which mineral. It is individual work, so the workbook carries your name and your reasoning.
  6. What it feeds. The same conversion returns in the GeoMet game and in the project, where a block model that carries only grades carries the wrong variable.

Sheet 2, the organised assays: what each of the 31 columns is and what to do with it
  • Keep SAMPLEID, SAMPFROM and SAMPTO, and add the mid-depth. The depth pair is what the chart of sheet 6 plots against. HOLEID says MUJ1 in every row, so it can move to the title instead of repeating 100 times. The samples are between 0.25 and 3.2 m long, and there are seven gaps between consecutive samples, which matters when you draw the chart.
  • Oxides and elements, marked in the header. Al2O3, CaO, Cr2O3, K2O, MgO, Na2O, SiO2 and TiO2 are reported as oxides. Fe, P, As, Ba, Cl, Co, Cu, Mn, Ni, Pb, Sn, Sr, S, V, Zn and Zr are reported as elements. Every assay column except P_Calc_D ends in "_pct_BEST", so every value is a percentage by mass.
  • FeO is the titrated ferrous iron, and Fe is the total iron. The total iron already includes the ferrous iron, so the two columns do not describe two separate irons. The Fe column carries all the iron of the sample, and the FeO column tells how much of it is ferrous.
  • One phosphorus column. P_Calc_D and P_pct_BEST are identical in all 100 samples, so you use one of them and say so. Using both would count apatite twice.
  • 0.0005 means below the detection limit. The laboratory writes 0.0005 where an element is too low to measure, and in MUJ1 this only happens in trace columns (As, Ba, Cr2O3, Cu, Pb, S, Sn and Zn). Keep the value as reported but mark it, for example in grey, so that a reader sees a detection flag rather than a measurement. It is far too small to move any mineral.
  • A negative LOI is a gain, not an error. Heating turns FeO into Fe2O3, and the oxygen that the iron takes up adds mass. In a sample rich in magnetite that gain is larger than the water lost, so the LOI comes out negative, which happens in 27 of the 100 samples (down to -1.34 %). LOI is not a mineral and does not enter the balance, but it becomes an independent check in step 7.
  • No empty cells. Every sample has a value in every column, so the missing values of the Canvas page come down to the detection flags above.
  • The element columns of the balance go next to the assays. The next fold converts the oxides, and those element values are what sheets 4 and 7 read.
From oxides to elements: one basis for the whole balance, and the factors for MUJ1
  • Basis = element weight per cent. The course's mineral table gives its compositions as element percentages, so converting the eight oxide assays once is simpler and safer than converting every composition to oxides. The choice itself is free, but it must be the same for every row and every column of the balance.
  • The factor is the element's share of the oxide's molar mass. For Al2O3 it is 2×26.982×26.98+3×16.00=0.529, which means that 1 % Al2O3 holds 0.529 % Al. For FeO it is 55.8555.85+16.00=0.777, so the FeO column becomes ferrous iron when it is multiplied by 0.777.
  • A worked example with the first sample. MUJ1-001 has 7.55 % Al2O3, which is 7.55×0.529=3.99 % Al, and 10.45 % FeO, which is 10.45×0.777=8.12 % ferrous iron.
  • Total iron and ferrous iron are two separate lines of the balance. The total iron must be carried by every iron-bearing mineral, while the ferrous iron can only be carried by magnetite and the silicates. Keeping the two lines apart is what lets the balance split magnetite from hematite.
  • P, Ba and S are already elements, so they enter the balance as they are.
  • Convert back when you report residuals. Dividing an element residual by the same factor gives it in the units the laboratory reported, so that a reader of sheet 7 compares like with like.

Sheet 3, the minerals: where each composition comes from, and the two traps in the course table
  • Source = the course's Malmberget table wherever it has the mineral. The file Ex 2.3 Malmberget Minerals.xlsx, in the folder of PC-lesson 1, holds microprobe analyses by Lund (2009) from the LTU database of HSC. It covers albite, apatite, biotite, magnetite, orthoclase, quartz, actinolite, tremolite and diopside, each with its density. Malmberget is an apatite iron ore with the same mineral set as MUJ1, so its measured silicates are a much closer start than ideal formulas.
  • Hematite, titanite and barite = ideal formulas. The table has no analysis of them, so their compositions are computed from Fe2O3, CaTiSiO5 and BaSO4 with the atomic weights. Their densities come from HSC Geo or from a mineral handbook, and sheet 3 says which.
  • Trap 1: the quartz row reads 100.39 under Si %. That number is the SiO2 total of the analysis, not silicon. Pure SiO2 holds 46.74 % Si, so quartz enters the balance with 46.74. Reading 100.39 as silicon would give every gram of quartz more than twice its real silicon, and it would underestimate quartz by a factor of about 2.1.
  • Trap 2: the magnetite oxygen of 20.99 % belongs to FeO. The row keeps 72.72 % Fe, but 20.99 % oxygen is what that iron would need if it were all FeO. In Fe3O4 one third of the iron is ferrous and two thirds are ferric, so the oxygen is 27.78 % and the ferrous iron is one third of 72.72, which is 24.24 %. Oxygen is not assayed, so the correction changes the composition total rather than the balance, but the ferrous iron line of magnetite has to be 24.24.
  • Ferrous iron in the silicates = all of their iron. A microprobe reports iron without its charge. Biotite, actinolite and diopside usually carry their iron as ferrous iron, so their ferrous iron line equals their total iron line (3.02, 8.01 and 7.47 % in the Malmberget table). The split between magnetite and hematite rests on this assumption, so it is written in sheet 3 with its reason.
  • Keep the rest of the database in view. The table holds minerals that are not on the expected list, and some of them were found by XRD in the ores of PC-lab 1. They become useful in step 7, as tests of where an element that the expected minerals cannot place might sit.
  • Record the source mineral by mineral. A column with the formula, one with the density and one with the source (Malmberget microprobe, or ideal formula) let the reader check every row. A total column also shows why some silicates add up to less than 100 %, because the microprobe does not measure their OH and F.

Sheet 4, the rounds: which assays open, which minerals are fitted together, and what comes last
  • Round 1 = the assays with a single carrier, which are Ba, P and Ti. Among the ten minerals, barium sits only in barite and phosphorus only in apatite, while titanium sits almost only in titanite, because the other minerals hold at most 0.02 % Ti. Each of these minerals is then the assay divided by its content of that element, for example apatite=P/0.160 with the 16.04 % P of the Malmberget apatite. The three minerals are fixed exactly, so their calcium and silicon are known before the silicates start.
  • Subtract what round 1 used. Apatite holds 40.36 % Ca, and titanite holds about 20 % Ca and 14 % Si, so that calcium and silicon are removed from the sample before the next round sees it.
  • Round 2 = the silicates that share elements, fitted together. Biotite, albite, actinolite and diopside share K, Na, Mg, Ca and Al. Potassium sits mostly in biotite and sodium mostly in albite, but magnesium sits in biotite, actinolite and diopside, calcium in actinolite and diopside, and aluminium in albite and biotite. Five assays against four minerals give one more equation than there are unknowns, so least squares fits all four minerals at once, and the misfit shows how well the compositions agree with the sample. The fold under step 6 explains the weights and why no amount may go negative.
  • Why not biotite from potassium alone. Taking biotite straight from K only works when the sample has at least as much MgO per unit of K2O as the biotite composition. Where the sample has less, biotite taken from K brings more magnesium than the sample holds, so actinolite and diopside are pushed to zero and a large negative MgO residual is left behind. Compare the MgO/K2O ratio of every sample with that of your biotite before you choose, because in many MUJ1 samples it is lower.
  • Round 3 = iron, after the silicates. The silicates carry ferrous iron, so their share is paid first from the titrated ferrous iron. What is left belongs to magnetite, whose iron is one third ferrous, so magnetite=Fe2+ left/0.242. Hematite then takes the total iron that is still unplaced, which gives hematite=Fe left/0.699.
  • Round 4 = quartz, last. Every silicate and titanite carry silicon, so quartz can only be the silicon left at the end, quartz=Si left/0.467.
  • Write it as a table with a worked sample. One row per round with the minerals, the assays and the method, followed by one sample worked through all four rounds, lets a reader follow the arithmetic without opening your formulas.

6

Build the rounds and change one thing per pass

ClickSolve round by round as planned in step 4, subtract what each solved mineral uses, and pass the remainder on. Fit the shared silicates together with weighted non-negative least squares, make the ferrous iron decision explicit, and leave quartz for the end. After each change, read the residuals again by element and by sample, and write one line in a log.
ExpectNo negative amounts, totals near 100 %, residuals that are about the size of the analytical uncertainty or explained, and a log of what changed between passes.
WhyThis is the loop of the assignment, and the log becomes the text of sheets 4 and 7.
Make one change per pass, or you will not know which change fixed the fit or which one broke it.
Solving a round with more assays than minerals: weighted, non-negative least squares
  • Least squares = the amounts that leave the smallest squared misfit. For one sample, the fit chooses the mineral amounts xj that make ∑iwi2(bi−∑jaijxj)2 as small as possible. Here bi is the amount of element i left in the sample, aij is the content of element i in mineral j, and wi is the weight of that element. Squaring makes a misfit count whatever its sign, and it makes large misfits count more than small ones.
  • Non-negative = no mineral below zero. A plain fit may return, for example, -2 % diopside because that improves the numbers, but a sample cannot hold a negative amount of a mineral. Non-negative least squares (NNLS for short) sets such a mineral to zero and refits the others, and the notes of the sample then say that the round found none of it.
  • Weights = one over the borehole mean of each element. Without weights the fit follows the large assays and ignores the small ones, because Al2O3 averages 7.8 % over the borehole while K2O averages only 1.7 %. Dividing each equation by the mean of its element makes a misfit of, say, a tenth of the usual grade count the same for every element. The weights therefore come from the data of this borehole rather than from a default of one.
  • Zero, not negative, for the minerals solved by difference. Magnetite, hematite and quartz come out of rounds 3 and 4 as a remainder divided by a content. When the minerals already placed hold more of the element than the sample does, that remainder is negative, so the mineral is set to zero and the excess stays in sheet 7 as a negative residual.
  • Tools. In HSC Geo each round fits its minerals to its assays, as in PC-lab 1. In Excel, Solver can minimise the weighted sum of squares with its option that keeps the variables non-negative.
  • One change per pass, written in the log. A log line such as "round 2 now weighted" or "diopside moved into round 2" is what turns sheet 4 from a table into an argument.
7

Check the calculation in sheet 7: residuals, totals and the shape against depth

ClickBuild sheet 7 with the measured assay, the calculated assay and their difference for every element and every sample, plus the total of each sample. Add a line per element saying whether its residual is random or systematic, and why. Compare the rounds with a global least-squares fit, use LOI as an independent check, and draw a first stacked bar against depth to look for jumps that no geology explains.
ExpectA residual table with comments, a comparison with a second solution, and a depth profile that makes geological sense or a note on where it does not.
WhyResiduals that are reported and commented on are a pass criterion, and the depth profile is your own quickest sanity check.
A residual that is large on one element in every sample points at a composition, while a residual that is large in one sample points at the assays of that sample.
Sheet 7, the goodness of the calculation: how to read a residual, a total, a second solution and LOI
  • Residual = measured minus calculated, in the units of the assay. A positive residual means that the sample holds some of that element which no mineral has taken, so it has not been placed. A negative residual means that the minerals hold more of it than the sample does, in other words part of it has been placed twice. For example, if a sample has 2.00 % K2O and your minerals carry 1.40 %, the residual is +0.60 %, which means that 0.60 % K2O is still looking for a host.
  • Some residuals are zero by construction. P, Ba and TiO2 close exactly because round 1 is solved from them, and FeO closes wherever magnetite is present because magnetite is solved from it. A zero there proves nothing, so judge the conversion on the assays that were fitted (K2O, Na2O, MgO, CaO and Al2O3), and on Fe and SiO2 wherever a mineral solved by difference went to zero.
  • Random or systematic. Report, for each element, the mean residual with its sign and the mean absolute residual, which is the average size of the residual ignoring its sign. When the mean residual is almost as large as the mean absolute residual, the residual has the same sign in most samples, so it is systematic and points at a composition or at a missing mineral. When the mean residual is near zero but the mean absolute residual is large, the residual scatters, which points at the assays or at a round that is unstable from sample to sample.
  • Total = the sum of the mineral amounts of a sample. A total below 100 % is the part that the balance does not see, which is the OH, F and water of the minerals, trace minerals and analytical error, and it is reported as "others". A total above 100 % means that more mass has been placed than the sample has, usually because one element has been placed twice.
  • A second solution = a global least-squares fit. Solve all ten minerals against all the balance lines at once, weighted and non-negative, and compare the result with the rounds mineral by mineral. If the two agree closely, the order of the rounds does not drive the result. Where they disagree, the result rests on an assumption, and sheet 7 names it.
  • A missing mineral is tested as a check, not added to the result. When one residual stays one-sided in most samples, for example one assay positive in most samples while another one stays negative, rerun the rounds with a candidate mineral that the database holds, and see whether the residuals collapse. The main result keeps the expected list of the sheet, and the check is reported next to it, together with what it costs on the other assays.
  • LOI = an independent check. LOI is not in the balance, so it can test the result from outside. The minerals predict an LOI equal to the OH and F of biotite and actinolite (what their analyses lack to reach 100 %) minus the oxygen that FeO takes up on ignition. That oxygen is 0.111×FeO, because turning FeO into Fe2O3 adds half an oxygen atom (8.0 g) to every 71.8 g of FeO. Compare the predicted and the measured LOI over all the samples, for example with the correlation coefficient r, which is 1 when two series rise and fall together perfectly and 0 when they are unrelated.

Results and the chart, sheets 5 and 6

ClickSheet 5 gets the modal mineralogy of every sample with its depth pair, its total and a note on what each round left out. Sheet 6 gets a stacked bar chart of the ten minerals against depth, with depth running downhole, one distinguishable colour per mineral, axes labelled with units, a legend and a title with the hole name.
ExpectA results table and a chart that a stranger can read without opening the other sheets.
WhyThe chart is graded on readability, not on beauty.
The samples have unequal lengths, and there are gaps between some of them. Bars of unequal height on a true depth scale are honest, and so are equal bars with the interval written on them, as long as you say which of the two you drew.
Sheet 5, the results: one row per sample, with its total and its notes
  • Columns = sample, from, to, the depth interval as text, the ten minerals in wt %, the total, "others" and the notes. The interval written as text, such as "0.1-3.0", becomes the label of the bar in sheet 6.
  • Minerals in the sheet's order. Keeping the order of the assignment sheet (magnetite, hematite, apatite, albite, biotite, actinolite, titanite, barite, quartz and diopside) lets a reader match sheet 5 with the assignment without searching.
  • Others = 100 minus the total. It makes visible the part of the sample that the balance does not see, and it turns negative wherever the total runs above 100 %.
  • Notes = what a round had to leave out. A short note such as "round 2 finds no actinolite" or "no quartz, the silicates already hold all the silicon" tells the reader that a zero is the chemistry speaking and not a typing error.
  • Average, minimum and maximum rows under the table. They give the borehole summary that sheet 7 and the discussion refer to.
  • Round the display, not the values. Show two decimals, but keep the full precision in the cells, so that sheet 7 calculates the assays back exactly.
Sheet 6, the stacked bar chart: depth down the page, one colour per mineral
  • Chart type = stacked horizontal bars, one bar per sample. Horizontal bars let depth run down the page, the way it runs down the borehole. In Excel this is the Stacked Bar chart with the category axis set to show the categories in reverse order, so that the shallowest sample sits at the top.
  • Axes = depth in metres and mineral content in wt %. Label both axes with their units, and fix the content axis a little above 100 %, so that a total above 100 shows instead of being cut off.
  • One colour per mineral, chosen so they can be told apart. Colours that echo the mineral help the reader, for example a near-black for magnetite and a red for hematite, while ten shades of one colour do not.
  • Unequal intervals and gaps, said out loud. Excel's category axis draws one equal bar per sample in sample order, so the seven gaps between samples and the lengths from 0.25 to 3.2 m do not show. Either say so in a note on the sheet, or add a second figure on a true depth scale, where each bar is as tall as its interval.
  • A legend and a title with the hole name, such as "MUJ1: modal mineralogy against depth", make the chart readable on its own.
  • Look at the chart before you trust the numbers. A mineral that jumps between neighbouring samples for no geological reason usually points at an unstable round, so the chart is also a check.

Write the assumptions where they will be read

ClickReturn to sheet 3 and complete the assumption list. Cover the compositions and their source, the corrections to the database, the ferrous iron rule, the order of the rounds and the weights, what happens to a negative amount, the potassium that biotite cannot carry, the detection limit, the duplicate phosphorus, LOI and the components that are not balanced. For each assumption, write what measurement would remove it.
ExpectThe examined part of the assignment, in full sentences, next to the data it applies to.
WhyThe Canvas page says that the assumptions, each with its justification, are the part being examined.
An assumption without a "because" does not meet the criterion.
Writing an assumption so that it can be examined
  • Each assumption = what you assumed, why, and what would remove it. For example, "the iron of biotite, actinolite and diopside is taken as ferrous, because these minerals usually carry their iron as ferrous iron, and a measurement of the iron charge in these minerals would remove the assumption".
  • Number the assumptions and refer to them. Sheet 7 can then say "see assumption 8" next to a residual, which ties the check to its reason.
  • Say where the chemistry does not decide, and what you did about it. Those places are the compositions of the solid-solution silicates, the ferrous iron of the silicates, the minerals that share K, Na, Mg, Ca and Al, the minerals solved by difference, and any element that no expected mineral can place.
  • Say what the data cannot tell. Oxygen, water, F and Cl are not assayed, so they are not balanced, and a total below 100 % is partly made of them. Saying it once saves a question for every sample.
  • Keep the result and the check apart. An assumption such as "the expected list is kept, and a candidate mineral is only tested as a check" tells the reader which numbers are the answer and which ones are evidence.

Deliverables checklist

Common mistakes

  • Treating the one-round solve as the result. The negative amounts and the residuals of the first pass are where the assignment actually starts.
  • Mixing oxide and element bases inside the balance, or counting phosphorus twice because the file carries it in two identical columns.
  • Reading the quartz row of the Malmberget table as 100.39 % Si. That number is SiO2, and quartz holds 46.74 % Si, so the slip underestimates quartz by a factor of about 2.1.
  • Using the magnetite oxygen of the table as it stands, or treating all the iron of magnetite as ferrous. In Fe3O4 only one third of the iron is ferrous.
  • Ignoring the FeO column, so that the split between magnetite and hematite is decided by the software's default rather than by a stated assumption.
  • Solving magnetite from the ferrous iron before the silicates have taken their share, which places the same ferrous iron twice.
  • Taking biotite from potassium alone without comparing the MgO/K2O ratio of the samples with that of the biotite, and then reading the large negative MgO residual as bad luck.
  • Fitting the shared elements without weights, so that the large assays such as Al2O3 and CaO decide the fit and the small ones such as K2O are ignored.
  • End-member compositions for the solid-solution silicates without saying so, followed by a systematic residual that nobody comments on.
  • Negative amounts left in the final round, or a mineral deleted from the list to make them disappear.
  • Treating 0.0005 as a measured value, or a negative LOI as an error or as a mineral.
  • Adding a mineral that is not on the expected list to the result, instead of reporting it as a check next to the result.
  • No residual table, or a residual table without a comment on where the fit is poor and why.
  • Reading the zero residuals of P, Ba, TiO2 and FeO as proof of a good fit, when they are zero by construction.
  • A chart with depth running upwards, unlabelled axes, or ten shades of one colour.
  • Assumptions made inside the software and never written in sheet 3.
