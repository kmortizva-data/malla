PC-lab, step by step: measuring a liberation distribution by hand

What they ask

The instruction is the Canvas page "PC-lab — mineral liberation (instruction)" of module M5 (the exercise instruction PDF with the particle images and the calculation template XLSX are handed out for the session). No hand-in. In the page's words, the session exists so that you have measured a liberation distribution with your own eyes at least once before you are asked to interpret one that a machine produced; keep the spreadsheet, it is the reference you come back to in M6 and M7. Purpose: to measure the liberation of a mineral by point counting on images of particles, to calculate the liberation distribution and the degree of liberation from the counts, to plot the liberation curve, and to see how the answer changes with particle size and with the texture of the ore.

Before the session: the core concepts page, especially the worked table and the paragraph on reading a liberation curve; the exercise instruction, reproducing its worked 1000 µm column yourself (amount of mineral, yield, cumulative yield, sample grade, degree of liberation); a spreadsheet you are fluent in; the calculation template, copied once per data set.

The grade classes. Every particle goes into one of nine classes by its content of the mineral of interest: 0, 0–5, 5–20, 20–35, 35–50, 50–65, 65–80, 80–95 and 95–100 %, represented by their averages 0, 2.5, 12.5, 27.5, 42.5, 57.5, 72.5, 87.5 and 97.5 %. The same classes throughout, so the data sets compare.

The procedure. Two ore textures (a breccia and an evenly grained ore) at three particle sizes each: 500, 300 and 50 µm; start with the 500 µm images of the breccia. Count: lay the grid over the image and, at every grid position, judge the mineral content of the particle underneath and assign it to a class; record the number of grids per class (20 grids for the 500 µm images, 54 for the 300 µm ones; the number for the 50 µm images is not stated, so ask at the start and write it down, because the sample grade depends on it). Calculate, class by class: amount of mineral = class average × number of grids; yield of a class = amount in the class ÷ total amount × 100; cumulative yield = the sum of the yields of that class and every richer class, from 100 % at the bottom of the composition axis to the top-class yield at 95–100 %. Extract the two summary numbers: mineral grade of the sample (vol. %) = total amount ÷ number of grids; degree of liberation (%) = amount in the 95–100 class ÷ total amount × 100. Plot the liberation curve: cumulative yield against the mineral content of the particle, 0 to 100 %. Repeat for the other two sizes of the texture, then for the second texture; three curves of one texture on one chart.

The five reading questions, one line each: how the degree of liberation changes as the particle size falls within one texture; whether the sample grade stays the same across the three sizes, whether it should, and if not whether that is the ore or your counting; which texture liberates earlier at the same size and what in the image explains it; from your curve, the highest recovery a perfect separator taking only particles above 80 % could reach, and the concentrate grade that implies; whether the breakage looks random or preferential, and the evidence in the images.

What must exist by the end: six completed count tables (two textures × three sizes) with amount, yield and cumulative yield per class; a sample grade and a degree of liberation for each; two charts, one per texture, with three curves each; your written answers to the five questions.

How the work flows

The session is one count repeated six times, one calculation repeated six times, and two charts read with five questions. Nothing is designed and nothing is tuned; what varies is the image (texture and size), and what you read is a distribution and two numbers from it. Four things have different roles:

  • Fixed for every run: the nine grade classes and their averages, the counting grid of each image size (20, 54, and the number you are told for 50 µm), the calculation itself.
  • Chosen once: the template, copied per data set; the mineral of interest; the rule you apply to a particle near a class boundary, written down so that all six counts use it.
  • Turned: only the image: breccia at 500, 300 and 50 µm, then the evenly grained ore at the same three sizes.
  • Read after every count: the amount per class, the yields, the cumulative yields, the sample grade and the degree of liberation; and, once three sizes are done, the three curves of one texture on one chart.
  • Compared at the end: the three sizes within a texture, the two textures at the same size, and the two summary numbers across all six.

In the page's words, "measure by point counting" is the count, "calculate the liberation distribution" is the table, "read what you have produced" is the five questions, and "a stereological measurement by hand" is what you will have done: a two-dimensional section of three-dimensional particles, judged at class boundaries, on a small number of particles. An automated instrument does the same thing faster and inherits the same bias; that is the reason for the session.

How to check a count

  • The grids add up. The number of grids over all classes equals the image's grid count (20, 54, or the number you were told); a missing grid changes the grade.
  • The yields sum to 100 and the cumulative yield starts at 100 % in the poorest class you counted and ends at the top-class yield at 95–100 %.
  • The grade should not depend on size. The mineral grade of the sample is a property of the ore; if it drifts across the three sizes of one texture, that is your counting (a boundary judged differently, a small number of particles), and the page asks you to say so.
  • The curve is a ceiling. The point at 95 % is the degree of liberation; the point at any other composition is the best recovery a perfect separator taking everything above that composition could reach. Read the 80 % question off it and compute the concentrate grade from the amounts in the classes above 80 %.

Step by step in the session

1

Copy the template once per data set, and write the boundary rule

ClickSix copies of the calculation template, named by texture and size. Agree in the group how a particle sitting near a class boundary is judged, and write the rule on the sheet.
ExpectSix identical empty tables and one rule that all six counts will follow.
WhyA boundary judged differently between counts shows up later as a grade that drifts with size.
2

Count the breccia at 500 µm

ClickGrid over the image; at each of the 20 positions judge the particle's content of the mineral and tick a class; record the grids per class.
ExpectTwenty ticks over the nine classes.
WhyThis is the measurement; everything else is arithmetic.
Count grid positions, not particles: a large particle under two positions is two ticks.
3

Calculate the table: amount, yield, cumulative yield

ClickAmount per class = average × grids; total; yield per class = amount ÷ total × 100; cumulative yield summed from the richest class down.
ExpectYields summing to 100 and a cumulative column running from 100 % to the top-class yield.
WhyThe table is the liberation distribution.
4

The two summary numbers

ClickSample grade = total amount ÷ grids; degree of liberation = amount in the 95–100 class ÷ total × 100.
ExpectA grade in vol. % and a liberation in %, written on the sheet.
WhyThe single number everybody quotes is the degree of liberation; the grade is your consistency check across sizes.
5

The other two sizes of the breccia, then the chart

ClickRepeat steps 2 to 4 for 300 µm (54 grids) and 50 µm (the number you were told). Plot the three curves, cumulative yield against particle composition, on one chart.
ExpectThree curves that rise towards the top class as the size falls.
WhyThe comparison within a texture is the first reading question.
If the grade changes across the sizes, note it now; the second question asks about it.
6

The evenly grained ore, three sizes, second chart

ClickSame procedure, same classes, same rule; the second chart.
ExpectThree more curves, probably liberating at a different size than the breccia.
WhyThe comparison between textures at the same size is the third question.
7

Answer the five questions from the charts

ClickOne line each: liberation against size within a texture; whether the grade stayed constant and why; which texture liberates earlier and what in the image shows it; the highest recovery a perfect separator taking only particles above 80 % reaches, and the concentrate grade that implies; random or preferential breakage, with the evidence.
ExpectFive lines that each point at a chart or an image.
WhyThe page asks for them by name; they are the reading the session exists for.
8

Keep the spreadsheet

ClickSave the six tables and the two charts where you will find them.
ExpectThe reference the page says you will come back to in M6 and M7.

Common mistakes

  • Reading one count as the result: the session is the comparison across sizes and textures.
  • Different boundary judgements between counts, which show up as a grade that drifts with size.
  • Grids that do not add up to the image's count, or yields that do not sum to 100.
  • The cumulative yield summed the wrong way (it starts at 100 % in the poorest class).
  • The 80 % question answered with the degree of liberation instead of the curve's value at 80 %.
  • The 50 µm grid count not asked for at the start, so the grade of those counts cannot be computed.
