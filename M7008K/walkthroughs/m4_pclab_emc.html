What they ask

The instruction is PC1 Element to mineral conversion.pdf (M7008K, three pages) with the Canvas page "PC-lab — element-to-mineral conversion (instruction)" of module M4 around it. The exercises are done together in the session, at the pace of the group, in HSC Chemistry 10 (the Geo module) on the PC-lab machines; there is no hand-in, and the session is the rehearsal for Assignment 1. The method is a mass balance, Ax=b: A the mass fractions of the elements in the minerals (known, from microprobe analyses or a database), x the mass fractions of the minerals in the sample (unknown), b the mass fractions of the elements in the sample (known, the assays). Three exercises, each the shape of one thing that goes wrong in the assignment:

Stoichiometric compositions for galena and chalcopyrite; for sphalerite and pyrite the deposit-specific compositions of the HSC database for Pyhäsalmi. What must exist by the end (Canvas page): a working calculation file per exercise with the mineral list, the compositions and the round structure visible; modal compositions for all samples; the iron distribution (Exercise 1), the residual and the silver distribution (Exercise 2), the two maximum iron recoveries and the theoretical concentrate's P2O5 grade (Exercise 3); and a few written lines listing every assumption you had to make and, for each, what you would measure to remove it. That note is the seed of the assumptions sheet of Assignment 1.

How the work flows

The three exercises are one method applied three times with the difficulty turned up: a system that a single round solves, a system that needs rounds and a choice of solver, and a system whose assays are oxides and whose minerals are solid solutions, ending in a process question. Nothing is designed; what you turn is how the calculation is set up, and what you read is whether the answer can be trusted. Four things have different roles:

  • Fixed for every run: the assays of each exercise (the tables above and the xls files), the phases that microscopy and XRD identified, and the report each exercise asks for.
  • Chosen once per exercise: the mineral list and the composition of every mineral (stoichiometric where the instruction says so, deposit-specific from the HSC database or the microprobe files where it says that), and the basis of the matrix (element or oxide, never both).
  • Turned: the round structure (which assays solve which minerals, in what order), and the solver (least squares against non-negative least squares). One change at a time.
  • Read after every solve: negative abundances, the residual per element, the totals, and the element distributions the exercise asks for (iron, silver, and in Exercise 3 the iron in magnetite against the iron in the silicates).
  • Kept at the end: the three files, and the note of assumptions with the measurement that would remove each one.

In the instruction's words, "calculate the modal composition" is the solve, "distribution of iron" and "distribution of silver" are read from the mineral compositions rather than from any assay (iron is never assayed in Exercise 1; silver sits in two hosts in Exercise 2), and "maximum iron recovery" and "P2O5 of a theoretical concentrate" are the first time in the course that a modal composition becomes a product specification.

How to check a conversion

  • Totals and signs. The minerals of a sample should add to about 100 %; anything negative is a signal that a round or a constraint is missing, not a number to report.
  • The residual. Back-calculate the assays from the fitted minerals and subtract the measured ones, element by element. Small and random is fine; large on one element points at a missing host or a wrong composition; systematic across the three streams of Exercise 2 or the six samples of Exercise 3 points at a composition.
  • The distributions. Iron in Exercise 1 is the sum over minerals of abundance times the iron content of each; silver in Exercise 2 is the same sum over galena and freibergite, and depends on the compositions you chose. Say so.
  • The basis. In Exercise 3 the assays are oxides and Lund's compositions are elements: convert one side, once, before the matrix is built. The conversion factors are the molar-mass ratios (P2O5 to P is 0.4364; the reverse 2.291).

Before the session: numbers by hand

Exercise 1, sample 1, with end-member compositions. Take galena 86.6 % Pb and 13.4 % S, chalcopyrite 34.6 % Cu, 30.5 % Fe and 34.9 % S, pyrite 46.6 % Fe and 53.4 % S, and, only to see the shape of the calculation, a sphalerite with 56.5 % Zn, 10.0 % Fe and 33.5 % S (the Pyhäsalmi composition in the HSC database replaces it in the session). Lead sits in galena alone, so galena is 1.8/0.866=2.08 %; copper gives chalcopyrite 0.37/0.346=1.07 %; zinc gives sphalerite 33.4/0.565=59.1 %. Sulphur is then shared: galena takes 2.08×0.134=0.28, chalcopyrite 0.37, sphalerite 19.8; the remaining 27.3−0.28−0.37−19.8=6.85 % of sulphur goes to pyrite, 6.85/0.534=12.8 %. The four sulphides make 75.1 % of the sample; the other 24.9 % is a gangue the assays never saw. Iron, never assayed, comes out of the compositions: 5.9 % in sphalerite, 0.3 % in chalcopyrite, 6.0 % in pyrite, 12.2 % in all, so about half of it in sphalerite and half in pyrite. That is the sequential answer; the least-squares answer will differ a little because it spreads the inconsistencies over all four minerals instead of pushing them into pyrite, and the difference is the residual.

Exercise 3, the two process numbers, as formulas. With magnetite at 72.72 % Fe (Lund) and a modal composition m, the iron carried by magnetite is 0.7272⋅mmgt and the maximum iron recovery of a feed is that share over the total iron assay; whatever iron the silicates carry (actinolite 8.0 %, diopside 7.5 %, biotite 3.0 % Fe in Lund's table) can never report to a magnetite concentrate. If magnetite and apatite are not separated, the theoretical concentrate is the two together, and its P2O5 grade is the phosphorus of apatite (16.04 % P, that is 16.04×2.291=36.7 % P2O5) times the apatite abundance, divided by the summed abundance of magnetite and apatite. Set the two formulas up in the spreadsheet before the session; the modal composition fills them in.

Step by step in the session

The menu paths of HSC Geo (the mineral database, the calculation module) are shown at the start of the session; write them into your log as you go. The steps below are the decisions and the checks.

1

Exercise 1: build the mineral list with the compositions the instruction names

ClickGalena and chalcopyrite stoichiometric; sphalerite and pyrite from the HSC database's Pyhäsalmi entries. Write the four compositions into your file next to the assays of the six samples.
ExpectA 4-by-4 matrix (Cu, Zn, Pb, S against the four minerals) whose entries you can read as "per cent of this element in that mineral".
WhyThe first thing that can go wrong in the assignment is the matrix; here it is small enough to see.
Note the difference between the end-member sphalerite and the Pyhäsalmi one: the iron it carries is the whole point of the iron distribution.
2

Exercise 1: solve in one round, then read the iron

ClickAll four minerals, the four assays as total assays (every mineral decomposes completely), one round, least squares. Read the modal composition of the six samples and compute the iron distribution from the compositions.
ExpectA modal composition close to the hand numbers for sample 1, a remainder that is gangue, and an iron split between sphalerite, chalcopyrite and pyrite.
WhyIron was never assayed: it is inferred from the mineral chemistry, and the exercise asks what that means for how much you trust it.
The remainder is not a mineral you can name from these four assays; say so rather than inventing one.
3

Exercise 2: try the obvious thing, all minerals in one round

ClickTwelve minerals from the microprobe file against the twelve assays, one round, least squares, for feed, concentrate and tail.
ExpectNegative abundances, and minerals whose values swing wildly between the three streams.
WhyThe negatives are the signal the exercise is built to show; they mark where the assays do not determine the answer.
Do not delete a phase that microscopy and XRD identified to make a negative disappear.
4

Exercise 2: rebuild in rounds

ClickRound 1 the minerals one element determines almost alone (lead to galena, zinc to sphalerite); round 2 the trace carriers (antimony, silver, copper: bournonite, freibergite, chalcopyrite; arsenic to arsenopyrite); round 3 the bulk phases (pyrite from the remaining sulphur, quartz, talc, garnet, fluorite, magnetite from silica, magnesium, calcium, fluorine and iron); finish with a residual phase calculated as 100 minus the others.
ExpectNon-negative abundances that behave between feed, concentrate and tail, and a residual per element you can read.
WhyStaged calculation is the practical tool of the method: solve, subtract what is consumed, pass the remainder on.
Antimony sits in bournonite and freibergite both, and silver in freibergite with a trace in galena: two hosts per element means an assumption, and the exercise asks you to state it.
5

Exercise 2: least squares against non-negative least squares, and the silver

ClickSwitch the solver on the same rounds and watch what changes; then report the modal composition, the calculation residual and the silver distribution for the three streams.
ExpectNon-negative least squares removes the negatives by construction; the silver distribution depends on the freibergite composition (29.28 % Ag) and on the galena trace (0.07 %), so it is an assumption-driven number.
Why"The silver distribution is the point of the exercise": it is in more than one phase, and the answer depends on assumptions you made rather than on the assays.
A solver that hides a negative has not removed the reason for it; the residual tells you whether the mineral list is still wrong.
6

Exercise 3: put the assays and Lund's compositions on one basis

ClickThe assays are oxides (except iron); Lund's compositions are elements. Convert the assays to elements (or the compositions to oxides), once, with the molar-mass factors, and build the matrix for magnetite, apatite, albite, orthoclase, quartz, actinolite and biotite.
ExpectA matrix with one basis, and a note in your file saying which basis and which factors.
WhyMixing oxide and element bases inside one matrix is the classic error of this exercise, and of the assignment.
Lund's silicates are averages of solid solutions; that is why they are given, and why end members would be wrong here.
7

Exercise 3: solve the six samples and read the iron and the apatite

ClickSolve feed, tail and concentrate of both ores, in rounds if needed (phosphorus to apatite, potassium to orthoclase and biotite, sodium to albite, then the rest). Read the modal mineralogy and, for each feed, the iron carried by magnetite against the total iron.
ExpectMagnetite dominant in the concentrates, the silicates concentrated in the tails, apatite tracking the P2O5 assay (small in ore 1, large in ore 2).
WhyThe maximum iron recovery and the P2O5 question both come from these numbers.
Iron in actinolite, diopside and biotite is real iron that no magnetite concentrate can recover; that is the whole meaning of "maximum".
8

Exercise 3: the two process numbers

ClickMaximum iron recovery of Feed1 and Feed2: iron in magnetite over total iron. Theoretical magnetite concentrate with apatite unseparated: P2O5 carried by apatite over the summed mass of magnetite and apatite. Write both with their formulas.
ExpectTwo recoveries below 100 % and two P2O5 grades, the second ore's far higher than the first's.
WhyThe first time a modal composition becomes a product specification; the argument returns in M7.
Report P2O5, not P: the factor is 2.291.
9

The assumptions note

ClickA few lines: every assumption you had to make in the three exercises (compositions taken, elements shared between hosts, the residual phase, the basis) and, for each, what measurement would remove it.
ExpectThe seed of sheet 3 of Assignment 1.
WhyThe Canvas page asks for it by name; the assignment is graded on exactly this.
10

Keep the three files

ClickSave the three calculation files with the mineral list, the compositions and the round structure visible rather than buried, together with the note.
ExpectFiles you can open again the week the assignment is due.
WhyEvery difficulty of Assignment 1 appears in miniature here; the files are your worked examples.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the first one-round solve as the answer, or deleting an identified phase to remove a negative.
  • Element and oxide bases mixed in one matrix (Exercise 3), or phosphorus reported as P where P2O5 was asked.
  • End-member compositions where the instruction gives deposit-specific ones (Pyhäsalmi sphalerite and pyrite; Lund's Malmberget silicates).
  • A silver or iron distribution presented as a measurement: they are inferred from the compositions and depend on the assumptions.
  • No residual reported, so nobody can say whether the mineral list was right.
  • The assumptions note skipped: it is the part of the session that the assignment reuses.
