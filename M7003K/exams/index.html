Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 1 · 6 p · T1 · Bjerrum diagrams, critical pH, collectors · module 5

2014-10-27 · question 1

Reads the Bjerrum diagram for lead: where n-hexyl xanthate can float galena, which xanthate chain still lets chromate depress it, whether thiosulphate depresses on its own, and the critical pH for a dithiophosphate collector from its solubility product.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

How to read a Bjerrum diagram, once and for all. The vertical axis is pPb=−log⁡{Pb2+}, the negative logarithm of the free lead-ion activity: the diagram puts small pPb (much lead in solution) at the top and large pPb (very little lead) at the bottom. Each straight line is the solubility limit of one solid: below the line that solid cannot exist, above it the metal ions precipitate it. Where a metal-collector line lies below the hydroxide line, the collector salt is the more insoluble solid, so it forms on the mineral surface and the mineral floats. The two lines cross at the critical pH; above it hydroxide wins and the mineral is depressed. The examiner's answer is in the fold above; here is the reasoning behind each part, and the redrawn diagram above the question shows each step in colour.

Note

Words used here. Activity {Pb2+}: the effective concentration of the free ion, in mol/l (M). Solubility product Ks: for a salt PbX2 the product {Pb2+}{X−}2 at saturation; the smaller it is, the less soluble the salt. Collector: the reagent (here a xanthate) that makes the mineral surface water-repellent so it can float. Depressant: a reagent (chromate, hydroxide) that takes the surface instead and stops flotation.

a) Where n-hexyl xanthate floats galena. The hexyl xanthate line stays below the lead hydroxide line up to their crossing at pH 12.2: flotation is possible at any pH below 12.2. That is nearly the whole useful range, which is why long-chain xanthates are strong but unselective collectors.

b) The longest chain that still lets chromate depress galena. Chromate (0.001 M) depresses by forming lead chromate; it can only win if its line lies below the xanthate line, i.e. lead chromate is less soluble than the lead xanthate. In the diagram the amyl (5 carbons) xanthate line is above the chromate line, the hexyl line is below it. So amyl xanthate (five carbons) is the longest chain that chromate can still beat.

c) Thiosulphate on its own. The 0.005 M thiosulphate line lies above every xanthate line: lead thiosulphate is more soluble than any lead xanthate, so thiosulphate cannot displace the collector. No, on its own it does not depress galena (its real effect is indirect: it consumes oxidants and reacts with dissolved copper).

d) Critical pH for a new collector from its solubility product. Write the solubility product of the collector salt Pb(p-CDTP)2 and put in the collector activity the question gives:

Ks={Pb2+}{p-CDTP−}2=7.7×10−18M3,{p-CDTP−}=3.0×10−5M
{Pb2+}=7.7×10−18(3.0×10−5)2=7.7×10−189.0×10−10=8.56×10−9M⇒pPb=−log⁡(8.56×10−9)=8.07

That is a horizontal line at pPb=8.07 in the diagram. Where it meets the lead hydroxide line is the critical pH: read off pH ≈ 10.6. Below 10.6 the dithiophosphate wins the surface (flotation); above, hydroxide does.

Why

The whole topic in one sentence: the mineral floats when the collector salt is the most insoluble thing that its metal ions can make at that pH. The diagram is only a plot of solubility products; if the exam gives you the numbers instead of the picture, the same calculation as in d) gives the answer (see variant C, the 2015 exam).

Note

Marking in 2014: 1 + 1 + 1 + 3 points. The calculation in d) carries half the marks; write the Ks expression, the {Pb2+} value and the pPb explicitly before reading the pH.

Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 2 · 6 p · T2 · frothers, cyanide, secondary concentration · module 5

2014-10-27 · question 2

Asks what secondary concentration in a flotation froth is and how it can be influenced and controlled.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above (3 + 3 points). In plain words:

a) What secondary concentration is. The froth is not a passive raft. Between the bubbles there are thin water films (lamellae) that drain downwards. Particles that are only weakly hydrophobic, and gangue that came up by entrainment in the water, are carried down with that drainage and fall back into the pulp. The strongly hydrophobic particles stay attached to the bubbles and move up. So the top of the froth is richer than the bottom: the froth concentrates a second time, after the collection in the pulp. That is secondary concentration.

b) How to influence and control it.

  • Froth depth. A deeper froth gives the lamellae more time to drain: higher grade, lower recovery. A shallow froth does the opposite. Cell level control sets it.
  • Wash water. Water sprayed on top of the froth (standard in flotation columns, also in some cells) replaces the entrained pulp water with clean water and washes gangue down: higher grade at the same recovery.
  • Secondary levers: frother type and dosage (a more stable froth drains less), air rate and froth removal rate (how long the froth lives before it is scraped), and the particle size (fine gangue is the most entrained).
Why

Secondary concentration is the reason a rougher concentrate can be much richer than the collection kinetics alone predict, and the reason columns with wash water make cleaner concentrates than mechanical cells. King treats the froth phase in section 9.7 (drainage through Plateau borders); the course's flotation slides have the froth thickness and wash water arguments.

Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 3 · 6 p · T3 · AG and SAG mills, critical stones · module 2

2014-10-27 · question 3

Asks for the physical and chemical reasons why fully autogenous grinding gives a better process result than conventional grinding.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above: two causes, one physical and one chemical, three points each. Here is the same answer with the reasoning behind each line, so that it can be rebuilt instead of memorised.

1. Physical: how the rock breaks. In an autogenous mill the ore grinds the ore. Big lumps tumble and rub against smaller ones, so most breakage is attrition and abrasion, not impact. Abrasion breaks rock preferentially along the weakest surfaces, which are the grain boundaries between minerals. The result is more particles that are one mineral only, i.e. better liberation at the same size, and fewer flat, shattered fragments. Steel balls in a conventional mill break by impact through the grains, which liberates less and makes more fines.

2. Chemical: what the pulp contains. Steel media wear away as iron. In a conventional mill that iron dissolves and oxidises: the pulp becomes reducing, oxygen is consumed, and colloidal iron hydroxides precipitate. Those hydroxides coat every mineral surface and make flotation less selective (they hide the surfaces the collector should find) and hurt magnetic separation. An AG mill adds no iron, so the pulp stays oxidising and clean, and the downstream separations work as the chemistry predicts.

How to write it for six points. One paragraph per cause with the mechanism and the consequence: liberation for the physical cause, surface chemistry for the chemical cause. The examiner explicitly notes that "better economics" alone is not the answer to this question.

Why

The course keeps coming back to this pair because it explains why LKAB and Boliden grind autogenously: iron ores for the magnetic separation (no iron slimes, no reducing pulp) and sulphide ores for the flotation (clean surfaces, selective liberation). Chapter 5.11 of King gives the model of the AG mill; the course slides SC-Autogenous.pdf give the plant reasons.

Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 4 · 6 p · T7 · W, Ni, Ta minerals and their separation · module 11

2014-10-27 · question 4

Asks for the two most important tungsten minerals and how each is concentrated.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above: two minerals, three points each. The reasoning that makes it easy to remember: each mineral's concentration method follows from one physical property.

Wolframite, (Fe,Mn)WO4. Density 7.1 to 7.5 g/cm³, so it is separated from the quartz gangue by gravity (jigs, spirals, shaking tables, dense media for lump). It contains iron and manganese, which make it weakly magnetic, so wet high-intensity magnetic separation (WHIMS) cleans the gravity concentrate and separates it from cassiterite and scheelite, which are non-magnetic. Flotation is rarely needed.

Scheelite, CaWO4. Density 5.9 to 6.1 g/cm³: gravity works for the coarse grains, but scheelite is brittle and much of it ends up as fines that gravity loses. It is a calcium mineral, so it floats with fatty acids (oleate, tall oil) at pH 9 to 10.5, with sodium silicate to depress the silicate gangue; the difficulty is separating it from calcite, fluorite and apatite, which share the calcium chemistry (the Petrov process, warm pulp with a large sodium silicate dose, is the classic answer). So gravity supplemented with flotation.

Bonus points if asked "how": tungsten concentrates are sold on WO3 content (65 % WO3 and above), and the grade is reached by cleaning with WHIMS, tables and flotation of the fines; the concentrate is then converted to ammonium paratungstate by hydrometallurgy.

Why

Module 11 (SC-Alloying metals.pdf) covers tungsten, niobium, tantalum and the other alloying metals; the examiner's 2014 solution gives the two-line version of this answer.

Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 5 · 6 p · T7 · W, Ni, Ta minerals and their separation · module 7

2014-10-27 · question 5

Asks which methods separate pyrrhotite from the disulphides pyrite and arsenopyrite.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above: magnetism (2 p) and selective flotation at high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp (4 p). Why those two, in plain words.

The problem. Pyrrhotite (Fe1-xS) and the disulphides pyrite (FeS2), arsenopyrite (FeAsS), gudmundite (FeSbS) and loellingite (FeAs2) all contain iron(II), all are dense (4.6 to 6.3 g/cm³, too close for gravity) and all respond to xanthate. They must be separated because pyrrhotite dilutes pyrite concentrates, consumes reagents and oxygen, and carries little value, while arsenopyrite often carries the gold.

Method 1: magnetic separation. Monoclinic pyrrhotite is ferrimagnetic, strongly enough for low-intensity magnetic separators (wet drums) to pull it out of a ground pulp; pyrite and the other disulphides are only weakly magnetic (paramagnetic) and stay. Cheap, no reagents, used as a pre-step before flotation. Limitation: hexagonal pyrrhotite is only weakly magnetic, and fine intergrowths follow the magnetic grains.

Method 2: flotation with high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp (about pH 10.8). In alkaline pulp iron hydroxide covers the pyrrhotite surface and no iron xanthate forms: pyrrhotite is depressed. The disulphides have sulphur (or arsenic, antimony) pairs at the surface where the collector oxidises to dixanthogen, which adsorbs physically and strongly, so pyrite and arsenopyrite float even at a pH where pyrrhotite does not. A high xanthate dosage is needed to reach that dixanthogen state. Soda ash rather than lime, because calcium would depress the disulphides too.

Combining them is common: LIMS takes the bulk of the pyrrhotite, flotation cleans the rest and yields a pyrite or arsenopyrite (gold) concentrate.

Why

This is the same Bjerrum logic as the lead questions applied to iron: iron xanthates are soluble and iron hydroxide is very stable, so iron sulphides cannot float through an iron-xanthate salt; they float only when the collector oxidises to dixanthogen on their surface, which pyrrhotite cannot do at high pH. Module 7 (complex ores) and the 2014 solution are the sources.

Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 6 · 6 p · T8 · sub-level caving, cut-and-fill · module 3

2014-10-27 · question 6

Asks for the main operations of a sub-level caving mine and the prerequisites of the method.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above: prerequisites (2 p) and the six operations (4 p). Here is the mental picture that makes the list natural.

The idea of sub-level caving. The mine is developed from the footwall side in horizontal levels 20 to 30 m apart (the sub-levels). From each sub-level, drifts are driven across the ore body. Above each drift the ore is drilled in fans and blasted slice by slice, from the hanging wall towards the footwall; as the blasted ore is drawn out at the drift, the rock above caves and follows it down. Nothing is left standing: the hanging wall is allowed to collapse into the void. LKAB's Kiruna mine is the world's largest example.

Prerequisites.

  • A large, steeply dipping ore body with continuity at depth, so that the levels can be repeated downward for decades.
  • A stable footwall (competent gangue) to hold the permanent openings: ramp, shafts, footwall drifts, ore passes.
  • An ore and hanging wall that cave and break up when undercut, and surface land that may subside (the caving reaches the surface).
  • Tolerance for dilution and ore loss at the draw points, since waste from the cave mixes with the ore (the reason gravity or magnetic cobbing precedes the mill).

The operations, in order.

  1. Development: ramp or incline and the footwall drifts of each transport level; shafts and ore passes.
  2. Drifting: on every sub-level, drifts driven from the footwall into and across the ore.
  3. Production drilling: long-hole rigs drill fans of holes upward into the ore above each drift.
  4. Charging and blasting: one fan at a time; the blasted ore fills the draw point at the drift face.
  5. Mucking (loading): LHD machines load the broken ore from the draw point and tip it into ore passes in the footwall; drawing stops when the waste content becomes too high.
  6. Transport and hoisting: the ore passes feed the main level, where trains or trucks take the ore to crushers and shafts, and it is hoisted to the surface.
Why

For the mineral-processing course the point is the interface: sub-level caving delivers a run-of-mine feed with variable waste dilution and lumps up to a metre, which is why Kiruna has underground crushing, cobbing and autogenous grinding. Module 3 and 9 (the geology lectures with the mining methods) are the source, together with the 2014 solution.

2014-10-27 · question 7

Water balance of a pilot circuit (mill, hydrocyclone, Wright Impact Tray): dilution ratios of the cyclone overflow, cyclone feed and WIT tailings, and the circulating load, from the solids flows, three water additions and two known dilution ratios.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

What the question really asks. Three streams leave or circulate without a dilution ratio (cyclone overflow, cyclone feed, WIT tailings). Everything else is given. Two balances solve it: solids first, then water. The examiner's own answer is in the fold above; the numbers below reproduce it.

Note

Words used here. Dilution ratio D=water/solids of a stream: kilograms of water per kilogram of solids (1 litre of water weighs 1 kg, so l/min of water divided by kg/min of solids). Balance: "what goes in equals what comes out" around a box you draw yourself, either the whole circuit or one unit. Circulating load: the solids that go round again, as a percentage of the fresh feed.

Step 1. Solids around the whole circuit. At steady state what goes in comes out. Only two streams leave: the cyclone overflow and the WIT concentrate.

overflow=feed−concentrate=14.3−3.2=11.1kg/min

Step 2. Solids around each unit.

  • Cyclone: feed=underflow+overflow=29.5+11.1=40.6kg/min.
  • WIT: it receives the cyclone underflow (29.5) and splits it into concentrate (3.2) and tailings: 29.5−3.2=26.3kg/min, which go back to the mill.

Step 3. Circulating load. The recirculating solids divided by the fresh feed:

CL=solids back to the millfresh feed×100=26.314.3×100=184%

Step 4. Water around the whole circuit. Dilution ratio D=water/solids, so water=D×solids (1 l of water is 1 kg). All the fresh water, 8.5+9.0+10.7=28.2 l/min, leaves with the two products:

Woverflow=28.2−3.2×0.92=28.2−2.94=25.26l/min⇒Doverflow=25.2611.1=2.28

Step 5. Water around the cyclone. Its feed water is the sum of the two outlets:

Wcyclone feed=29.5×0.37+25.26=10.92+25.26=36.17⇒Dcyclone feed=36.1740.6=0.89

Step 6. Water around the WIT. In: the underflow water plus the two water additions; out: concentrate and tailings.

Wtailings=10.92+9.0+10.7−2.94=27.67⇒Dtailings=27.6726.3=1.05

Step 7. Close the loop. Mill water in = 8.5 (fresh) +27.67 (tailings) =36.17 = the cyclone feed water found in step 5. The balance closes, so the three answers are consistent.

Reads the Bjerrum diagram for copper, zinc and manganese with the collector DIDA: the pH window for zinc flotation, how copper-sulphate activation widens it, and the order of the metal-collector compounds by solubility, with reasons.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The diagram this time has three metals and one collector, DIDA, at 0.00001 M. For each metal there are two lines: the metal-DIDA salt and the metal hydroxide. The circles mark where they cross, i.e. the critical pH of each metal. Reading them from the figure: manganese about pH 10.5, zinc about pH 10.2, copper about pH 11. Note

Words used here. DIDA: a thiol-type collector (a dithiocarbamate); its anion DIDA− forms an insoluble salt with a metal ion the way xanthate does. pMe: −log⁡ of the free metal-ion activity, plotted downward. a) Zinc minerals with DIDA (1 p). The Zn-DIDA line lies below the Zn(OH)2 line up to their crossing: flotation is possible at pH below about 10.2. Above that, zinc hydroxide takes the surface.

b) Sphalerite activated with copper sulphate (1 p). Copper ions replace zinc on the sphalerite surface, so the surface now behaves as a copper mineral and the copper lines apply. The Cu-DIDA line stays below the Cu(OH)2 line up to about pH 11: activation extends the flotation window by nearly one pH unit, and the copper-DIDA salt is far less soluble, so the surface is more strongly hydrophobic at every pH.

c) Order of solubility, with reasons (4 p). All three lines are drawn for the same collector concentration, so at any pH the line height gives the metal-ion activity in equilibrium with the solid salt: the lower the line (higher pMe), the less soluble the salt. In the diagram the Cu-DIDA line is the lowest, Zn-DIDA in the middle, Mn-DIDA the highest. Increasing solubility:

Cu-DIDA < Zn-DIDA < Mn-DIDA

Why: the solubility product Ks={Me2+}{DIDA−}2 is smallest for copper. Copper(II) and, less so, zinc are soft, polarisable ions that form partly covalent bonds with the sulphur of a thiol-type collector; manganese(II) is a hard ion that only forms ionic, weakly bound and therefore soluble salts. The same order (Cu < Zn < Mn, and generally Cu < Pb < Zn < Fe < Mn) holds for xanthates and explains the classic selectivity of sulphide flotation: copper minerals float first and at the highest pH, manganese-bearing minerals hardly at all.

Why

Two things the examiner is checking: that you read "below the line = floats" correctly, and that you connect the picture to the chemistry (Ks and the softness of the metal ion). 2014-12-22 · question 2

Asks for the two different uses of cyanide as a modifier in sulphide flotation and the purpose of each.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Cyanide in sulphide flotation is a modifier with two jobs, and the exam wants both named with their purpose.

1. Depressant for iron sulphides and sphalerite in the copper or lead rougher. Small dosages of NaCN (tens of grams per tonne) stop pyrite, pyrrhotite, arsenopyrite and sphalerite from taking up xanthate. On pyrite the cyanide forms iron-cyanide complexes at the surface and removes the iron sites where dixanthogen would form; on sphalerite it complexes copper and zinc ions so that the surface cannot be activated. Purpose: selectivity, so that only the copper minerals (or galena) float in the first stage. Usually dosed together with zinc sulphate for sphalerite.

2. Deactivation, i.e. removal of activating copper ions from sphalerite (and from pyrite). In complex ores, copper ions dissolved during grinding settle on sphalerite and activate it, so it floats where it should not. Cyanide dissolves those copper ions as very stable copper-cyanide complexes and strips the activation. Purpose: reverse an unwanted activation before the zinc circuit, or keep sphalerite out of the copper concentrate.

A third use worth one line: at higher dosage cyanide depresses the copper sulphides themselves, which is used in copper-molybdenum separation (molybdenite floats while chalcopyrite is depressed) and in some Cu-Pb separations.

The cost of using it: cyanide also dissolves gold and silver (losses to solution), is toxic, needs alkaline pulp to stay as CN− and not as HCN gas, and the plant needs destruction of the residual cyanide in the tailings.

Why

Both uses come from the same chemistry: cyanide forms extremely stable complexes with copper and iron. Where those metals are the activators (copper on sphalerite) or the collector's anchor (iron on pyrite), taking them away removes the flotation. The Boliden and Garpenberg circuits in the module 7 material are the practical examples.

2014-12-22 · question 3

Asks which process options remove or reduce the accumulation of critical stones in autogenous grinding.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

First say what a critical stone is. In an AG mill, pieces of about 20 to 50 mm (the limits depend on the ore) are too small to act as grinding media and too big to be ground quickly by the rest of the charge. They accumulate in the mill, take up volume and draw power without producing fines. The 2015 retake asks the same question with "diminish"; the answer is the same list.

The process options, from the cheapest to the most expensive.

  1. Take them out and break them elsewhere (pebble crushing). Pebble ports in the grate let the critical size leave the mill; a screen separates the pebbles and a cone crusher breaks them to a size the mill can grind; the crushed product returns to the mill feed. This is the standard remedy in modern AG circuits.
  2. Use them as media in a second mill (pebble mill). In two-stage autogenous circuits the primary mill's critical-size product becomes the grinding media of the secondary pebble mill: the problem turns into a resource.
  3. Change the feed so fewer are made. Blend the run-of-mine feed so that enough competent large lumps arrive (the media), and avoid feeding the 20-50 mm range directly: pre-screening, or crushing the intermediate fraction before the mill.
  4. Add steel balls (semi-autogenous grinding). A few percent of large balls break the critical size by impact. It works, at the price of steel consumption and iron in the pulp, which is exactly what fully autogenous grinding wanted to avoid.
  5. Adjust the mill itself. A larger grate opening and pebble ports, higher mill speed or a different lifter design change the balance between building up and breaking the critical size. These are fine-tuning, not a cure.

In the exam: define the critical stone in one sentence, give the options with the reason each works, and rank them: pebble crushing as the main answer, SAG as the fallback, feed control as the preventive measure.

Why

Why the problem exists at all: breakage rate as a function of size in an AG mill has a minimum in the middle. Big rocks break by self-impact, fine ones by abrasion between big rocks, and the middle size has neither mechanism. King treats it in section 5.13 (breakage rates in autogenous mills); the course slides SC-Autogenous.pdf show the plant circuits with pebble crushers.

2014-12-22 · question 6

Asks under what conditions gold minerals can be floated.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Gold floats well, on three conditions: the right size, a clean surface, and the right chemistry.

1. Size. Native gold is heavy and malleable; grains coarser than about 100 to 150 µm are too heavy for a bubble to lift and are flattened rather than broken in the mill. Those go to gravity. Flotation handles gold from roughly 10 to 100 µm, and gold-bearing sulphide particles of normal flotation size.

2. Surface. Gold floats when its surface is clean and unoxidised: no iron-hydroxide slimes (autogenous grinding, no reducing pulp), no coatings from grinding media, freshly ground. Tarnished or coated gold floats poorly; a short conditioning with a little copper sulphate (activation) or a sulphidiser can help.

3. Chemistry.

  • Collectors: xanthates (amyl for strength), dithiophosphates and especially the mercaptobenzothiazole and thionocarbamate types, which are selective gold collectors; moderate dosages.
  • pH: neutral to mildly alkaline, about 7 to 9, with soda ash rather than lime. High lime concentrations depress native gold (calcium films), which is why gold plants avoid the pH 11 to 12 used for copper-pyrite ores.
  • Frother: MIBC or a glycol; a stable froth helps the flaky gold particles stay attached.
  • Pyrite: when the gold sits in or on pyrite, float the pyrite too (no depression: neutral pH, enough collector) and treat the concentrate afterwards.

4. Circuit. Gold flotation is usually a bulk sulphide-gold rougher with a scavenger, gravity in the grinding circuit for the coarse gold, and a concentrate that goes to smelting or to cyanide leaching.

Why

The trap in this question is lime: what is right for a copper ore (pH 11.5) is wrong for free gold. Answer with the three conditions and the reagents that meet them; the Björkdal question (variant A) shows the plant that does exactly this.

Asks what semi-autogenous grinding is, what characterises it, when it is applied, and its advantages and disadvantages.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Four parts in one question: what, what characterises it, when, and pros and cons. Answer them in that order with a heading each; the examiner marks by part.

What it is. Semi-autogenous grinding (SAG) is autogenous grinding with a small charge of large steel balls, typically 2 to 12 % of the mill volume (often 4 to 8 %), added to an ore charge that otherwise grinds itself.

What characterises it.

  • Large-diameter, short mills with grates, like AG mills, running at a similar speed.
  • Breakage is a mix: the balls add impact breakage of the coarse and critical sizes to the abrasion that the ore provides.
  • Higher and more stable power draw than AG on the same ore, because the ball charge is denser than rock.
  • Usually one stage from a primary-crushed feed to a size that a ball mill or a cyclone circuit can finish; often closed with a pebble crusher.

When it is applied.

  • When the ore is not competent enough to provide its own media, or the feed is variable (soft and hard zones), so that a fully autogenous mill would starve for media or choke on critical stones.
  • When throughput matters more than the last percent of liberation or steel consumption: large-tonnage copper and gold plants.
  • As a way to skip secondary and tertiary crushing: SAG accepts a coarse primary-crusher product.

Advantages. Robust against feed changes; fewer critical-stone problems; higher throughput per mill; simpler flowsheet than crushing plus rod and ball mills; lower capital cost per tonne.

Disadvantages. Steel consumption (balls and liners) and the cost that goes with it; iron in the pulp (reducing conditions, iron-hydroxide slimes) that AG avoided, so flotation or magnetic separation can be a little less selective; more impact breakage means more fines and less grain-boundary liberation than AG; higher energy per tonne than a well-run AG mill on a competent ore.

Why

The short version the examiner wants: SAG buys stability and throughput with steel, and pays for it in the pulp chemistry and the steel bill. The AG question of the 2014 paper (variant A) gives the two reasons why AG is cleaner; SAG gives up part of both.

Asks how the gold mineralogy at Björkdal (mostly native gold grains, some gold with pyrite) shaped the design of the flowsheet.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Start from the two gold forms and let each dictate a process step.

1. Most of the gold is native, in coarse grains: gravity, early and inside the grinding circuit. Free gold grains are heavy (19 g/cm³ against 2.7 for quartz) and malleable, so they do not break: they liberate early and would otherwise circulate in the mill and flatten. Björkdal therefore recovers gravity gold from the grinding circuit (from the cyclone underflow or the mill discharge) with jigs, spirals and shaking tables or centrifugal concentrators, producing a high-grade gravity concentrate that is smelted directly. This step takes a large share of the gold before any reagent is used, cheaply and without cyanide.

2. Some gold is with pyrite: flotation of the sulphides. The gold that gravity misses (finer grains, gold locked in or attached to pyrite) is recovered by flotation of the sulphides with a xanthate or dithiophosphate at near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH, without lime excess (lime would depress gold). The gold-bearing pyrite concentrate is sold to a smelter or treated further.

3. Consequences for the flowsheet layout.

  • Grinding is closed with the gravity unit so that free gold is pulled out as soon as it is liberated, instead of over-grinding it.
  • The grind can stay relatively coarse because the flotation only needs to liberate pyrite grains, not fine gold.
  • Two products (gravity gold, flotation concentrate) instead of one, and no cyanide plant at the mine: lower cost and simpler permitting.
  • Tailings carry little gold because the two gold forms are each caught by the method suited to them.
Why

The general rule the examiner wants: match the recovery method to the gold form. Free coarse gold → gravity; gold in sulphides → flotation; fine and refractory gold → leaching after oxidation. Björkdal is the course's example (App_Björkdal_A3.pdf and SC-Precious metal.pdf in module 12).

2015-10-26 · question 6

Asks which fill materials are common in cut-and-fill mining, how they are produced and what is demanded of the fill.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Three parts again: which fills, how they are made, what they must do. Two points each.

What cut-and-fill is, in one sentence. The ore is mined in horizontal slices from the bottom up (or top down); after each slice the void is filled, and the fill becomes the floor (or the roof) for the next slice, so the walls of narrow, irregular or weak ore bodies stay supported. Common in small and medium Swedish mines (Boliden area, Garpenberg, Zinkgruvan).

Types of fill in use today, and how they are won.

  1. Waste rock fill. Development waste (drifts, ramps) and sometimes crushed rock from surface quarries or pre-concentration rejects, tipped dry into the stope by LHD or truck. Cheapest where waste is available underground.
  2. Hydraulic fill (sand fill). Deslimed flotation tailings from the concentrator: the tailings are cycloned to remove the fines (slimes would not drain), the sand fraction is pumped as a slurry through boreholes and pipes into the stope, where the water drains off through barricades and is pumped out. Sometimes natural sand from eskers or gravel pits is used.
  3. Cemented fill, paste fill. Tailings (including the fine fraction) thickened to a paste, mixed with 3 to 7 % cement or slag binder, and pumped into the stope; it hardens, needs no drainage and can stand as a free wall when the neighbouring stope is mined. Cemented rock fill mixes waste rock with a cement slurry for the same reason.

Demands on the fill.

  • Strength and stiffness: it must carry LHD traffic and workers (floor) and stand as a vertical wall when the adjacent slice or stope is mined (cemented fills), without liquefying.
  • Drainage and placement: hydraulic fill must drain fast (hence the desliming and a permeability specification), the barricades must hold, and paste must be pumpable through long pipelines without segregating.
  • Small settlement and shrinkage, so the next slice starts on a firm, level floor.
  • Cost and availability: binder content is the main cost, so the grading of the tailings is chosen to need as little cement as possible; the fill must be available at the mine's own rate of production.
  • Environment and safety: using tailings as fill reduces the surface tailings pond; the fill must not release acid or metals, and the cement must not create dust or heat problems.
Why

The processing connection the examiner is after: the concentrator's tailings are the mine's fill, so the tailings' size distribution, water and chemistry are designed with the mine in mind (desliming cyclones, paste plants). Module 3 and 9 (mining methods with the geology) are the source.

2015-12-22 · question 1

Reads the Bjerrum diagram for iron: which pH and other conditions keep carboxylic-acid collectors (used to float apatite off magnetite) from adsorbing on magnetite surfaces.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The situation. LKAB-type ore: magnetite with apatite as the phosphorus carrier. The apatite is floated off with a fatty-acid collector (carboxylate: oleate, palmitate). The danger is that the same collector precipitates as an iron carboxylate on the magnetite surface, which then floats with the apatite and dirties the phosphate concentrate while losing iron. The Bjerrum diagram for iron shows where the iron-carboxylate salts and the iron hydroxides are stable.

Reading the diagram. The hydroxide lines (Fe(OH)3 on the left axis, pFe3+; Fe(OH)2 on the right axis, pFe2+) slope down steeply; the carboxylate lines (a palmitic acid, b 0.2 mM palmeate, c oleic acid, d 0.2 mM oleate) cross them at moderate pH. Silicate (e) and phosphate (f) lines lie below the carboxylate lines over most of the range: the iron silicate and iron phosphate surfaces are more stable than iron oleate.

Conditions that keep the collector off the magnetite.

  1. High pH, about 9.5 to 10.5. Above the crossing points the iron hydroxides are the least soluble solids: iron ions at the surface stay as Fe(OH)3 / Fe(OH)2 and no iron-oleate can form. Apatite still floats because calcium oleate is stable there.
  2. Keep dissolved iron low. No reducing pulp, no steel wear: autogenous grinding (which LKAB uses) and no acid conditions. Less Fe2+ and Fe3+ in solution means the carboxylate lines are never reached.
  3. A depressant for the iron oxides: sodium silicate or phosphate. Their lines lie below the oleate line, so silicate and phosphate ions win the magnetite surface (lines e and f in the diagram), which then stays hydrophilic. Sodium silicate also disperses slimes.
  4. Minimum collector dosage and a collector that is selective. The 0.2 mM lines (b, d) already cross the hydroxide lines; a higher dosage moves the carboxylate lines up and widens the range where iron carboxylate is possible. Use tall-oil fatty acids or their emulsions at the lowest dose that floats the apatite, with a conditioning that lets calcium sites on apatite adsorb first.
  5. Temperature and water quality. Warm pulp helps the oleate stay dissolved and selective; hard water (Ca2+, Mg2+) precipitates the collector and should be avoided.
Why

The same logic as the lead questions, turned around: there the collector was wanted on the surface, so the question was the highest pH where the collector salt still wins. Here the collector is unwanted on magnetite, so the answer is the pH range where the hydroxide wins and a depressant whose iron salt is even more stable. Flotation chemistry is one diagram read in two directions.

2015-12-22 · question 2

Asks for the purpose of the frother, the effects of under- and over-dosing it, and examples of common frothers.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Three parts: purpose, the two dosing errors, examples. Two points each.

Purpose of the frother. A surface-active molecule that adsorbs at the air-water interface, lowers its surface tension and slows the coalescence of bubbles. Result: many small bubbles of stable size in the pulp (more collision surface for the particles) and a froth on top that is stable long enough to be scraped off but breaks in the launder. The frother does not adsorb on the minerals; that is the collector's job.

Under-dosage. Bubbles coalesce into a few large ones: less bubble surface, fewer particle-bubble collisions, coarse particles fall off. The froth is thin and brittle, it collapses before it reaches the lip. Recovery drops, especially of the coarse and the weakly hydrophobic particles.

Over-dosage. The froth becomes too stable and voluminous: it overflows uncontrolled, carries entrained gangue and water (lower grade), does not break in launders and pumps, and the effect can last through the whole circuit (cleaners flooded, "froth in the pump sump"). Above a certain point the extra frother even reduces recovery because the bubbles become so small and stable that they stop rising properly.

Common frothers. MIBC (methyl isobutyl carbinol), the classic selective frother with a short-lived froth; pine oil and cresylic acid, the older natural frothers; polyglycols such as Dowfroth 250 (polypropylene glycol ethers) for a stronger, more persistent froth; TEB (triethoxybutane); blends of alcohols and glycols.

Why

The rule of thumb the course gives: choose the frother by the froth you need (brittle and selective for cleaners, strong for coarse roughing) and dose to the minimum that keeps the froth stable to the lip. Secondary concentration (variant A of this topic) is what a well-dosed froth does for grade.

2015-12-22 · question 6

Asks for methods to concentrate iron ores in which hematite is the main iron mineral.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Hematite is weakly magnetic, so the magnetite tricks do not work. Three families of methods, plus the two that prepare the ore.

Preparation. Crushing and screening: high-grade hematite ore is sold as lump (6 to 30 mm) and fines directly, without concentration. Washing and desliming with hydrocyclones removes clays and ultrafines that would spoil every later step. Grinding only to the liberation size, which for coarse hematite can be several hundred micrometres.

1. Gravity separation. Hematite (5.26 g/cm³) against quartz (2.65): a large density contrast. Jigs for the coarse fractions, spirals for 0.1 to 1 mm sand-sized material (the workhorse of Australian and Brazilian plants), shaking tables and Reichert cones for fine sand, dense-medium separation for lump. Cheap, no reagents; loses the fines.

2. High-intensity and high-gradient magnetic separation. Wet high-intensity magnetic separators (WHIMS) and the pulsating high-gradient SLon machines create fields and gradients strong enough to pull weakly magnetic hematite (and goethite) out of the gangue, down to a few tens of micrometres. Used as a stage after gravity or after desliming to recover the fines.

3. Flotation. For the fine fraction and for itabirite-type ores: reverse cationic flotation (an amine floats the quartz, starch depresses the iron oxides, pH around 10) is the industrial standard; direct anionic flotation with fatty acids (oleate) floats the hematite itself, less selective in the presence of slimes.

4. Magnetising roasting. Reduce the hematite to magnetite in a roaster (or by a reducing gas), then use the cheap low-intensity magnetic separation. Energy-intensive, so used only where the other routes fail.

The typical plant stacks them: crushing, screening, desliming, spirals for the sand, WHIMS or SLon and reverse flotation for the fines, with the slimes to tailings.

Why

Recommends flotation conditions (reagents, pH) for the rougher and cleaner stages of a copper ore made of chalcopyrite, covellite, bornite, 8-15 % pyrite, quartz and kaolinite.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Read the mineralogy like a checklist. Three copper sulphides of different character (chalcopyrite, and the secondary sulphides covellite and bornite, which release copper ions and tarnish), a lot of pyrite (8 to 15 %, the enemy), quartz (inert) and kaolinite (clay: slimes, viscosity, coatings). Then give the rougher and the cleaner their own conditions.

Rougher stage.

  1. Grind to liberation, about 100 to 150 µm, preferably in an autogenous or SAG mill so the pulp carries little iron; keep the pulp density at 30 to 35 % solids so the clay does not thicken it.
  2. Deal with the clay first: a dispersant (sodium silicate, 200 to 500 g/t) and, if needed, a desliming or a lower pulp density, so kaolinite does not coat the sulphides or ride into the concentrate by entrainment.
  3. pH 10.5 to 11.5 with lime. This is the pyrite depression: calcium films on pyrite and no dixanthogen at high pH, while chalcopyrite, bornite and covellite still float.
  4. Collector: an isopropyl or ethyl xanthate (20 to 60 g/t) for the chalcopyrite, plus a dithiophosphate (or an ester/thionocarbamate) that collects the secondary sulphides without collecting pyrite. Avoid long-chain amyl xanthate, which floats pyrite too.
  5. Frother: MIBC or a glycol, at the minimum that keeps a stable froth; kaolinite already stabilises froth.
  6. Expect a bulk copper concentrate of 10 to 15 % Cu with the entrained pyrite that the cleaners must remove; scavenger on the rougher tail, its concentrate back to the rougher feed.

Cleaner stage.

  1. Regrind the rougher concentrate (to 30 to 50 µm) to free the composite copper-pyrite particles; regrinding also exposes fresh surfaces.
  2. Raise the pH to 11.5 to 12 with lime in the cleaners: pyrite depression is the main job here; a small dose of cyanide (10 to 30 g/t) if the pyrite is stubborn, remembering that too much cyanide also depresses the secondary copper minerals.
  3. Less collector, more froth control: a deeper froth and, in columns, wash water, so the entrained pyrite and clay drain back (secondary concentration).
  4. Two or three cleaning stages with the cleaner tails scavenged and recycled; target 25 to 30 % Cu with pyrite below a few percent.
Why

The reasoning the examiner wants to see: every reagent answers one mineral in the list. Lime answers the 8 to 15 % pyrite, sodium silicate answers the kaolinite, the dithiophosphate answers covellite and bornite, regrinding answers the composites. Module 6 (copper ores, the Aitik flowsheet in the material) is the source.

2020-10-26 · question 2

Asks for the advantages of autogenous (AG) mills compared with conventional grinding.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Six points for a list, so give six advantages with a reason each. The 2014 paper asked why AG gives a better process result (variant A); the examiner's answer there, better liberation and a clean pulp, is the core of this one too.

  1. No grinding media to buy. The ore grinds itself: no balls, no rods, far lower steel consumption. Only liners wear.
  2. Cleaner pulp for the separation that follows. No iron from steel media, so the pulp is not reducing and no colloidal iron hydroxides coat the mineral surfaces. Flotation stays selective; magnetic separation is not fouled by iron slimes.
  3. Better liberation. Breakage by attrition and abrasion between ore pieces follows the grain boundaries, so the minerals are freed at a coarser size and with fewer shattered fines than by ball impact.
  4. A shorter flowsheet. An AG mill takes primary-crushed feed (hundreds of millimetres), so secondary and tertiary crushing and often the rod mill disappear. Fewer machines, fewer transfer points.
  5. High capacity in one unit. Large-diameter AG mills handle several thousand tonnes per hour; the coarse charge is also a buffer that smooths short feed variations.
  6. Lower operating cost per tonne when the ore is competent: media savings and fewer stages outweigh the somewhat higher specific energy.

And name the condition (the examiner likes it): all this requires a competent ore that supplies its own media; otherwise critical stones build up and the plant turns to pebble crushing or to SAG.

2020-10-26 · question 4

Asks what a Slon separator is and what its advantages are.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

What SLon is. A vertical-ring, pulsating, high-gradient wet magnetic separator, developed in China (Ganzhou) and now standard for fine, weakly magnetic minerals. Its parts:

  • A vertical ring that carries a matrix of magnetic rods (or expanded metal) through a strong electromagnet's field (up to about 1 to 1.3 T at the poles, with very high field gradients at the rod surfaces).
  • The pulp is fed from above into the matrix inside the magnetic zone; weakly magnetic particles (hematite, limonite, ilmenite, chromite, wolframite) are trapped on the rods, non-magnetics pass through and leave as tailings at the bottom.
  • A pulsating mechanism (a diaphragm) makes the pulp in the matrix oscillate up and down, so the trapped bed is loosened continuously and the mechanically entrapped gangue is washed out while the magnetics stay held.
  • As the ring rotates out of the field, the magnetic product is flushed off with water from the top into a launder, and the clean matrix rotates back into the feed zone.

Its advantages.

  1. High recovery of fine weakly magnetic minerals (down to 10 to 20 µm) thanks to the high gradient at the rods.
  2. High concentrate grade, because the pulsation and the top-flush remove the gangue that a static matrix would hold by entrapment.
  3. The matrix does not clog: vertical ring, feed from the top, flushing in the opposite direction, pulsation; no blocking by coarse particles or slimes, which was the weakness of the older horizontal-ring WHIMS.
  4. Low operating cost and simple operation: low water and energy per tonne, few moving parts, long matrix life, continuous operation.
  5. Large capacity in one unit and a wide feed size range (about 1 mm to 10 µm).

Where it is used: hematite and limonite ores (upgrading the fines after spirals), ilmenite from beach sands, removing iron from feldspar and quartz, wolframite and chromite fines.

Why

Why the examiner likes Slon: it solved the one thing that kept fine hematite in the tailings, entrapment and clogging in high-gradient matrices. Written exam, 2022-10-28 · Ex. 1 · 6 p · T4 · Cu and Pb-Zn flowsheets, reagents and pH · module 7

2022-10-28 · question 1

Recommends flotation conditions (reagents, pH) for separating a Cu-Pb-Zn ore of chalcopyrite, sphalerite, galena, 8-15 % pyrite, quartz and kaolinite through its various stages.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Same recipe as the copper question of 2020, one more stage. With chalcopyrite, sphalerite and galena in the same ore the plant floats sequentially: copper (and often lead with it) first, zinc second, pyrite to the tail. Kaolinite and pyrite (8 to 15 %) are the same nuisances as in 2020.

Stage 0. Grinding and slimes. Grind to liberation (100 to 150 µm), ideally autogenous (no iron in the pulp). Sodium silicate as dispersant for the kaolinite; moderate pulp density.

Stage 1. Copper-lead flotation (bulk Cu-Pb) at pH 9 to 10. Lime or soda ash for the pH. Depress sphalerite and pyrite with zinc sulphate + sodium cyanide (or sodium metabisulphite where cyanide is unwanted); collector a short-chain xanthate or a dithiophosphate, dosed low for selectivity. Product: a Cu-Pb bulk concentrate; the tail goes on to zinc flotation. If the ore is coarse-grained enough, copper and lead can also be floated one after the other (copper first with a dithiophosphate, galena depressed by dichromate or sulphite, then galena).

Stage 2. Copper-lead separation of the bulk concentrate. Two classic routes: depress galena with sodium dichromate (or SO2 / sodium sulphite plus starch) and float chalcopyrite; or depress chalcopyrite with cyanide (high dosage) and float galena. Choice depends on which mineral is the minor one and on the silver deportment.

Stage 3. Zinc flotation from the bulk tail at pH 10.5 to 11.5 with lime. Activate sphalerite with copper sulphate (200 to 500 g/t), then a longer-chain xanthate (isopropyl to amyl); the lime keeps pyrite depressed. Cleaning of the zinc concentrate at higher pH, sometimes with a little cyanide against the remaining pyrite.

Stage 4. Pyrite. Stays in the tail; if there is a market or an acid plant, a pyrite flotation at low pH (about 5 to 6, with amyl xanthate) can follow.

Everywhere: regrinding before cleaning, wash water or deep froth in the cleaners, scavengers on each tail with their concentrates recycled.

Why

The order of the stages follows the Bjerrum picture of this course: copper minerals float at the highest pH and with the least collector, galena next, sphalerite only when activated, pyrite last and only when nothing depresses it. Garpenberg and Boliden's complex ores in module 7 are the worked examples.

Written exam, 2022-10-28 · Ex. 2 · 6 p · T3 · AG and SAG mills, critical stones · module 2

2022-10-28 · question 2

Asks for the advantages of autogenous (AG) mills compared with conventional grinding.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Same six statements as the 2020 paper, question 2. The answer below is the one written for 2020-10-26.

Six points for a list, so give six advantages with a reason each. The 2014 paper asked why AG gives a better process result (variant A); the examiner's answer there, better liberation and a clean pulp, is the core of this one too.

  1. No grinding media to buy. The ore grinds itself: no balls, no rods, far lower steel consumption. Only liners wear.
  2. Cleaner pulp for the separation that follows. No iron from steel media, so the pulp is not reducing and no colloidal iron hydroxides coat the mineral surfaces. Flotation stays selective; magnetic separation is not fouled by iron slimes.
  3. Better liberation. Breakage by attrition and abrasion between ore pieces follows the grain boundaries, so the minerals are freed at a coarser size and with fewer shattered fines than by ball impact.
  4. A shorter flowsheet. An AG mill takes primary-crushed feed (hundreds of millimetres), so secondary and tertiary crushing and often the rod mill disappear. Fewer machines, fewer transfer points.
  5. High capacity in one unit. Large-diameter AG mills handle several thousand tonnes per hour; the coarse charge is also a buffer that smooths short feed variations.
  6. Lower operating cost per tonne when the ore is competent: media savings and fewer stages outweigh the somewhat higher specific energy.

And name the condition (the examiner likes it): all this requires a competent ore that supplies its own media; otherwise critical stones build up and the plant turns to pebble crushing or to SAG.

2022-10-28 · question 3

Six true-or-false statements: xanthate decomposition and pH, chain length and dissociation, depressants and bubble toughness, NaOH for depressing high-pyrite copper-gold ores, collectors and surface tension, cassiterite as a tantalum source.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Six statements, one point each. Same set in the 2022 paper (with d) reworded to "suitable for depression of").

StatementAnswerWhy
aDecomposition of xanthates increases with a reduction in the pH.TrueXanthate is the salt of a weak acid; in acid pulp it turns into xanthic acid, which decomposes to carbon disulphide and alcohol. Xanthates are stable only in alkaline pulp, which is why sulphide flotation runs at pH 8 to 12.
bLonger carbon chain xanthates dissociate faster during the flotation process.FalseChain length hardly changes the decomposition rate, and the longer chains give the less soluble, more stable metal xanthates. What the chain length changes is collecting power and selectivity.
cDepressants increase the bubble toughness.FalseDepressants make a mineral surface hydrophilic (cyanide, zinc sulphate, lime, starch); bubble toughness and froth stability are the frother's job.
dNaOH is suitable for (the depression of) high-pyrite copper-gold ores.FalsePyrite is depressed with lime: the calcium ions form calcium sulphate and hydroxide films on pyrite and the high pH stops dixanthogen forming. Sodium hydroxide raises the pH but lacks the calcium effect and costs more, so it is not the reagent of choice for pyrite depression. (Lime can depress gold too; plants weigh that, but the statement as written is false.)
eCollectors control the surface tension between mineral and frother.FalseCollectors adsorb at the mineral-water interface and make it hydrophobic. Frothers act at the air-water interface and lower its surface tension. There is no "mineral-frother" interface to control.
fCassiterite is one of the main sources of tantalum.FalseCassiterite (SnO2) is the tin mineral. Tantalum comes from tantalite-columbite (coltan) and from microlite; tin smelter slags are only a by-product source.
Why

A good true/false answer names the mechanism in half a sentence. The examiner can give the point for a bare "false", but the sentence protects you when the statement is tricky, like d), where the honest answer is "false, because pyrite depression needs the calcium of lime, not just the pH".

Written exam, 2022-10-28 · Ex. 4 · 6 p · T6 · magnetite, hematite, Slon · module 10

2022-10-28 · question 4

Asks what a Slon separator is and what its advantages are.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Same question as the 2020 paper, question 4. The answer below is the one written for 2020-10-26.

What SLon is. A vertical-ring, pulsating, high-gradient wet magnetic separator, developed in China (Ganzhou) and now standard for fine, weakly magnetic minerals. Its parts:

  • A vertical ring that carries a matrix of magnetic rods (or expanded metal) through a strong electromagnet's field (up to about 1 to 1.3 T at the poles, with very high field gradients at the rod surfaces).
  • The pulp is fed from above into the matrix inside the magnetic zone; weakly magnetic particles (hematite, limonite, ilmenite, chromite, wolframite) are trapped on the rods, non-magnetics pass through and leave as tailings at the bottom.
  • A pulsating mechanism (a diaphragm) makes the pulp in the matrix oscillate up and down, so the trapped bed is loosened continuously and the mechanically entrapped gangue is washed out while the magnetics stay held.
  • As the ring rotates out of the field, the magnetic product is flushed off with water from the top into a launder, and the clean matrix rotates back into the feed zone.

Its advantages.

  1. High recovery of fine weakly magnetic minerals (down to 10 to 20 µm) thanks to the high gradient at the rods.
  2. High concentrate grade, because the pulsation and the top-flush remove the gangue that a static matrix would hold by entrapment.
  3. The matrix does not clog: vertical ring, feed from the top, flushing in the opposite direction, pulsation; no blocking by coarse particles or slimes, which was the weakness of the older horizontal-ring WHIMS.
  4. Low operating cost and simple operation: low water and energy per tonne, few moving parts, long matrix life, continuous operation.
  5. Large capacity in one unit and a wide feed size range (about 1 mm to 10 µm).

Where it is used: hematite and limonite ores (upgrading the fines after spirals), ilmenite from beach sands, removing iron from feldspar and quartz, wolframite and chromite fines.

Why

Why the examiner likes Slon: it solved the one thing that kept fine hematite in the tailings, entrapment and clogging in high-gradient matrices. Say "pulsation plus vertical ring" and what each does; that is the essence. Module 10, SC-Iron ores.pdf and OH_IronOres.pdf, is the source; the same question appears in the 2022 paper.
