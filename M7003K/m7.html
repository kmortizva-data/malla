What the exam wants · T4 · asked in 2015, 2020, 2022

Copper, lead and zinc ores: flotation conditions

  1. Start from the mineralogy: which sulphides carry the value, how much pyrite, what gangue (quartz, clays such as kaolinite).
  2. Choose the sequence: bulk rougher then cleaning for a simple copper ore; selective or collective-then-separation for Pb-Zn; pyrite always the enemy.
  3. Give the chemistry per stage: collector (xanthate, shorter chain for selectivity), pH set with lime (pyrite depressed above pH 10-11), activation of sphalerite with CuSO4 before zinc flotation, depressants for sphalerite/pyrite in the lead circuit (ZnSO4, NaCN).
  4. Mention the physical side: grind to liberation, regrind the concentrate for cleaning, clays need dispersion and dilution.
  5. Close with what the products are and what limits grade and recovery.

What the exam wants · T7 · asked in 2014, 2015

Other metals: tungsten, nickel, tantalum, iron sulphides

  1. Name the ore minerals first (wolframite and scheelite for tungsten; pentlandite and violarite in sulphide nickel ores, laterites for oxidised nickel; tantalite and columbite for tantalum).
  2. Tie the method to a property: density for wolframite and scheelite (gravity), magnetism for wolframite (WHIMS) and pyrrhotite (LIMS), flotation for scheelite and for sulphide nickel.
  3. For nickel: sulphide ores by flotation (with pyrrhotite rejection by magnetic separation or depression), laterites by hydrometallurgy (HPAL) or smelting.
  4. For pyrrhotite against pyrite and arsenopyrite: pyrrhotite is magnetic (LIMS), and the disulphides float with high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp (pH about 10.8) while pyrrhotite stays depressed.
  5. True/false trap: cassiterite is a tin mineral, not a tantalum source.

What the exam wants · T11 · asked in 2014, 2015, 2020, 2022

Products balance and the two-product formula

  1. Draw the flowsheet with the streams named (feed, concentrates, middlings, final tail); the balance table has one row per stream.
  2. Concentrate mass from recovery: M_c = R x M_f x f / c (recovery fixes the mass when grade and recovery are given), or from grades alone with the two-product formula M_c = M_f (f - t)/(c - t).
  3. Fill the metal columns: metal = mass x grade; distribution (%) = metal in stream / metal in feed x 100. For several concentrates work stream by stream: feed minus concentrate 1 gives the middlings that feed stage 2.
  4. Concentrator difference = feed metal minus the sum of the products' metal (zero for the elements whose recovery was given, because the recovery defined the masses; non-zero for by-product elements such as silver). Concentrator loss = metal in the tail per tonne of feed (g/t or kg/t).
  5. Recovery R = c(f - t)/(f(c - t)) x 100 when masses are unknown; always check that masses and metal close to the feed.

Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 5 · 6 p · T7 · W, Ni, Ta minerals and their separation · module 7

2014-10-27 · question 5

Asks which methods separate pyrrhotite from the disulphides pyrite and arsenopyrite.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above: magnetism (2 p) and selective flotation at high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp (4 p). Why those two, in plain words.

The problem. Pyrrhotite (Fe1-xS) and the disulphides pyrite (FeS2), arsenopyrite (FeAsS), gudmundite (FeSbS) and loellingite (FeAs2) all contain iron(II), all are dense (4.6 to 6.3 g/cm³, too close for gravity) and all respond to xanthate. They must be separated because pyrrhotite dilutes pyrite concentrates, consumes reagents and oxygen, and carries little value, while arsenopyrite often carries the gold.

Method 1: magnetic separation. Monoclinic pyrrhotite is ferrimagnetic, strongly enough for low-intensity magnetic separators (wet drums) to pull it out of a ground pulp; pyrite and the other disulphides are only weakly magnetic (paramagnetic) and stay. Cheap, no reagents, used as a pre-step before flotation. Limitation: hexagonal pyrrhotite is only weakly magnetic, and fine intergrowths follow the magnetic grains.

Method 2: flotation with high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp (about pH 10.8). In alkaline pulp iron hydroxide covers the pyrrhotite surface and no iron xanthate forms: pyrrhotite is depressed. The disulphides have sulphur (or arsenic, antimony) pairs at the surface where the collector oxidises to dixanthogen, which adsorbs physically and strongly, so pyrite and arsenopyrite float even at a pH where pyrrhotite does not. A high xanthate dosage is needed to reach that dixanthogen state. Soda ash rather than lime, because calcium would depress the disulphides too.

Combining them is common: LIMS takes the bulk of the pyrrhotite, flotation cleans the rest and yields a pyrite or arsenopyrite (gold) concentrate.

Why

This is the same Bjerrum logic as the lead questions applied to iron: iron xanthates are soluble and iron hydroxide is very stable, so iron sulphides cannot float through an iron-xanthate salt; they float only when the collector oxidises to dixanthogen on their surface, which pyrrhotite cannot do at high pH. Module 7 (complex ores) and the 2014 solution are the sources.

What changed against 2014: collective flotation first (a bulk Pb-Zn concentrate), then a separation step; no silver; and this time the tail grades are given for both metals. The arithmetic is the same: recoveries fix the concentrate masses, everything else follows by difference.

2015-12-22 · question 8

Column-flotation trial results by sieve fraction (concentrate and tailings weights and lead and zinc grades): recoveries by fraction and in total for lead and zinc, and the average grades of concentrate and tailings.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

A products balance by size fraction. The trial gives the total mass rates (1.02 kg/h of concentrate, 27.8 kg/h of tailings) and, for each sieve fraction, the sample weight and the grades. The recovery of a fraction is the metal in the concentrate of that fraction divided by the metal in concentrate plus tailings of the same fraction. No feed sample is needed: feed = concentrate + tailings.

Note

Words used here. Size fraction: the material caught between two sieves (150 µm, 106 µm, ...). Sample weight: the grams of each fraction in the laboratory sieving; it only tells you the proportions of the stream, not its rate. Recovery by fraction: how much of the metal of that size ended in the concentrate.

Why

Why a size-by-size balance is asked: it shows where a flotation cell loses metal. Here the losses sit at the coarse end (locked or too heavy to lift, 0 % at 150 µm) and in the slimes (< 38 µm, 82 g/h of lead in the tailings). A plant metallurgist reads that as "grind a little finer, but not too fine, and consider a slimes stage".

Do not weight the grades by the sample weights (grams): the sample weights are only proportions. Always convert to kg/h with the total rates first; the two samples were taken from streams of very different size, 1.02 against 27.8 kg/h.

Written exam, 2020-10-26 · Ex. 5 · 6 p · T4 · Cu and Pb-Zn flowsheets, reagents and pH · module 7

2020-10-26 · question 5

Recommends flotation conditions (steps, reagents, pH) for processing coarse-grained lead-zinc ores.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

"Coarse-grained" is the hint. Coarse grains mean liberation at a coarse size, so the plant can grind coarsely (150 to 250 µm), may pre-concentrate by density, and can afford a simple sequential circuit: lead first, zinc second, pyrite to the tail.

Step 1. Pre-concentration and grinding. Coarse liberation allows dense-medium separation or jigging of the crushed ore to reject barren gangue before grinding (common in coarse Pb-Zn ores), then a coarse grind in a rod or AG mill; keep iron out of the pulp.

Step 2. Lead flotation at pH 8 to 9.5. pH with soda ash (or a little lime). Depress sphalerite and pyrite with zinc sulphate, 200 to 500 g/t, plus a small dose of sodium cyanide if the pyrite is active. Collector: ethyl or isopropyl xanthate, or a dithiophosphate, 20 to 50 g/t, for selectivity against sphalerite. Frother MIBC. Product: galena concentrate (55 to 75 % Pb after cleaning).

Step 3. Zinc flotation from the lead tail at pH 10 to 11.5. Raise the pH with lime (pyrite depression). Activate sphalerite with copper sulphate, 300 to 600 g/t, conditioning 5 to 10 minutes, then a stronger collector (isopropyl to amyl xanthate, 50 to 100 g/t). Product: sphalerite concentrate (50 to 55 % Zn after cleaning).

Step 4. Cleaning. Two or three cleaner stages per concentrate, with regrinding of the rougher concentrate only if composites remain (coarse grains often make it unnecessary), deep froth or wash water to drain entrained pyrite.

Step 5. Tail. Pyrite and quartz; if pyrite is wanted, float it at pH 5 to 6 with amyl xanthate.

Why

Compare with the 2022 Cu-Pb-Zn question: same reagents, one fewer stage (no copper-lead separation). Coarse grains reduce the work at every step: less grinding, fewer composites, less need for regrinding, and the chance to throw away gangue before the mill.

Written exam, 2022-10-28 · Ex. 1 · 6 p · T4 · Cu and Pb-Zn flowsheets, reagents and pH · module 7

2022-10-28 · question 1

Recommends flotation conditions (reagents, pH) for separating a Cu-Pb-Zn ore of chalcopyrite, sphalerite, galena, 8-15 % pyrite, quartz and kaolinite through its various stages.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Same recipe as the copper question of 2020, one more stage. With chalcopyrite, sphalerite and galena in the same ore the plant floats sequentially: copper (and often lead with it) first, zinc second, pyrite to the tail. Kaolinite and pyrite (8 to 15 %) are the same nuisances as in 2020.

Stage 0. Grinding and slimes. Grind to liberation (100 to 150 µm), ideally autogenous (no iron in the pulp). Sodium silicate as dispersant for the kaolinite; moderate pulp density.

Stage 1. Copper-lead flotation (bulk Cu-Pb) at pH 9 to 10. Lime or soda ash for the pH. Depress sphalerite and pyrite with zinc sulphate + sodium cyanide (or sodium metabisulphite where cyanide is unwanted); collector a short-chain xanthate or a dithiophosphate, dosed low for selectivity. Product: a Cu-Pb bulk concentrate; the tail goes on to zinc flotation. If the ore is coarse-grained enough, copper and lead can also be floated one after the other (copper first with a dithiophosphate, galena depressed by dichromate or sulphite, then galena).

Stage 2. Copper-lead separation of the bulk concentrate. Two classic routes: depress galena with sodium dichromate (or SO2 / sodium sulphite plus starch) and float chalcopyrite; or depress chalcopyrite with cyanide (high dosage) and float galena. Choice depends on which mineral is the minor one and on the silver deportment.

Stage 3. Zinc flotation from the bulk tail at pH 10.5 to 11.5 with lime. Activate sphalerite with copper sulphate (200 to 500 g/t), then a longer-chain xanthate (isopropyl to amyl); the lime keeps pyrite depressed. Cleaning of the zinc concentrate at higher pH, sometimes with a little cyanide against the remaining pyrite.

Stage 4. Pyrite. Stays in the tail; if there is a market or an acid plant, a pyrite flotation at low pH (about 5 to 6, with amyl xanthate) can follow.

Everywhere: regrinding before cleaning, wash water or deep froth in the cleaners, scavengers on each tail with their concentrates recycled.

Why

The order of the stages follows the Bjerrum picture of this course: copper minerals float at the highest pH and with the least collector, galena next, sphalerite only when activated, pyrite last and only when nothing depresses it. Garpenberg and Boliden's complex ores in module 7 are the worked examples.
