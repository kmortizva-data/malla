Autogenous grinding

6 questions across the papers · module 2

The pattern that solves all of them
  1. Autogenous grinding uses the ore itself as grinding media; SAG adds a few percent of steel balls.
  2. Physical advantage: more attrition and abrasion breakage, which frees mineral grains along their boundaries (better liberation).
  3. Chemical advantage: no iron from steel media, so the pulp stays non-reducing and there are no iron-hydroxide slimes that coat mineral surfaces and hurt flotation.
  4. Weakness: the mill depends on the feed. Lumps of 20-50 mm (critical stones) are too small to grind and too big to be ground; they build up in the charge and take capacity.
  5. Remedies for critical stones: screen them out and crush them (pebble crushing), change the feed size distribution (blending, pre-screening), or add a few percent of balls (turn AG into SAG).

The variants, year by year

Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 3 · 6 p · T3 · AG and SAG mills, critical stones · module 2

Variant A · 2014-10-27 · question 3

Asks for the physical and chemical reasons why fully autogenous grinding gives a better process result than conventional grinding.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above: two causes, one physical and one chemical, three points each. Here is the same answer with the reasoning behind each line, so that it can be rebuilt instead of memorised.

1. Physical: how the rock breaks. In an autogenous mill the ore grinds the ore. Big lumps tumble and rub against smaller ones, so most breakage is attrition and abrasion, not impact. Abrasion breaks rock preferentially along the weakest surfaces, which are the grain boundaries between minerals. The result is more particles that are one mineral only, i.e. better liberation at the same size, and fewer flat, shattered fragments. Steel balls in a conventional mill break by impact through the grains, which liberates less and makes more fines.

2. Chemical: what the pulp contains. Steel media wear away as iron. In a conventional mill that iron dissolves and oxidises: the pulp becomes reducing, oxygen is consumed, and colloidal iron hydroxides precipitate. Those hydroxides coat every mineral surface and make flotation less selective (they hide the surfaces the collector should find) and hurt magnetic separation. An AG mill adds no iron, so the pulp stays oxidising and clean, and the downstream separations work as the chemistry predicts.

How to write it for six points. One paragraph per cause with the mechanism and the consequence: liberation for the physical cause, surface chemistry for the chemical cause. The examiner explicitly notes that "better economics" alone is not the answer to this question.

Why

The course keeps coming back to this pair because it explains why LKAB and Boliden grind autogenously: iron ores for the magnetic separation (no iron slimes, no reducing pulp) and sulphide ores for the flotation (clean surfaces, selective liberation). Chapter 5.11 of King gives the model of the AG mill; the course slides SC-Autogenous.pdf give the plant reasons.

Variant B · 2014-12-22 · question 3

Asks which process options remove or reduce the accumulation of critical stones in autogenous grinding.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

First say what a critical stone is. In an AG mill, pieces of about 20 to 50 mm (the limits depend on the ore) are too small to act as grinding media and too big to be ground quickly by the rest of the charge. They accumulate in the mill, take up volume and draw power without producing fines. The 2015 retake asks the same question with "diminish"; the answer is the same list.

The process options, from the cheapest to the most expensive.

  1. Take them out and break them elsewhere (pebble crushing). Pebble ports in the grate let the critical size leave the mill; a screen separates the pebbles and a cone crusher breaks them to a size the mill can grind; the crushed product returns to the mill feed. This is the standard remedy in modern AG circuits.
  2. Use them as media in a second mill (pebble mill). In two-stage autogenous circuits the primary mill's critical-size product becomes the grinding media of the secondary pebble mill: the problem turns into a resource.
  3. Change the feed so fewer are made. Blend the run-of-mine feed so that enough competent large lumps arrive (the media), and avoid feeding the 20-50 mm range directly: pre-screening, or crushing the intermediate fraction before the mill.
  4. Add steel balls (semi-autogenous grinding). A few percent of large balls break the critical size by impact. It works, at the price of steel consumption and iron in the pulp, which is exactly what fully autogenous grinding wanted to avoid.
  5. Adjust the mill itself. A larger grate opening and pebble ports, higher mill speed or a different lifter design change the balance between building up and breaking the critical size. These are fine-tuning, not a cure.

In the exam: define the critical stone in one sentence, give the options with the reason each works, and rank them: pebble crushing as the main answer, SAG as the fallback, feed control as the preventive measure.

Why

Why the problem exists at all: breakage rate as a function of size in an AG mill has a minimum in the middle. Big rocks break by self-impact, fine ones by abrasion between big rocks, and the middle size has neither mechanism. King treats it in section 5.13 (breakage rates in autogenous mills); the course slides SC-Autogenous.pdf show the plant circuits with pebble crushers.

Exam Sp 1, 2015-10-26 · Ex. 2 · 6 p · T3 · AG and SAG mills, critical stones · module 2

Variant C · 2015-10-26 · question 2

Asks what semi-autogenous grinding is, what characterises it, when it is applied, and its advantages and disadvantages.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Four parts in one question: what, what characterises it, when, and pros and cons. Answer them in that order with a heading each; the examiner marks by part.

What it is. Semi-autogenous grinding (SAG) is autogenous grinding with a small charge of large steel balls, typically 2 to 12 % of the mill volume (often 4 to 8 %), added to an ore charge that otherwise grinds itself.

What characterises it.

  • Large-diameter, short mills with grates, like AG mills, running at a similar speed.
  • Breakage is a mix: the balls add impact breakage of the coarse and critical sizes to the abrasion that the ore provides.
  • Higher and more stable power draw than AG on the same ore, because the ball charge is denser than rock.
  • Usually one stage from a primary-crushed feed to a size that a ball mill or a cyclone circuit can finish; often closed with a pebble crusher.

When it is applied.

  • When the ore is not competent enough to provide its own media, or the feed is variable (soft and hard zones), so that a fully autogenous mill would starve for media or choke on critical stones.
  • When throughput matters more than the last percent of liberation or steel consumption: large-tonnage copper and gold plants.
  • As a way to skip secondary and tertiary crushing: SAG accepts a coarse primary-crusher product.

Advantages. Robust against feed changes; fewer critical-stone problems; higher throughput per mill; simpler flowsheet than crushing plus rod and ball mills; lower capital cost per tonne.

Disadvantages. Steel consumption (balls and liners) and the cost that goes with it; iron in the pulp (reducing conditions, iron-hydroxide slimes) that AG avoided, so flotation or magnetic separation can be a little less selective; more impact breakage means more fines and less grain-boundary liberation than AG; higher energy per tonne than a well-run AG mill on a competent ore.

Why

The short version the examiner wants: SAG buys stability and throughput with steel, and pays for it in the pulp chemistry and the steel bill. The AG question of the 2014 paper (variant A) gives the two reasons why AG is cleaner; SAG gives up part of both.

Variant D · 2015-12-22 · question 3

Asks in what ways the critical-stones problem of autogenous grinding can be reduced.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Same question as the 2014 retake (variant B in this topic), asked more briefly. The answer is the same list; write it in the same order.

  • Definition first: critical stones are the 20 to 50 mm pieces that are too small to grind others and too large to be ground; they build up in the AG charge and steal capacity.
  • Pebble crushing: let them out through pebble ports in the grate, screen them off, crush them in a cone crusher, return the product to the mill. The main industrial answer.
  • Pebble mill: in a two-stage circuit, use the critical size of the primary mill as the media of the secondary mill.
  • Feed management: blend the feed to keep enough competent large lumps, and do not feed the critical range directly (pre-screen or crush the intermediate fraction).
  • Semi-autogenous grinding: a few percent of large steel balls break the critical size by impact; costs steel and puts iron in the pulp.
  • Mill settings: grate opening, pebble ports, speed and lifters shift the balance; useful for tuning, not a cure.

For six points: definition (1), pebble crushing with how it works (2), two or three more options with one reason each (3).

Written exam, 2020-10-26 · Ex. 2 · 6 p · T3 · AG and SAG mills, critical stones · module 2

Variant E · 2020-10-26 · question 2

Asks for the advantages of autogenous (AG) mills compared with conventional grinding.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Six points for a list, so give six advantages with a reason each. The 2014 paper asked why AG gives a better process result (variant A); the examiner's answer there, better liberation and a clean pulp, is the core of this one too.

  1. No grinding media to buy. The ore grinds itself: no balls, no rods, far lower steel consumption. Only liners wear.
  2. Cleaner pulp for the separation that follows. No iron from steel media, so the pulp is not reducing and no colloidal iron hydroxides coat the mineral surfaces. Flotation stays selective; magnetic separation is not fouled by iron slimes.
  3. Better liberation. Breakage by attrition and abrasion between ore pieces follows the grain boundaries, so the minerals are freed at a coarser size and with fewer shattered fines than by ball impact.
  4. A shorter flowsheet. An AG mill takes primary-crushed feed (hundreds of millimetres), so secondary and tertiary crushing and often the rod mill disappear. Fewer machines, fewer transfer points.
  5. High capacity in one unit. Large-diameter AG mills handle several thousand tonnes per hour; the coarse charge is also a buffer that smooths short feed variations.
  6. Lower operating cost per tonne when the ore is competent: media savings and fewer stages outweigh the somewhat higher specific energy.

And name the condition (the examiner likes it): all this requires a competent ore that supplies its own media; otherwise critical stones build up and the plant turns to pebble crushing or to SAG.
