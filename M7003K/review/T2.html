M7003K · review · T2

Reagents and the froth

3 questions across the papers · module 5

The pattern that solves all of them
  1. Name the reagent class and its job in one sentence (collector: makes the mineral hydrophobic; frother: stabilises bubbles; modifier: changes selectivity).
  2. Say what happens with too little and too much: under-dosed frother gives a thin, fragile froth and low recovery; over-dosed frother gives a persistent froth that carries gangue and floods the cleaners.
  3. For modifiers such as cyanide give BOTH uses: depressing pyrite/sphalerite in Cu-Pb roughing and dissolving activating copper ions off sphalerite surfaces (deactivation).
  4. For froth phenomena (secondary concentration) explain the mechanism (drainage in the lamellae) and the two control levers (froth depth, wash water).
  5. Give one or two concrete examples (MIBC, pine oil, polyglycol frothers; NaCN, ZnSO4 as depressants).

Variant A · 2014-10-27 · question 2

Asks what secondary concentration in a flotation froth is and how it can be influenced and controlled.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above (3 + 3 points). In plain words:

a) What secondary concentration is. The froth is not a passive raft. Between the bubbles there are thin water films (lamellae) that drain downwards. Particles that are only weakly hydrophobic, and gangue that came up by entrainment in the water, are carried down with that drainage and fall back into the pulp. The strongly hydrophobic particles stay attached to the bubbles and move up. So the top of the froth is richer than the bottom: the froth concentrates a second time, after the collection in the pulp. That is secondary concentration.

b) How to influence and control it.

  • Froth depth. A deeper froth gives the lamellae more time to drain: higher grade, lower recovery. A shallow froth does the opposite. Cell level control sets it.
  • Wash water. Water sprayed on top of the froth (standard in flotation columns, also in some cells) replaces the entrained pulp water with clean water and washes gangue down: higher grade at the same recovery.
  • Secondary levers: frother type and dosage (a more stable froth drains less), air rate and froth removal rate (how long the froth lives before it is scraped), and the particle size (fine gangue is the most entrained).
Why

Secondary concentration is the reason a rougher concentrate can be much richer than the collection kinetics alone predict, and the reason columns with wash water make cleaner concentrates than mechanical cells. King treats the froth phase in section 9.7 (drainage through Plateau borders); the course's flotation slides have the froth thickness and wash water arguments.

Variant B · 2014-12-22 · question 2

Asks for the two different uses of cyanide as a modifier in sulphide flotation and the purpose of each.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Cyanide in sulphide flotation is a modifier with two jobs, and the exam wants both named with their purpose.

1. Depressant for iron sulphides and sphalerite in the copper or lead rougher. Small dosages of NaCN (tens of grams per tonne) stop pyrite, pyrrhotite, arsenopyrite and sphalerite from taking up xanthate. On pyrite the cyanide forms iron-cyanide complexes at the surface and removes the iron sites where dixanthogen would form; on sphalerite it complexes copper and zinc ions so that the surface cannot be activated. Purpose: selectivity, so that only the copper minerals (or galena) float in the first stage. Usually dosed together with zinc sulphate for sphalerite.

2. Deactivation, i.e. removal of activating copper ions from sphalerite (and from pyrite). In complex ores, copper ions dissolved during grinding settle on sphalerite and activate it, so it floats where it should not. Cyanide dissolves those copper ions as very stable copper-cyanide complexes and strips the activation. Purpose: reverse an unwanted activation before the zinc circuit, or keep sphalerite out of the copper concentrate.

A third use worth one line: at higher dosage cyanide depresses the copper sulphides themselves, which is used in copper-molybdenum separation (molybdenite floats while chalcopyrite is depressed) and in some Cu-Pb separations.

The cost of using it: cyanide also dissolves gold and silver (losses to solution), is toxic, needs alkaline pulp to stay as CN− and not as HCN gas, and the plant needs destruction of the residual cyanide in the tailings.

Why

Both uses come from the same chemistry: cyanide forms extremely stable complexes with copper and iron. Where those metals are the activators (copper on sphalerite) or the collector's anchor (iron on pyrite), taking them away removes the flotation. The Boliden and Garpenberg circuits in the module 7 material are the practical examples.

Variant C · 2015-12-22 · question 2

Asks for the purpose of the frother, the effects of under- and over-dosing it, and examples of common frothers.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Three parts: purpose, the two dosing errors, examples. Two points each.

Purpose of the frother. A surface-active molecule that adsorbs at the air-water interface, lowers its surface tension and slows the coalescence of bubbles. Result: many small bubbles of stable size in the pulp (more collision surface for the particles) and a froth on top that is stable long enough to be scraped off but breaks in the launder. The frother does not adsorb on the minerals; that is the collector's job.

Under-dosage. Bubbles coalesce into a few large ones: less bubble surface, fewer particle-bubble collisions, coarse particles fall off. The froth is thin and brittle, it collapses before it reaches the lip. Recovery drops, especially of the coarse and the weakly hydrophobic particles.

Over-dosage. The froth becomes too stable and voluminous: it overflows uncontrolled, carries entrained gangue and water (lower grade), does not break in launders and pumps, and the effect can last through the whole circuit (cleaners flooded, "froth in the pump sump"). Above a certain point the extra frother even reduces recovery because the bubbles become so small and stable that they stop rising properly.

Common frothers. MIBC (methyl isobutyl carbinol), the classic selective frother with a short-lived froth; pine oil and cresylic acid, the older natural frothers; polyglycols such as Dowfroth 250 (polypropylene glycol ethers) for a stronger, more persistent froth; TEB (triethoxybutane); blends of alcohols and glycols.

Why

The rule of thumb the course gives: choose the frother by the froth you need (brittle and selective for cleaners, strong for coarse roughing) and dose to the minimum that keeps the froth stable to the lip. Secondary concentration (variant A of this topic) is what a well-dosed froth does for grade.
