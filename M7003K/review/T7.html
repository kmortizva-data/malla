M7003K · review · T7

Other metals: tungsten, nickel, tantalum, iron sulphides

3 questions across the papers · module 11 · module 7

The pattern that solves all of them
  1. Name the ore minerals first (wolframite and scheelite for tungsten; pentlandite and violarite in sulphide nickel ores, laterites for oxidised nickel; tantalite and columbite for tantalum).
  2. Tie the method to a property: density for wolframite and scheelite (gravity), magnetism for wolframite (WHIMS) and pyrrhotite (LIMS), flotation for scheelite and for sulphide nickel.
  3. For nickel: sulphide ores by flotation (with pyrrhotite rejection by magnetic separation or depression), laterites by hydrometallurgy (HPAL) or smelting.
  4. For pyrrhotite against pyrite and arsenopyrite: pyrrhotite is magnetic (LIMS), and the disulphides float with high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp (pH about 10.8) while pyrrhotite stays depressed.
  5. True/false trap: cassiterite is a tin mineral, not a tantalum source.

Variant A · 2014-10-27 · question 4

Asks for the two most important tungsten minerals and how each is concentrated.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above: two minerals, three points each. The reasoning that makes it easy to remember: each mineral's concentration method follows from one physical property.

Wolframite, (Fe,Mn)WO4. Density 7.1 to 7.5 g/cm³, so it is separated from the quartz gangue by gravity (jigs, spirals, shaking tables, dense media for lump). It contains iron and manganese, which make it weakly magnetic, so wet high-intensity magnetic separation (WHIMS) cleans the gravity concentrate and separates it from cassiterite and scheelite, which are non-magnetic. Flotation is rarely needed.

Scheelite, CaWO4. Density 5.9 to 6.1 g/cm³: gravity works for the coarse grains, but scheelite is brittle and much of it ends up as fines that gravity loses. It is a calcium mineral, so it floats with fatty acids (oleate, tall oil) at pH 9 to 10.5, with sodium silicate to depress the silicate gangue; the difficulty is separating it from calcite, fluorite and apatite, which share the calcium chemistry (the Petrov process, warm pulp with a large sodium silicate dose, is the classic answer). So gravity supplemented with flotation.

Bonus points if asked "how": tungsten concentrates are sold on WO3 content (65 % WO3 and above), and the grade is reached by cleaning with WHIMS, tables and flotation of the fines; the concentrate is then converted to ammonium paratungstate by hydrometallurgy.

Variant B · 2014-10-27 · question 5

Asks which methods separate pyrrhotite from the disulphides pyrite and arsenopyrite.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The examiner's answer is in the fold above: magnetism (2 p) and selective flotation at high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp (4 p). Why those two, in plain words.

The problem. Pyrrhotite (Fe1-xS) and the disulphides pyrite (FeS2), arsenopyrite (FeAsS), gudmundite (FeSbS) and loellingite (FeAs2) all contain iron(II), all are dense (4.6 to 6.3 g/cm³, too close for gravity) and all respond to xanthate. They must be separated because pyrrhotite dilutes pyrite concentrates, consumes reagents and oxygen, and carries little value, while arsenopyrite often carries the gold.

Method 1: magnetic separation. Monoclinic pyrrhotite is ferrimagnetic, strongly enough for low-intensity magnetic separators (wet drums) to pull it out of a ground pulp; pyrite and the other disulphides are only weakly magnetic (paramagnetic) and stay. Cheap, no reagents, used as a pre-step before flotation. Limitation: hexagonal pyrrhotite is only weakly magnetic, and fine intergrowths follow the magnetic grains.

Method 2: flotation with high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp (about pH 10.8). In alkaline pulp iron hydroxide covers the pyrrhotite surface and no iron xanthate forms: pyrrhotite is depressed. The disulphides have sulphur (or arsenic, antimony) pairs at the surface where the collector oxidises to dixanthogen, which adsorbs physically and strongly, so pyrite and arsenopyrite float even at a pH where pyrrhotite does not. A high xanthate dosage is needed to reach that dixanthogen state. Soda ash rather than lime, because calcium would depress the disulphides too.

Combining them is common: LIMS takes the bulk of the pyrrhotite, flotation cleans the rest and yields a pyrite or arsenopyrite (gold) concentrate.

Why

This is the same Bjerrum logic as the lead questions applied to iron: iron xanthates are soluble and iron hydroxide is very stable, so iron sulphides cannot float through an iron-xanthate salt; they float only when the collector oxidises to dixanthogen on their surface, which pyrrhotite cannot do at high pH. Module 7 (complex ores) and the 2014 solution are the sources.

Variant C · 2015-12-22 · question 5

Asks for the major process options for treating nickel ores.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Nickel comes in two ore families, and each has its own route. Say the mineralogy, then the process, then the new options.

1. Sulphide ores (pentlandite (Fe,Ni)9S8 with pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite, sometimes pyrite; 0.5 to 3 % Ni). Sudbury, Norilsk, Kambalda, the Scandinavian deposits.

  • Autogenous or SAG grinding to liberation (the sulphides are intergrown, often finely).
  • Flotation of the nickel sulphides with xanthate at slightly alkaline pH, with pyrrhotite rejection by low-intensity magnetic separation (magnetic pyrrhotite) or by depression (high pH, soda, oxidation), because pyrrhotite carries little nickel and makes sulphur dioxide in the smelter.
  • Copper-nickel separation of the bulk concentrate when chalcopyrite is present (lime, depression of the nickel minerals at high pH, or vice versa).
  • Concentrate to smelting (flash smelting to a nickel-copper matte) and refining (matte separation, leaching or electrorefining, carbonyl process).

2. Laterite ores (oxidised nickel in the weathered layer: limonitic goethite with 1 to 1.5 % Ni, saprolitic silicates with 1.5 to 3 % Ni). Indonesia, New Caledonia, Cuba, the Philippines. No mineral concentration is possible (the nickel sits in the lattice of the oxides and silicates), only upgrading by screening and washing; the process is metallurgical:

  • Saprolite: drying, calcining and smelting to ferronickel or nickel matte (electric furnace).
  • Limonite: high-pressure acid leaching (HPAL) with sulphuric acid at 250 °C, then precipitation of a mixed sulphide or hydroxide and refining; the older Caron process (reduction roast and ammonia leach) for mixed ores.

3. Newer options. Heap bioleaching of low-grade black-shale sulphide ore at Talvivaara (Finland): crushed ore stacked, bacteria oxidise the sulphides, the solution goes to sulphide precipitation; chloride and atmospheric leaching of laterites; nickel recovery from pyrrhotite by roasting.

Why

The exam wants the two-family structure and the reason: sulphide nickel can be concentrated by flotation because pentlandite is a floatable mineral, laterite nickel cannot because there is no nickel mineral to liberate, so the chemistry has to be done on the whole ore. Module 11 (Ni-Co.pdf, Ni-Co_NewProcess.pdf, OH_Talvivaara.pdf, the Nickel Mountain presentation) is the source.
