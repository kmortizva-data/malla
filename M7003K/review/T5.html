M7003K · review · T5

Gold

4 questions across the papers · module 12

The pattern that solves all of them
  1. Gold sorts into free (native) gold, gold locked in sulphides (pyrite, arsenopyrite), and refractory gold (very fine, in solid solution or with carbon).
  2. Free coarse gold: gravity (jigs, spirals, tables, Knelson concentrators) early in the circuit, often from the grinding circuit.
  3. Gold in sulphides: flotation of the sulphides (xanthate, slightly alkaline or neutral pH; gold surfaces float easily when clean), then treatment of the concentrate.
  4. Fine gold: cyanide leaching (CIL/CIP with activated carbon), heap leaching for low grades; refractory ores need roasting, pressure oxidation or bio-oxidation first.
  5. Björkdal is the course's example: mostly native gold grains plus some gold in pyrite, so the flowsheet combines gravity concentration with flotation of the sulphides.

Variant A · 2014-12-22 · question 6

Asks under what conditions gold minerals can be floated.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Gold floats well, on three conditions: the right size, a clean surface, and the right chemistry.

1. Size. Native gold is heavy and malleable; grains coarser than about 100 to 150 µm are too heavy for a bubble to lift and are flattened rather than broken in the mill. Those go to gravity. Flotation handles gold from roughly 10 to 100 µm, and gold-bearing sulphide particles of normal flotation size.

2. Surface. Gold floats when its surface is clean and unoxidised: no iron-hydroxide slimes (autogenous grinding, no reducing pulp), no coatings from grinding media, freshly ground. Tarnished or coated gold floats poorly; a short conditioning with a little copper sulphate (activation) or a sulphidiser can help.

3. Chemistry.

  • Collectors: xanthates (amyl for strength), dithiophosphates and especially the mercaptobenzothiazole and thionocarbamate types, which are selective gold collectors; moderate dosages.
  • pH: neutral to mildly alkaline, about 7 to 9, with soda ash rather than lime. High lime concentrations depress native gold (calcium films), which is why gold plants avoid the pH 11 to 12 used for copper-pyrite ores.
  • Frother: MIBC or a glycol; a stable froth helps the flaky gold particles stay attached.
  • Pyrite: when the gold sits in or on pyrite, float the pyrite too (no depression: neutral pH, enough collector) and treat the concentrate afterwards.

4. Circuit. Gold flotation is usually a bulk sulphide-gold rougher with a scavenger, gravity in the grinding circuit for the coarse gold, and a concentrate that goes to smelting or to cyanide leaching.

Why

The trap in this question is lime: what is right for a copper ore (pH 11.5) is wrong for free gold. Answer with the three conditions and the reagents that meet them; the Björkdal question (variant A) shows the plant that does exactly this.

Variant B · 2015-10-26 · question 4

Asks how the gold mineralogy at Björkdal (mostly native gold grains, some gold with pyrite) shaped the design of the flowsheet.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Start from the two gold forms and let each dictate a process step.

1. Most of the gold is native, in coarse grains: gravity, early and inside the grinding circuit. Free gold grains are heavy (19 g/cm³ against 2.7 for quartz) and malleable, so they do not break: they liberate early and would otherwise circulate in the mill and flatten. Björkdal therefore recovers gravity gold from the grinding circuit (from the cyclone underflow or the mill discharge) with jigs, spirals and shaking tables or centrifugal concentrators, producing a high-grade gravity concentrate that is smelted directly. This step takes a large share of the gold before any reagent is used, cheaply and without cyanide.

2. Some gold is with pyrite: flotation of the sulphides. The gold that gravity misses (finer grains, gold locked in or attached to pyrite) is recovered by flotation of the sulphides with a xanthate or dithiophosphate at near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH, without lime excess (lime would depress gold). The gold-bearing pyrite concentrate is sold to a smelter or treated further.

3. Consequences for the flowsheet layout.

  • Grinding is closed with the gravity unit so that free gold is pulled out as soon as it is liberated, instead of over-grinding it.
  • The grind can stay relatively coarse because the flotation only needs to liberate pyrite grains, not fine gold.
  • Two products (gravity gold, flotation concentrate) instead of one, and no cyanide plant at the mine: lower cost and simpler permitting.
  • Tailings carry little gold because the two gold forms are each caught by the method suited to them.
Why

The general rule the examiner wants: match the recovery method to the gold form. Free coarse gold → gravity; gold in sulphides → flotation; fine and refractory gold → leaching after oxidation. Björkdal is the course's example (App_Björkdal_A3.pdf and SC-Precious metal.pdf in module 12).

Variant C · 2015-12-22 · question 4

Asks which enrichment methods win gold from ores where gold is the main value.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Gold-only ores: list the methods by the gold form they suit, and say when each is used.

  1. Gravity concentration. For free, coarse gold (above about 50 to 100 µm): sluices and jigs for placers, spirals, shaking tables and centrifugal concentrators (Knelson, Falcon) in the grinding circuit of hard-rock mines. Cheap, no reagents, gives a smeltable concentrate; recovers only the liberated coarse gold.
  2. Flotation. For fine free gold (10 to 100 µm) and for gold carried by sulphides (pyrite, arsenopyrite): xanthate or dithiophosphate collectors, near-neutral pH without lime excess. Produces a sulphide-gold concentrate that is sold, smelted, or oxidised and leached.
  3. Cyanide leaching. For fine gold that gravity and flotation miss, and for oxide ores: cyanidation in agitated tanks with carbon-in-pulp or carbon-in-leach (CIP/CIL) to adsorb the dissolved gold, or heap leaching of crushed low-grade ore on lined pads; gold recovered from carbon by elution and electrowinning, or by zinc cementation (Merrill-Crowe) from clear solutions.
  4. Pre-treatment of refractory ores before leaching, when the gold is locked in sulphides or tied to carbon: roasting, pressure oxidation (autoclave), bacterial oxidation (BIOX) of the sulphide concentrate; carbon-in-leach against preg-robbing carbon.
  5. Amalgamation, historical and artisanal (mercury), and smelting of high-grade gravity concentrates.

The usual plant combines them: gravity in the mill circuit, then either flotation of the sulphides or whole-ore cyanidation, depending on the mineralogy and the grade.

Why

The examiner grades the pairing, not the list: coarse free gold → gravity, fine free gold → leaching, gold in sulphides → flotation (and oxidation before leaching if refractory), low-grade oxide ore → heap leach. Module 12 (GoldMineralogy.pdf, SC-Precious metal.pdf, the Emperor and Kanieri gold appendices) is the source.

Variant D · 2022-10-28 · question 5

Asks for the process options to recover gold from different kinds of deposit.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

"Different deposits" means: answer by deposit type, one process option each. Same knowledge as the 2015 retake (variant C), organised the other way round.

Deposit / gold typeProcess optionWhy
Placer and alluvial deposits (free coarse gold in gravel)Gravity only: sluices, jigs, spirals, tables, centrifugal concentratorsGold already liberated and heavy; no grinding, no reagents
Free-milling hard-rock ores (native gold in quartz veins)Gravity in the grinding circuit for the coarse gold, then cyanide leaching (CIP/CIL) of the restCoarse gold would over-grind and dissolve slowly; fine gold leaches fast
Gold associated with sulphides (pyrite, arsenopyrite), non-refractoryFlotation of the sulphides to a concentrate, then smelting or fine grinding and cyanidation of the concentrateConcentrates the gold into a few percent of the mass before the expensive step
Refractory ores (gold locked in pyrite or arsenopyrite, sub-microscopic)Flotation, then oxidation of the concentrate (roasting, pressure oxidation, bio-oxidation), then cyanidationCyanide cannot reach gold inside a sulphide lattice until it is broken open
Carbonaceous ores (preg-robbing carbon)Carbon-in-leach (CIL), or roasting to destroy the organic carbon; flotation of the carbon in some casesActivated carbon added to the leach outcompetes the ore's own carbon for the dissolved gold
Low-grade oxide oresHeap leaching of crushed ore with dilute cyanide, gold recovered on carbonGrade too low to pay for grinding and agitation
Copper-gold porphyries (gold as a by-product)Flotation with the copper minerals, gold reports to the copper concentrate and is paid by the smelter; gravity for coarse gold in the mill circuitThe gold rides with the copper sulphides
Silver-rich or telluride oresFlotation plus intensive cyanidation, or roasting for telluridesSilver and tellurides leach slowly and need stronger conditions

Say the principle: the gold form (free or locked, coarse or fine, with or without sulphides and carbon) chooses the process; the grade chooses between agitated leaching, heap leaching and flotation-first.
