Flotation chemistry and Bjerrum diagrams

6 questions across the papers · module 5

The pattern that solves all of them
  1. A Bjerrum diagram plots pMe (= -log of the free metal-ion activity) against pH. Each straight line is the solubility limit of one solid: a metal-collector salt, a metal hydroxide, a metal-chromate, a metal-thiosulphate.
  2. Flotation with a collector is possible where the metal-collector line lies BELOW the hydroxide line: the collector salt is the more insoluble solid, so the collector wins the metal ions on the surface. Where the hydroxide line is lower, hydroxide wins and the mineral is depressed.
  3. The crossing of the two lines is the critical pH. Above it, no flotation with that collector.
  4. To compute a critical pH from data: write K_s = {Me}{X}^n, insert the collector activity, solve for {Me}, take pMe = -log{Me}, then read the pH where the hydroxide line reaches that pMe (or solve the hydroxide K_s the same way).
  5. Longer carbon chain = more insoluble metal-collector salt = line further down = flotation possible at higher pH; a depressant works when its line lies below the collector line.

The variants, year by year

Exam Sp 1, 2014-10-27 · Ex. 1 · 6 p · T1 · Bjerrum diagrams, critical pH, collectors · module 5

Variant A · 2014-10-27 · question 1

Reads the Bjerrum diagram for lead: where n-hexyl xanthate can float galena, which xanthate chain still lets chromate depress it, whether thiosulphate depresses on its own, and the critical pH for a dithiophosphate collector from its solubility product.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

The examiner's solution to this question exists (2014 paper); it is on the PC copy.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

How to read a Bjerrum diagram, once and for all. The vertical axis is pPb = −log of the free Pb2+ activity (up = more lead in solution, down = less). Each straight line is the solubility limit of one solid: below the line that solid cannot exist, above it the metal ions precipitate it. Where a metal-collector line lies below the hydroxide line, the collector salt is the more insoluble solid, so it forms on the mineral surface and the mineral floats. The two lines cross at the critical pH; above it hydroxide wins and the mineral is depressed. The examiner's answer is in the fold above; here is the reasoning behind each part.

a) Where n-hexyl xanthate floats galena. The hexyl xanthate line stays below the lead hydroxide line up to their crossing at pH 12.2: flotation is possible at any pH below 12.2. That is nearly the whole useful range, which is why long-chain xanthates are strong but unselective collectors.

b) The longest chain that still lets chromate depress galena. Chromate (0.001 M) depresses by forming lead chromate; it can only win if its line lies below the xanthate line, i.e. lead chromate is less soluble than the lead xanthate. In the diagram the amyl (5 carbons) xanthate line is above the chromate line, the hexyl line is below it. So amyl xanthate (five carbons) is the longest chain that chromate can still beat.

c) Thiosulphate on its own. The 0.005 M thiosulphate line lies above every xanthate line: lead thiosulphate is more soluble than any lead xanthate, so thiosulphate cannot displace the collector. No, on its own it does not depress galena (its real effect is indirect: it consumes oxidants and reacts with dissolved copper).

d) Critical pH for a new collector from its solubility product. Write the solubility product of the collector salt Pb(p-CDTP)2:

K_s = {Pb2+}{p-CDTP−}^2 = 7.7 × 10^−18 M^3, with {p-CDTP−} = 3.0 × 10^−5 M:

{Pb2+} = 7.7 × 10^−18 / (3.0 × 10^−5)^2 = 7.7 × 10^−18 / 9.0 × 10^−10 = 8.56 × 10^−9 M → pPb = 8.07.

That is a horizontal line at pPb = 8.07 in the diagram. Where it meets the lead hydroxide line is the critical pH: read off pH ≈ 10.6. Below 10.6 the dithiophosphate wins the surface (flotation); above, hydroxide does.

Why

The whole topic in one sentence: the mineral floats when the collector salt is the most insoluble thing that its metal ions can make at that pH. The diagram is only a plot of solubility products; if the exam gives you the numbers instead of the picture, the same calculation as in d) gives the answer (see variant C, the 2015 exam).

Note

Marking in 2014: 1 + 1 + 1 + 3 points. The calculation in d) carries half the marks; write the K_s expression, the {Pb2+} value and the pPb explicitly before reading the pH.

Variant B · 2014-12-22 · question 1

Reads the Bjerrum diagram for copper, zinc and manganese with the collector DIDA: the pH window for zinc flotation, how copper-sulphate activation widens it, and the order of the metal-collector compounds by solubility, with reasons.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The diagram this time has three metals and one collector, DIDA, at 0.00001 M. For each metal there are two lines: the metal-DIDA salt and the metal hydroxide. The circles mark where they cross, i.e. the critical pH of each metal. Reading them from the figure: manganese about pH 10.5, zinc about pH 10.2, copper about pH 11.

a) Zinc minerals with DIDA (1 p). The Zn-DIDA line lies below the Zn(OH)2 line up to their crossing: flotation is possible at pH below about 10.2. Above that, zinc hydroxide takes the surface.

b) Sphalerite activated with copper sulphate (1 p). Copper ions replace zinc on the sphalerite surface, so the surface now behaves as a copper mineral and the copper lines apply. The Cu-DIDA line stays below the Cu(OH)2 line up to about pH 11: activation extends the flotation window by nearly one pH unit, and the copper-DIDA salt is far less soluble, so the surface is more strongly hydrophobic at every pH.

c) Order of solubility, with reasons (4 p). All three lines are drawn for the same collector concentration, so at any pH the line height gives the metal-ion activity in equilibrium with the solid salt: the lower the line (higher pMe), the less soluble the salt. In the diagram the Cu-DIDA line is the lowest, Zn-DIDA in the middle, Mn-DIDA the highest. Increasing solubility:

Cu-DIDA < Zn-DIDA < Mn-DIDA

Why: the solubility product K_s = {Me2+}{DIDA−}^2 is smallest for copper. Copper(II) and, less so, zinc are soft, polarisable ions that form partly covalent bonds with the sulphur of a thiol-type collector; manganese(II) is a hard ion that only forms ionic, weakly bound and therefore soluble salts. The same order (Cu < Zn < Mn, and generally Cu < Pb < Zn < Fe < Mn) holds for xanthates and explains the classic selectivity of sulphide flotation: copper minerals float first and at the highest pH, manganese-bearing minerals hardly at all.

Why

Two things the examiner is checking: that you read "below the line = floats" correctly, and that you connect the picture to the chemistry (K_s and the softness of the metal ion). The sentence "soluble complexes may form at higher DIDA concentrations" under the figure is a hint that the picture is only valid at the low collector concentration given.

Exam Sp 1, 2015-10-26 · Ex. 1 · 6 p · T1 · Bjerrum diagrams, critical pH, collectors · module 5

Variant C · 2015-10-26 · question 1

Computes the critical pH for floating a lead mineral with isopropyl xanthate from the solubility products of lead isopropyl xanthate and lead hydroxide and the collector concentration.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

No diagram this time: the exam gives the two solubility products and asks for the crossing point by calculation. Same idea as part d) of the 2014 question, done twice.

Step 1. The lead-ion activity that the collector salt allows. Lead isopropyl xanthate: K_s = {Pb2+}{iPX−}^2 = 10^−17.8. With {iPX−} = 10^−5 M:

{Pb2+} = 10^−17.8 / (10^−5)^2 = 10^−17.8 / 10^−10 = 10^−7.8 M → pPb = 7.8

Below this lead-ion activity the xanthate salt does not precipitate; above it, it does.

Step 2. The pH at which lead hydroxide gives the same lead-ion activity. Lead hydroxide: K_s = {Pb2+}{OH−}^2 = 10^−14.37. Insert {Pb2+} = 10^−7.8:

{OH−}^2 = 10^−14.37 / 10^−7.8 = 10^−6.57 → {OH−} = 10^−3.285 → pOH = 3.29 → pH = 14 − 3.29 = 10.7

Step 3. Say what it means. At pH 10.7 both solids are in equilibrium with the same lead-ion activity: that is the critical pH. Below 10.7 the xanthate line is the lower one, lead xanthate forms on the galena surface and the mineral floats. Above 10.7 lead hydroxide is the more insoluble solid, it takes the surface, and galena is depressed.

QuantityValue
{iPX−}10^−5 M
{Pb2+} fixed by the xanthate10^−7.8 M
{OH−} at the crossing10^−3.29 M
Critical pH10.7
Watch out

Watch the exponent arithmetic: (10^−5)^2 is 10^−10, not 10^−25, and pH = 14 − pOH. A slip here moves the answer by whole pH units, and the examiner can see it because the intermediate steps must be shown.

Why

Isopropyl xanthate (3 carbons) has a smaller critical pH than the hexyl xanthate of the 2014 question (12.2): the shorter the chain, the more soluble the lead salt, the earlier hydroxide wins. That is exactly why short-chain xanthates are the selective ones: you can depress with pH alone.

Variant D · 2015-12-22 · question 1

Reads the Bjerrum diagram for iron: which pH and other conditions keep carboxylic-acid collectors (used to float apatite off magnetite) from adsorbing on magnetite surfaces.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

The situation. LKAB-type ore: magnetite with apatite as the phosphorus carrier. The apatite is floated off with a fatty-acid collector (carboxylate: oleate, palmitate). The danger is that the same collector precipitates as an iron carboxylate on the magnetite surface, which then floats with the apatite and dirties the phosphate concentrate while losing iron. The Bjerrum diagram for iron shows where the iron-carboxylate salts and the iron hydroxides are stable.

Reading the diagram. The hydroxide lines (Fe(OH)3 on the left axis, pFe3+; Fe(OH)2 on the right axis, pFe2+) slope down steeply; the carboxylate lines (a palmitic acid, b 0.2 mM palmeate, c oleic acid, d 0.2 mM oleate) cross them at moderate pH. Silicate (e) and phosphate (f) lines lie below the carboxylate lines over most of the range: the iron silicate and iron phosphate surfaces are more stable than iron oleate.

Conditions that keep the collector off the magnetite.

  1. High pH, about 9.5 to 10.5. Above the crossing points the iron hydroxides are the least soluble solids: iron ions at the surface stay as Fe(OH)3 / Fe(OH)2 and no iron-oleate can form. Apatite still floats because calcium oleate is stable there.
  2. Keep dissolved iron low. No reducing pulp, no steel wear: autogenous grinding (which LKAB uses) and no acid conditions. Less Fe2+ and Fe3+ in solution means the carboxylate lines are never reached.
  3. A depressant for the iron oxides: sodium silicate or phosphate. Their lines lie below the oleate line, so silicate and phosphate ions win the magnetite surface (lines e and f in the diagram), which then stays hydrophilic. Sodium silicate also disperses slimes.
  4. Minimum collector dosage and a collector that is selective. The 0.2 mM lines (b, d) already cross the hydroxide lines; a higher dosage moves the carboxylate lines up and widens the range where iron carboxylate is possible. Use tall-oil fatty acids or their emulsions at the lowest dose that floats the apatite, with a conditioning that lets calcium sites on apatite adsorb first.
  5. Temperature and water quality. Warm pulp helps the oleate stay dissolved and selective; hard water (Ca2+, Mg2+) precipitates the collector and should be avoided.
Why

The same logic as the lead questions, turned around: there the collector was wanted on the surface, so the question was the highest pH where the collector salt still wins. Here the collector is unwanted on magnetite, so the answer is the pH range where the hydroxide wins and a depressant whose iron salt is even more stable. Flotation chemistry is one diagram read in two directions.

Written exam, 2020-10-26 · Ex. 3 · 6 p · T1 · Bjerrum diagrams, critical pH, collectors · module 5

Variant E · 2020-10-26 · question 3

Six true-or-false statements: xanthate decomposition and pH, chain length and dissociation, depressants and bubble toughness, NaOH for high-pyrite copper-gold ores, collectors and surface tension, cassiterite as a tantalum source.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Six statements, one point each. Same set in the 2022 paper (with d) reworded to "suitable for depression of").

StatementAnswerWhy
aDecomposition of xanthates increases with a reduction in the pH.TrueXanthate is the salt of a weak acid; in acid pulp it turns into xanthic acid, which decomposes to carbon disulphide and alcohol. Xanthates are stable only in alkaline pulp, which is why sulphide flotation runs at pH 8 to 12.
bLonger carbon chain xanthates dissociate faster during the flotation process.FalseChain length hardly changes the decomposition rate, and the longer chains give the less soluble, more stable metal xanthates. What the chain length changes is collecting power and selectivity.
cDepressants increase the bubble toughness.FalseDepressants make a mineral surface hydrophilic (cyanide, zinc sulphate, lime, starch); bubble toughness and froth stability are the frother's job.
dNaOH is suitable for (the depression of) high-pyrite copper-gold ores.FalsePyrite is depressed with lime: the calcium ions form calcium sulphate and hydroxide films on pyrite and the high pH stops dixanthogen forming. Sodium hydroxide raises the pH but lacks the calcium effect and costs more, so it is not the reagent of choice for pyrite depression. (Lime can depress gold too; plants weigh that, but the statement as written is false.)
eCollectors control the surface tension between mineral and frother.FalseCollectors adsorb at the mineral-water interface and make it hydrophobic. Frothers act at the air-water interface and lower its surface tension. There is no "mineral-frother" interface to control.
fCassiterite is one of the main sources of tantalum.FalseCassiterite (SnO2) is the tin mineral. Tantalum comes from tantalite-columbite (coltan) and from microlite; tin smelter slags are only a by-product source.
Why

A good true/false answer names the mechanism in half a sentence. The examiner can give the point for a bare "false", but the sentence protects you when the statement is tricky, like d), where the honest answer is "false, because pyrite depression needs the calcium of lime, not just the pH".
