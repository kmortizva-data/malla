Copper ores: the chalcopyrite surface, the iron sulphides, and how a copper plant is run

22 min read · module 6

Why this module exists

Copper was the first metal people used and it is still the one the flotation industry is built around: more than 60 % of the world's copper comes from porphyry ores at less than 1 % Cu, and every one of those tonnes goes through a flotation plant. Sweden's own copper is Aitik, a strongly transformed porphyry, and its plant is the working example of this module's problem: the copper minerals are easy to float, the difficulty is everything that floats with them, above all the iron sulphides that outnumber the copper minerals in most ores.

The module builds on the flotation lesson and adds three things: the copper minerals and the ores they come in; the chemistry of a chalcopyrite surface and of a pyrite surface, which is what a copper plant actually manages; and the reagent schemes, circuits and alternatives (leaching, sulphidisation) that follow from that chemistry. The exam's copper questions (topic T4) and its pyrrhotite-against-pyrite question (topic T7) are answered here.

The ideas

1. The copper minerals, and which of them float

More than 170 copper minerals are known; ten to fifteen matter. The compendium sorts them by how they can be won. The primary minerals are sulphides in which copper is Cu(I), and they float with the ordinary thio-collectors:

MineralFormula% CuNote
ChalcopyriteCuFeS234.6The copper mineral of nearly every ore
BorniteCu5FeS463.3With chalcopyrite in porphyries and shales
ChalcociteCu2S79.9Secondary enrichment zones
CovelliteCuS66.5Copper as Cu(II): needs other reagents
Enargite, tennantite, tetrahedriteCu-As and Cu-Sb sulphosalts38 to 48Arsenic contaminants; tetrahedrite carries silver

The secondary minerals are what oxidation makes of them: native copper (won by wet gravity), the oxides cuprite (Cu(I), xanthate may work) and tenorite, the carbonates malachite and azurite, the sulphates chalcanthite, antlerite and brochantite, the silicates chrysocolla and dioptase. In all of these but cuprite the copper is Cu(II), and Cu(II) does not form the insoluble copper xanthate that flotation depends on. That is why oxidic copper is mostly leached with dilute sulphuric acid, and why the leaching route grew as smelters were forced to turn their sulphur dioxide into cheap acid.

In plain words: Cu(I) floats, Cu(II) leaches, and the surface of a sulphide is drifting from the first to the second the whole time it sits in aerated pulp.

2. The company copper keeps: molybdenite, gold, and the iron sulphides

Three kinds of companion decide the flowsheet. Molybdenite, MoS2, occurs in porphyries at grades worth a separate concentrate; it is a layered mineral with non-polar cleavage faces, so it floats with only a frother or a drop of diesel. Gold and silver sit partly in their own minerals but more often as micron-size inclusions or rims on pyrite and arsenopyrite, so their recovery follows the fate of the iron sulphides, and since a copper concentrate dirtied with gold-bearing pyrite seldom pays, pressure or bacterial oxidation followed by cyanide leaching is the usual alternative. And the iron sulphides are in every copper ore, from 10 % to 90 % of the sulphides:

MineralFormulaStructureWhy it matters
PyriteFeS2cubic, density 5.0The disulphide that floats with xanthate at high pH
MarcasiteFeS2orthorhombic, 4.8Same chemistry as pyrite
Pyrrhotite, monoclinicFe7S8ferrimagneticRemoved by low-intensity magnetic drums
Pyrrhotite, hexagonalFe11S12antiferromagneticOxidises easily; sulphur fires in mines
Arsenopyrite, gudmunditeFeAsS, FeSbSdisulphide-like anion pairsFloat like pyrite; carry gold and arsenic

Pyrite, marcasite, arsenopyrite and gudmundite are all built of Fe(II) paired with an anion pair (S22−, AsS2−, SbS2−): same cation, similar anions, and therefore very hard to separate from each other by flotation. Pyrrhotite is the odd one out, and the simplest way to take it out is that its monoclinic form is strongly magnetic. Iron sulphides go to the tailings today; pyrite concentrates for sulphuric acid, roasted to cinders that served as an iron source, are no longer economic.

3. Three ore types, and what their size and grade mean

The compendium follows Evans: most ores formed by heat-driven convection in the crust, and copper ores fall in three fields of a size-against-grade plot. Porphyry ores hold the largest reserves: an intrusion alters its host into a porphyry with copper minerals as droplets and veinlets in a sericitic, micaceous, clayey gangue; chalcopyrite, chalcocite, a little covellite and bornite; grades generally below 1 % Cu, but the ore bodies are enormous and cheap to mine and mill. Gold-rich porphyries formed from iron-deficient solutions near island arcs (OK Tedi), molybdenum-rich ones from iron-rich solutions on continental plates (the Chilean giants). Massive sulphide ores, formed by submarine volcanism, are small and rich: complex Kuroko-type ores (chalcopyrite, pyrite, pyrrhotite, galena, sphalerite, with gold and silver: Bergslagen, the Skellefte field), copper-zinc ores (Besshi and the "primitive" ores, greywacke and shale gangue: Folldal), and Cyprus-type ores at spreading zones (pyrite, pyrrhotite and chalcopyrite in basalts and greenstones: Cyprus, Newfoundland, Løkken). Their feed zones are the stockwork ores with high chalcopyrite and iron sulphide and almost no zinc or lead. Stratiform ores formed in shallow seas without volcanism: the European copper shale from England to Poland (bornite, chalcocite, chalcopyrite in carbonate, shale and clay, greyish black from organic matter; Poland's 1.5 % Cu at 600 to 1500 m makes it Europe's largest producer), and the copper sandstones of the Zambian and Congolese Copper Belt at 1.5 to 3 % Cu with cobalt, nickel and uranium.

4. The chalcopyrite surface: four states of oxidation

There is no thermodynamically stable chalcopyrite surface in aerated water, and aerated water is what flotation is. Ground chalcopyrite keeps turning into copper minerals of higher oxidation state, and the collector has to catch the copper atoms while they are still Cu(I). The compendium's Pourbaix diagram for a rougher pulp (chalcopyrite at 10−4 M, carbon dioxide at 3.2⋅10−4 bar) gives four practical states:

  1. Insufficient oxidation. At very low potential the mineral is stable and nothing bonds chemically to it; only physical adsorption is possible. It does not float.
  2. Self-floating. Very gently oxidised in a pulp of low ionic strength, which in practice means an autogenous or pebble-grinding pulp with almost no iron from media, chalcopyrite floats without collector: a metastable, non-polar sulphur layer is thought to form on it.
  3. Sufficient. A little more oxidation, and islets of copper xanthate form on the surface and become the contact points for bubbles. This is the state a plant aims for.
  4. Over-oxidised. The surface enters the region where Cu(I) sulphides are no longer stable; any copper xanthate that formed co-exists with a growing hydrophilic hide of copper oxides and hydroxides, reagent consumption rises to fight it, and once the surface is Cu(II) hydroxide or a malachite-like compound, xanthate flotation stops working.

The mechanism the compendium proposes for xanthate on a Cu(I) sulphide surface: the xanthate ion adsorbs, gives up an electron, and as the surface oxidises it rearranges into a surface complex (≡Cu−OH+X−↔≡Cu−X+OH−), after which islets of stoichiometric copper xanthate precipitate and make the surface hydrophobic, while oxidised copper species grow between them. In the end the copper xanthate itself is oxidised to a Cu(II) compound and dixanthogen, 2CuX+O2+2H2O→2Cu(OH)2+X2. Read on the predominance diagram, three old plant truths become visible: gentle oxidation is needed because the copper-xanthate field sits on top of the oxidation products of chalcopyrite, not on chalcopyrite itself; the upper boundary of that field is a competitive precipitation, hydroxide replacing xanthate while Cu(I) oxidises to Cu(II) and xanthate to dixanthogen; and copper floats independently of pH, because the copper-xanthate field has no alkaline limit in the range of interest, which is why the critical-pH curve of chalcopyrite lies at about pH 11 where pyrite's lies at 6 and galena's at 9.

5. The pyrite surface, and why lime and aeration are the copper plant's depressants

Pyrite forms under reducing conditions and, when it oxidises, passes through Fe(II) compounds before ferric hydroxide forms. Two kinetic facts widen its floatable region: the precipitation of ferric hydroxide is slow, so there is often not enough of it to make the surface hydrophilic within the one or two hours of flotation, and the oxidation of the disulphide group is slow too; pyrite, like chalcopyrite, is even self-floating in weakly oxidising pulps of low ionic strength. Pyrrhotite is the opposite, oxidising readily in water and even in air, to the point of self-igniting in mine stopes as a sulphur fire.

From the diagram the compendium draws the depressant strategy: deliberately oxidise the iron sulphide surfaces until they are covered by hydrophilic iron-oxygen compounds. The same cloaking happens unintentionally when the ore is ground with steel media: iron chips oxidise, pull the potential of the grinding and conditioning stages down, and precipitate as colloidal ferric hydroxide slime that smears every surface. The arithmetic is worth remembering: media wear of 110 to 120 g per kWh at 10 to 15 kWh per tonne puts 1 to 2 kg of iron per tonne of ore into the pulp, the largest "reagent addition" in the plant, and the reason steel grinding can give worse selectivity and recovery than autogenous or pebble grinding.

How, then, does pyrite float at all? Iron xanthates exist, but the stable one is the Fe(III) salt and it only survives below pH 5; between pH 5 and 7 basic iron xanthates may form; above pH 7 iron sulphides should be unfloatable with xanthate. Yet pyrite concentrates are produced at pH 10. The explanation is a second binding site, the disulphide group, and the collector that binds to it is the xanthate dimer, dixanthogen, possibly catalysed by the pyrite surface itself. The five facts that point to it, and that the exam's pyrrhotite question rests on:

  1. Fe(III) xanthate is not stable at alkaline pH, so the ordinary metal-xanthate route is closed.
  2. The xanthate needed to float a disulphide is about an order of magnitude more than in ordinary xanthate flotation.
  3. Collectors that do not dimerise easily, dithiophosphate above all, do not float pyrite at alkaline pH.
  4. At alkaline pH only the disulphides float, never pyrrhotite, which has no disulphide group.
  5. High dosages of alkaline earths stop it: calcium adsorbs on the disulphide site and blocks the adsorption site for dixanthogen.

Arsenopyrite and gudmundite float the same way at alkaline pH. The critical pH for xanthate flotation of Fe(III) minerals is about 6.5; the iron Bjerrum diagram also shows that iron minerals could, in theory, be floated with carboxylic acids, which is the danger the apatite question of the 2015 retake is about.

6. Running a copper plant: reagent schemes by ore type

Simple copper ores. Cu(I) is a soft acid with a strong affinity for sulphur, so the collectors are the thio-reagents: potassium amyl xanthate (sometimes isoamyl or n-butyl), sodium dithiophosphates with isopropyl or isobutyl chains, and thionocarbamate when copper-against-iron selectivity is difficult; xanthate and dithiophosphate are often added at the same point in a ratio of about 1:2 for better total recovery. Because the ore is of low value, the reagents are cheap and few: polypropylene glycol ether or branched-alcohol frothers, and only pH regulators as modifiers, chosen from the critical-pH relations of the minerals present. Highland Valley in British Columbia is the compendium's example; the lecture's porphyry scheme is the same in one line: lime as pH modifier to depress pyrite, xanthate as primary collector, a secondary collector varied among dithiophosphates, mercaptans, thionocarbamates and xanthogen formates.

The porphyry conditioning numbers. Flotation pH above 10 and most often above 11, where soluble copper and iron are precipitated and the froth is stable; pyrite depression at about pH 11.5 when chalcocite is the copper mineral, 10.5 to 11 with chalcopyrite. The lecture's lime series says what the pH buys: at pH 8 with 150 g/t of lime the rougher recovers 70.5 % of the copper into a 14.2 % concentrate; at pH 11 with 1200 g/t it recovers 92.1 % into a 10.0 % concentrate, and the coarse +150 µm fraction recovers 55 % against 20 %. Collector combinations follow the gangue (nine combinations in the lecture's table, from PAX with thionocarbamate and fuel oil for chalcopyrite-molybdenite in arkose and limestone to dithiophosphate with mercaptan for chalcocite-molybdenite in andesite), and frothers follow the clay: MIBC, pine oil, Dow 250, TEB and the alcohol-in-amine-oxide products in ratios tuned to kaolin, illite or montmorillonite, because clay slimes slow the kinetics (a pure kaolinite feed floats to 60 % copper in eight minutes where a quartz feed reaches 90 %).

Copper ores with iron sulphides. Recovering the iron sulphide is never the problem; keeping it out of the copper concentrate is. The general procedure is to depress the iron sulphides in the earlier, selective stages: float at high pH, where iron xanthates cannot form and where lime supplies the calcium that blocks the disulphide sites; use collectors other than xanthate to avoid dixanthogen-induced flotation; and, when a reactive pyrrhotite and steel grinding have pushed the pulp below the copper-xanthate field, add an oxidation step, aeration, before the copper flotation: in the compendium's laboratory example twenty minutes of aeration at pH 11 cut the pyrite in the copper concentrate from 50 % to 10 % by weight without touching the copper recovery. This is sometimes miscalled "activation by aeration". Small amounts of cyanide depress pyrite very efficiently (chalcopyrite, pyrite and marcasite are depressed at a few tens of mg/l around pH 8 to 10, where galena and covellite survive to pH 12 or more), the depression being surface precipitates of copper and iron cyanides and hexacyanoferrate complexes, but occupational safety limits its use. Aitik is the example plant: pyrite, pyrrhotite and a little molybdenite with the chalcopyrite, autogenous grinding, and the products balance of the retake exam of 2014.

Copper with molybdenum. Molybdenite floats by itself, so the separation is a depression of chalcopyrite: sodium sulphide or sodium hydrogen sulphide at kilograms per tonne pulls the potential below the copper-xanthate field, the molybdenite is floated with frother alone, often under nitrogen or oxygen-poor blower air to save sulphide reagent. Pinto Valley is the case.

Oxidic and weathered ores. A weathered ore has undergone secondary enrichment and is still treated as a sulphide ore; an oxide ore is fully oxidised to carbonates, sulphates and silicates. Three routes exist for Cu(II) minerals: direct flotation with a fatty acid (oleate or cottonseed oil), which cannot separate copper from calcium minerals in a limestone gangue; sulphidisation with sodium sulphide or, better, sodium hydrogen sulphide, which displaces carbonate and sulphate from the copper sites and reduces Cu(II) to Cu(I) so that the ordinary reagents work (dithiophosphates, thionocarbamates or mercaptobenzothiazole rather than xanthate, because these fine-grained ores auto-activate other minerals); and the old two-stage float, sulphide first then oxide with fatty acid, now replaced by leaching. Weathering also mobilises Cu2+ that activates sphalerite and pyrite, so either a bulk "brass" concentrate is made or cyanide destroys the copper-activated layer; the clayey gangue is washed off on wash screens or in log-washers and dispersed with hexametaphosphate or water glass. The compendium's leaching table is the decision rule: primary sulphides leach very slowly and need bacteria and high aeration, secondary sulphides leach fast, oxides very fast after acid pre-treatment, and flotation is the alternative for the first, possible for the second unless Cu(II) dominates, and preferably not for the third.

7. A plant that plays the chemistry both ways: clean pyrite

The compendium's Swedish example of soda activation shows what an engineer can do with ideas 4 and 5. A pyrite concentrate for sulphuric acid had to be very clean (at least 51.3 % S, little pyrrhotite, low Cu, Zn, Pb and As) so that the roaster cinders could be sold as iron ore. The route: milk of lime already in the grinding depresses the disulphides through adsorbed Ca2+ and pyrrhotite through the high pH (at least 10.5 in the copper-lead flotation, about 11.5 in the zinc flotation); the zinc middlings are deslimed and thickened; the underflow is conditioned with soda, which strips the calcium from the disulphide surfaces and buffers the pulp at pH 10.8; a large addition of isopropyl or isobutyl xanthate, 300 to 400 g/t, floats the pyrite by dixanthogen but not the simple iron sulphides; the concentrate is conditioned with lime again to desorb the dixanthogen; and a reverse flotation floats off the remaining copper and lead sulphides, leaving the pyrite as the middlings. Every step is one line of the chemistry above turned into a tank.

8. Circuits

Grinding is dictated by the work index: SAG and ball mills for most porphyries (El Teniente, Escondida, Batu Hijau at 44 to 52 % passing 74 µm), rod and ball mills in older plants, and for hard ores of work index 14 to 18 a SAG mill closed with a screen and a pebble crusher feeding rod and ball mills. The typical porphyry flotation circuit is a primary and a secondary rougher, a cyclone and regrind mill on the rougher concentrate, cleaners, and a column for the final concentrate; a cascade arrangement that re-treats the tailing raised a plant's copper recovery by about 2 %. Copper-gold ores use a rougher, scavenger, regrind and three cleaning stages; massive sulphide copper ores need a rougher, scavenger, regrind, high-intensity conditioning and four cleaners, and their chronic problem is low recovery and poor selectivity among all the sulphides. High-intensity conditioning, stirring energy of 4 to 8 kWh per cubic metre in the conditioner, pays especially when the rougher concentrate must be reground to minus 20 µm: it aggregates the fine copper particles selectively and moves the grade-recovery curve outward.

Worked example

Reading the lime series. The lecture's porphyry test gave, for the same ore, rougher recovery and grade against pH. The trade-off in numbers: moving from pH 9 (480 g/t lime) to pH 11 (1200 g/t) raised the copper recovery from 75.2 % to 92.1 % and cut the rougher grade from 13.1 % to 10.0 % Cu. On a 50 000 t/day plant at 0.6 % Cu that recovery step is worth 50000×0.006×(0.921−0.752)=51 t of copper per day, for an extra 50000×0.72kg=36 t of lime per day; the lower grade is the cleaners' job, and the coarse fraction (55 % instead of 34 % recovered above 150 µm) is where most of the gain came from. That is why porphyry plants sit above pH 11 even though the collector would work at 9.

The iron nobody ordered. A ball mill drawing 12 kWh/t with liner and ball wear of 120 g/kWh releases 12×120=1440 g of iron per tonne of ore. A porphyry collector dose is about 30 g/t: the mill adds fifty times more reagent than the flotation operator does, and it is a reducing, hydroxide-forming reagent. Twenty minutes of aeration before the rougher, or autogenous grinding, is the answer the compendium gives, and the reason the 2014 examiner asked why autogenous grinding is chemically better.

How the exam asks it

Topic T4, the flotation conditions of copper and lead-zinc ores, has appeared in 2015, 2020 and 2022, and the copper half of it is this lesson: name the copper minerals and the iron sulphides in the ore; say what state the chalcopyrite surface must be in (idea 4) and how the plant gets it there (aeration, no steel if possible); give the pH and its reagent (lime above 10 to 11 and why: iron xanthates unstable, calcium on the disulphide sites), the collectors (amyl xanthate with a dithiophosphate or thionocarbamate) and the frother; and draw the circuit with its regrind and cleaners. A six-point answer has all four blocks with one reason each. Topic T7 in 2014 asked for two ways to separate pyrrhotite from the disulphides: the magnetism of monoclinic pyrrhotite (idea 2) and the dixanthogen flotation of disulphides at high xanthate dosage in soda-alkaline pulp, where pyrrhotite cannot float (idea 5); the official solution is in the exam bank. Cyanide as a depressant (retake 2014) and the pyrite-versus-chalcopyrite critical-pH curves are the same chemistry from the other side.
