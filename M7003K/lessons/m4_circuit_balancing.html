Circuit balancing: products, water, and how far to trust your numbers

18 min read · module 4

Why this module exists

A concentrator is sampled every shift: tonnes on the belt scale, assays from the laboratory, per cent solids from a Marcy scale. None of those numbers agree with each other exactly, and most streams inside the plant are never weighed at all. Circuit balancing is the craft of turning that incomplete, slightly contradictory set of measurements into one consistent picture of the plant: how many tonnes go where, how much metal each stream carries, how much water travels with it, and how much of the feed comes round again. Every recovery figure, every reagent dosage per tonne and every plant report rests on it.

The module has three layers that build on each other. The products balance uses assays to find the tonnage of streams you did not weigh. The water balance uses dilution ratios to follow the water and gives you the circulating load. And when there are more measurements than unknowns, which is always the case in a real plant, least-squares balancing with HSC Sim adjusts every number a little so that the whole circuit closes, and tells you which measurements it had to bend the most. Three of the eleven exam topics live here (T9, T10, T11), and the Friday computer class is the third layer in practice.

The ideas

1. A balance is bookkeeping around a box you draw yourself

Draw a box around any part of the plant: one flotation cell, a mill with its cyclone, the whole concentrator. At steady state, nothing accumulates inside the box, so for every quantity that is conserved, what enters equals what leaves. Solids are conserved (a mill does not create tonnes), each element is conserved (copper does not turn into zinc), water is conserved. Particle size is not conserved through a mill, and mineral grades are only conserved if the minerals are not transformed.

In plain words: choose the box so that the unknown you want crosses its edge and as few other unknowns as possible. Around the whole circuit the recycle vanishes, which is exactly what you want when you are after the products; around one unit the recycle appears, which is what you want when you are after the circulating load.

2. The products balance: tonnes you did not weigh, from assays you did

A concentrator with a feed of Mf tonnes per hour makes n products. You weigh the feed and assay everything, but you rarely weigh the products. The solids balance is one equation; each element assayed gives another one, because the metal in the feed must be found in the products:

Mf=∑iMi,Mfaf=∑iMiai

where a is the grade of one element in each stream. With n products you need n equations: the solids balance and n−1 element balances. The compendium writes this as a matrix equation, Ap=m, where p holds the unknown product tonnages, the first row of A is all ones (the solids balance) and each further row is the assays of one element; the solution is p=A−1m. The Excel sheet PRODUCT.XLS does exactly this for up to five products.

In plain words: one element per product beyond the first, and the natural choice is each concentrate's main metal (copper for the copper concentrate, zinc for the zinc concentrate), because that is where the grade contrast between streams is largest and the answer least sensitive to assay error.

Two things fall out of the products balance and appear in every worksheet of this course:

  • Recovery of a metal: the share of it in the feed that reached its own concentrate, R=MccMff×100.
  • Concentrator difference: metal in minus metal out, for every element. When the tonnages were computed from that element's own assays it is zero by construction; for the other elements (silver in a lead-zinc plant, gold at Aitik) it is a real number and it measures how well sampling and assaying hang together.

3. The two-product formula: the whole balance in one line

When a unit or a whole plant makes only two products, concentrate and tail, the solids and one element balance solve in closed form. With F, C and T the tonnages and f, c, t the grades:

CF=f−tc−tandR=c(f−t)f(c−t)×100

In plain words: the concentrate mass is the feed times how far the feed grade sits between the tail grade and the concentrate grade. No tonnage of the concentrate is needed for the recovery, only three assays, which is why plants quote recoveries every shift.

The formula is exact only if the assays are. Its weak point is the denominator c−t: for an element with little contrast between the streams, a small assay error moves the answer a lot. That is the mathematical reason for choosing the main metal of the concentrate and it will return in idea 6.

4. Water: the dilution ratio carries everything

Slurry streams are described by the dilution ratio, D=W/M, the mass of water per mass of solids in the stream (1 m³ of water is 1 tonne, so litres of water per kilogram of solids). Everything else is a rearrangement:

W=D⋅M,Fw=1001+D,D=100Fw−1

where Fw is the weight per cent solids. The pulp density ρp that a Marcy scale reads, with water at ρl=1 and solids at ρs, is

ρp=D+1D+1/ρsso thatD=ρl(ρs−ρp)ρs(ρp−ρl)

Water balances follow the same box logic as solids: around any box, water in equals water out, and water in a stream is D×M. Because D is measured on every pumped stream and the solids are known from the products balance, the water in each stream is known, and the water additions (the ones nobody measures) come out by difference.

In plain words: D is the exchange rate between the tonnes you know and the water you want. A stream with D=2 is one third solids by weight; a thickener overflow with D=36 is almost clear water; a cyclone underflow at D=0.4 is thick pulp.

5. Circulating load: how many times the ore goes round

In a closed grinding circuit the cyclone underflow returns to the mill. The circulating load is that recycle stream as a percentage of the fresh feed:

CL=MrecycleMfresh feed×100

A circulating load of 200 % means the mill discharges three tonnes for every fresh tonne fed. It is found from a solids balance around the classifier once its feed or its overflow is known, and in the exam it is always the last line of a water-balance question, because the recycle tonnage is what the water balance was hunting for.

Note

A trap the examiner sets every year: the base is the fresh feed, not the cyclone feed. And which stream "returns" depends on the flowsheet: in the WIT circuit it is the tray's tailings, in the Hukki circuit the cone underflow, in a lamella circuit you must decide whether the recycled thickener overflow counts as solids going round (state your choice).

6. Too many measurements: least squares and the weights

In a real plant you have more measurements than unknowns: several elements, several per cent solids, a few tonnages. They will not all agree, so instead of n equations for n unknowns you write every measurement as a row of an overdetermined system Ap=y and look for the tonnages p that violate all the rows as little as possible. The ordinary least-squares answer minimises the sum of squared residuals S=∑(yˆi−yi)2:

p=(A'A)−1A'y

Ordinary least squares treats every measurement as equally trustworthy, which is false: a belt scale reads to 1 %, a pump-sump estimate to 30 %, a copper assay to three significant digits, a lead assay to two, a low-grade gold assay hardly to one. So each row gets a weight wi=1/σi2 from the relative standard deviation you believe in, and the answer becomes

p=(A'WA)−1A'Wy,S*=∑wi(yˆi−yi)2,sadj=S*m−n

The compendium's rule of thumb for the relative standard deviations: measured mass flows 1 %; very good assays 1 %, good 10 %, fair 30 %, bad 50 %, hopeless 100 %. All assays must be in the same unit (per cent, not g/t next to per cent) so that the coefficients of A have the same magnitude.

In plain words: weighting does not make the tonnages more accurate. It decides where the disagreement is dumped: on the measurements you told the program to distrust. That is why the compendium warns that with clever weights you do not get better flows, you get an error distributed to the assays, sometimes as absurd adjustments. You cannot force a balance out of bad data; somewhere the error has to pop up.

7. Reading the output: adjustments, residuals and the three-sigma rule

HSC Sim (and its predecessors, MATBAL and the CIRCUIT spreadsheets) prints, for every stream and element, the measured value, the balanced value, the standard deviation you assigned, the difference and the relative difference. Three habits turn that table into a diagnosis:

  1. Look for large adjustments. A correction of more than about three times the standard deviation you gave a measurement is too large: either the measurement is worse than you said, or the flowsheet is wrong (a stream you forgot, a node that does not exist).
  2. Change one thing at a time. Raise or lower the precision of one element consistently over the whole circuit, or of all assays at one badly sampled point; never tune a single number until the picture looks nice.
  3. Avoid negatives and zeros. A negative tonnage or a zero flow after balancing means the equations, not the plant, are wrong: check node connections and which streams are internal.

The copper concentrator example in the compendium shows the routine: a full flowsheet of rougher, scavenger, two cleaner banks and a regrind mill is reduced to seven nodes and thirteen streams (the grinding circuit becomes one mixing node, the flotation banks become nodes with a concentrate and a middling each), the Cu assays and per cent solids are entered, and the balanced table shows the feed, the cleaner feed and the final concentrate adjusted by 1 to 4 % relative, the rest by a fraction of a per cent.

8. From element assays to minerals

The laboratory reports elements; the plant, the flotation chemist and the circulating-load plot of the lab want minerals. Converting is stoichiometry: each mineral has a fixed element content, so the element assays are shared out among the minerals that carry them. For a copper concentrate with chalcopyrite (CuFeS2: 34.6 % Cu, 30.4 % Fe, 34.9 % S) and pyrite (FeS2: 46.6 % Fe, 53.4 % S), all the copper sits in chalcopyrite, so the chalcopyrite grade is the copper assay divided by 0.346; the sulphur that chalcopyrite accounts for is subtracted from the sulphur assay, and the rest is pyrite. What is left to 100 % is gangue. Once the mineral grades are known, the solids density of the stream follows from the mineral densities:

1ρs=∑ixiρi

with xi the mass fractions. This is the "Note! You need to calculate mineral grades from elements first" of the lab sheet, and idea 1 in disguise: minerals, not elements, are what the classifier sorts by density.

Worked example

Minerals and density from assays (the MINERAL exercise of the lesson sheet). A copper concentrate assays 28 % Cu and 30 % S, the sulphides being chalcopyrite and pyrite.

  1. Chalcopyrite carries all the copper: xcp=28/34.6=80.9%.
  2. Sulphur in that chalcopyrite: 80.9×0.349=28.3%; sulphur left for pyrite: 30−28.3=1.7%.
  3. Pyrite: xpy=1.7/53.4=3.2%; gangue by difference 100−80.9−3.2=15.9%.
  4. Density, with chalcopyrite 4.2, pyrite 5.0 and a quartz-like gangue at 2.7 g/cm³:
1ρs=0.8094.2+0.0325.0+0.1592.7=0.193+0.006+0.059=0.258⇒ρs=3.9g/cm3

Two-product formula, one line. A copper plant feeds 1.00 % Cu and makes a 25.0 % concentrate and a 0.10 % tail: C/F=(1.00−0.10)/(25.0−0.10)=3.6% of the feed becomes concentrate, and R=25.0×0.90/(1.00×24.9)=90.4%. No tonnage was weighed.

Water, one line. A cyclone feed at 40 % solids has D=100/40−1=1.5; if the solids density is 2.7, its pulp density is (1.5+1)/(1.5+1/2.7)=2.5/1.87=1.34 g/cm³, which is what the Marcy scale must read if the sample was good.

Tip

Before Friday's computer class, do the lab sheet's task a) on paper: a block diagram of the rod mill, pebble mill and cyclone circuit with every node and stream numbered. Then read the four conditions of task b) as weights: the rod-mill feed and the pebbles are weighed (1 %), the pebbles' assays are borrowed from the feed (a low weight on them), the middlings flow is only inferred (very low weight), sulphur and lead assays are the weak ones. That reading is the whole lesson in one exercise.

How the exam asks it

Three topics of the written exam come from this module, and each has a fixed shape.

  • T9, theory as true or false. Statements about nodes, the number of equations a balance needs, what a weight means, what MATBAL or HSC Sim does with the error. A correct answer is the verdict plus one line of reason, and the reason is always one of ideas 1, 2, 6 or 7.
  • T10, the water balance. A flowsheet with a mill, a pump, a cyclone and one more unit; tonnages and dilution ratios at some points; asked: the missing dilution ratios and the circulating load. The pattern: solids around the whole circuit, solids around each unit, then water around the same boxes; the recycle tonnage divided by the fresh feed closes it. Every balance written as an equation before the numbers; the examiner says so on the cover.
  • T11, the products balance. A worksheet: feed tonnage, grades, sometimes recoveries; asked for the tonnages of each concentrate, the metal columns, the concentrator difference and loss. Recoveries fix concentrate masses; the two-product formula fixes them when only grades are given; everything else is by difference; then say which elements close by construction and which show a real difference.

A six-point answer contains three things: the box you chose (a sketch with the streams named), the equations in symbols, the numbers with units and a check that the balance closes.

Words to know

TermIn plain words
NodeA place where streams meet or split, drawn as a box; every node gives a solids balance and one balance per element
Products balanceFinding the tonnage of each product from the feed tonnage and the assays
Two-product formulaConcentrate share of the feed from three grades: (f−t)/(c−t)
RecoveryThe share of a metal in the feed that reaches its own concentrate
Concentrator differenceMetal in the feed minus metal found in all products; zero by construction for the element that sized the streams
Dilution ratio DWater mass per solids mass in a stream; Fw=100/(1+D)
Pulp densityMass per volume of the slurry, what a Marcy scale reads; fixed by D and the solids density
Circulating loadRecycled solids as a percentage of the fresh feed
Overdetermined systemMore measurements than unknown tonnages; solved by least squares
Weight wi=1/σi2How much a measurement is trusted; the error is dumped on the low-weight rows
Residual, adjustmentBalanced minus measured value; more than three standard deviations is a red flag
Flowsheet reductionCollapsing units into the fewest nodes that the sampling points can support
