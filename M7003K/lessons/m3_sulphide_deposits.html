Sulphide deposits: how ores form, and why the process engineer cares

Why this module exists

Every flowsheet in this course is an answer to a question the geologist asked first: what minerals are in the rock, how big are their grains, what are they grown together with, and how much waste came along from the mine. A massive magnetite ore at 65 % Fe yields close to a tonne of concentrate per tonne of ore; a disseminated copper ore at 0.3 % Cu yields ten kilograms. Both are mined in the same country by the same company, and their concentrators look nothing alike. The ore-forming process decided that long before anyone bought a mill.

This module (Olof Martinsson's lecture, given in the course since 2013) walks through the sulphide deposits that feed the Nordic concentrators: magmatic nickel-copper ores, porphyry copper, volcanogenic massive sulphides, sedimentary-exhalative lead-zinc, and the gold deposits of the Skellefte district and Finnish Lapland. The reading below keeps the geology to what changes the processing: which minerals, in what texture, at what grade, with which companions.

The ideas

1. A mineral deposit is an enrichment; an ore is one you can sell

Every ore is a mineral deposit, a place where geological processes concentrated one or more minerals far above their crustal average. Not every mineral deposit is an ore: "ore" is an economic word, and it moves with metal prices, technology and the cost of the mine. Metallic commodities are grouped by use, and the groups return in every later module of the course:

GroupMetals
Iron and ferro-alloy metalsFe, Co, Ni, V, Mn, Cr, Mo, W
Base metalsCu, Zn, Pb, Sn
Precious metalsAu, Ag, platinum-group elements (PGE)
Minor metals and related non-metalsSb, As, Be, Bi, Cd, Mg, Hg, REE, Se, Ta, Te, Ti, Zr, and others
Fissionable metalsU, Th

In plain words: the processing course is organised by commodity because the mineral carrying each metal, and therefore the separation method, follows the commodity: sulphides float, iron oxides are magnetic, gold is heavy.

2. Four steps make any ore: source, extraction, transport, deposition

The lecture's frame for all ore-forming processes has four steps. Metals come from a source (the mantle or the crust); they are extracted from it by leaching or by partial melting; they are transported by a fluid or a melt; and they are deposited as ore minerals where something makes the fluid or melt precipitate them: cooling, mixing, boiling, a change in pressure or in chemistry.

Deposition happens in three main ways, and this is the step that fixes the texture of the ore, which is why it matters to mineral processing:

  1. Infilling of open spaces, by a silicate melt or by fluids (hydrothermal or meteoric): veins, breccia fillings, coarse crystals.
  2. Replacement of an existing rock, hydrothermal or metamorphic: intergrown, often irregular grain boundaries.
  3. Chemical sediment, precipitated from meteoric water or from hydrothermal fluids on the sea floor: laminated, banded, often fine-grained.

Where a commodity sits in the triangle predicts the concentrator. Orthomagmatic ores are coarse and rich in iron sulphide; hydrothermal ores are the flotation ores of the base metals; sedimentary ones are fine-grained and laminated, the hardest to liberate.

3. Texture is the first process parameter

The lecture shows four textures side by side, and each one answers a processing question before any test is run:

  • Massive ore: more than 50 % ore minerals, gangue mixed in. High grade, high mass pull; liberation is often easy, the problem is separating ore minerals from each other (copper from zinc, pyrrhotite from pentlandite).
  • Disseminated ore: grains of ore mineral scattered through the host rock. Low grade, the concentrate is a few per cent of the feed; every tonne must be ground fine enough to free grains that were never touching.
  • Banded ore: ore minerals alternate with other ore or gangue minerals in layers. Liberation depends on band thickness; selective mining or sorting can help.
  • Vein ore: a fracture filled with ore and gangue minerals. Coarse, high-grade, narrow: gravity and hand-sorting history, dilution from the wall rock.

The lecture's own numbers make the point. A massive iron ore at 65 % Fe reaches the mill at 59 % Fe after 10 % waste is mixed in during mining, and one tonne of it gives about 850 kg of magnetite concentrate. A disseminated copper ore at 0.3 % Cu reaches the mill at 0.27 % Cu, and one tonne gives about 10 kg of chalcopyrite concentrate. Same 10 % mining dilution, a factor of 85 in the amount of product, and therefore a factor of 85 in how much tailing the plant must handle per tonne of concentrate.

4. Orthomagmatic nickel-copper: sulphide droplets that sank

A mafic or ultramafic magma that becomes saturated in sulphur separates an immiscible sulphide melt, like oil in water. The droplets are dense, they sink, and they collect at the base of the intrusion: massive ore at the bottom, net-textured ore above it (sulphide filling the spaces between olivine grains), disseminated ore higher up. The sulphide melt scavenges nickel, copper, cobalt and the platinum-group elements from the whole magma, which is why a small volume of sulphide carries the value.

The ore is dominated by pyrrhotite, with pentlandite (the nickel mineral), chalcopyrite and PGE minerals. Whether the sulphur came from inside the magma or was taken from the surrounding rocks (an external source, as at Norilsk) decides how rich the sulphide is: internal sulphur gives high nickel and copper in the sulphide fraction, external contamination gives a larger tonnage of poorer sulphide.

The Nordic examples of the lecture, with the numbers it quotes: Kevitsa in Finnish Lapland, a 2060 Ma ultramafic intrusion, 160 Mt of reserves at 0.30 % Ni and 0.40 % Cu with cobalt, gold, platinum and palladium, mined in a large open pit at a strip ratio of 3 to 1, producing two concentrates, a nickel-cobalt-PGE concentrate near 12 % Ni and a copper-PGE-gold concentrate near 28 % Cu. Sakatti, disseminated, vein, semi-massive and massive copper-nickel-PGE in an ultrabasite. Pechenga on the Kola peninsula: 392 Mt at 1.2 % Ni and 0.9 % Cu in gabbro-wehrlite intrusions, with about 8 % Ni in the pure sulphide. Rönnbäcken (Nickel Mountain) in Västerbotten: a low-grade nickel ore (about 0.18 % Ni total, 0.10 % as sulphide) whose nickel sits in heazlewoodite, Ni3S2, with more than 70 % Ni and hardly any iron sulphide, so the concentrate can reach 25 to 30 % Ni, and magnetite is recovered as a second product at 66 % Fe.

For the concentrator: pyrrhotite is the enemy and the tool. It dilutes the nickel concentrate, consumes reagents and sends sulphur to the smelter, but monoclinic pyrrhotite is magnetic, so low-intensity magnetic separation and pH control are used to part it from pentlandite; when the ore has no pyrrhotite at all, as at Rönnbäcken, the grade jumps.

5. Talvivaara: sulphides in a black shale, and a plant without flotation

The lecture puts one sedimentary sulphide deposit next to the magmatic ones for contrast. Talvivaara in Finland is the largest sulphide nickel deposit in the EU: about 1000 Mt at 0.22 % Ni, 0.50 % Zn, 0.13 % Cu and 0.02 % Co, hosted by a graphitic black schist, with uranium as a by-product. The grades are far too low for a conventional concentrator, so the ore is crushed, stacked on heaps and bioleached: bacteria oxidise the sulphides, the metals dissolve, and nickel, zinc, copper and cobalt are precipitated from the solution as sulphides. It is the module's reminder that the ore-forming process, here a fine-grained sedimentary sulphide with little contrast to its host, can rule flotation out and force hydrometallurgy in.

6. Porphyry copper: large, low-grade, and zoned

Porphyry systems form in the root zone of stratovolcanoes, where a hot intrusion cracks its cover and fills the fractures with quartz veinlets. They are the large-tonnage, low-grade deposits of copper, gold, silver and molybdenum. Chalcopyrite (with bornite) and pyrite sit disseminated and in quartz veinlets; gold is mostly inside the chalcopyrite and bornite, though later processes may free it as native gold; silver mainly in chalcopyrite. The Lowell-Guilbert model describes the zoning: a low-grade core, an ore shell (about 1 % pyrite, 1 to 3 % chalcopyrite, traces of molybdenite), a pyrite-rich shell outside it (10 % pyrite, little chalcopyrite), and a peripheral zone with lead, zinc, gold and silver veins.

Aitik, Boliden's mine near Gällivare, is the Swedish porphyry-style example and the largest open pit in the Nordic countries. The lecture's numbers: 595 Mt mined between 1968 and 2012 at 0.38 % Cu, 0.2 g/t Au and 4 g/t Ag; reserves of 702 Mt at 0.25 % Cu and 0.14 g/t Au; resources of 2757 Mt at 0.17 % Cu. Host rocks are gneiss, biotite schist and muscovite schist; chalcopyrite, pyrite, pyrrhotite and a little molybdenite occur disseminated and in veinlets. The concentrator makes a chalcopyrite concentrate at 24.85 % Cu and 7.3 g/t Au, with a copper recovery of 89 % and a gold recovery of 52 %.

For the concentrator: everything follows from "disseminated at 0.25 %": autogenous grinding of enormous tonnages, a mass pull near 1 %, and a gold recovery that lags copper because part of the gold is not in the chalcopyrite. The Aitik products balance in the exam bank (retake 2014, question 8) is this deposit in numbers.

7. Volcanogenic massive sulphides: black smokers frozen in the rock

VMS deposits form where hot, saline seawater circulating through submarine volcanic rocks vents on the sea floor, the black smokers of today's mid-ocean ridges. The TAG mound on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is the lecture's model: a mound of massive sulphide over a stockwork of veins in an alteration pipe, anhydrite and quartz inside, a zinc-rich margin outside. They are an important source of base metals (Cu, Zn, Pb) and of silver and gold, and they come with arsenic, cadmium, mercury, antimony and bismuth. Ore minerals: pyrite, pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite, sphalerite, galena, sulphosalts, sometimes magnetite; gangue quartz, chlorite, anhydrite, carbonate. Typical grades are 3 to 10 % Cu + Zn + Pb and 0.2 to 2 g/t Au.

The Nordic VMS districts and their pre-mining tonnages: Skellefte (160 Mt), Bergslagen (250 Mt), Vihanti-Pyhäsalmi (70 Mt), Outokumpu (70 Mt), Orijärvi (2 Mt) in the Svecofennian, and Trondheim (75 Mt), Sulitelma (22 Mt) and Stekenjokk (23 Mt) in the Caledonides. The Skellefte district has more than 80 deposits, together 160 Mt at 0.7 % Cu, 3.0 % Zn, 0.4 % Pb, 1.9 g/t Au, 47 g/t Ag, 0.8 % As and 25.6 % S: Kristineberg, Renström, Maurliden, the gold-tellurium ore of Kankberg, and the old Boliden mine with its 15.5 g/t Au and 6.8 % As. Bergslagen's deposits formed in large caldera structures of felsic volcanoes; Garpenberg, mined since the 14th century, is a Cu-Zn-Pb-Ag deposit of VMS style hosted by felsic pyroclastic rocks and dolomite, with more than 15 Mt produced and 100 Mt of pre-mining resources in the area.

For the concentrator: zoned, often banded and fine-grained ore with four sulphides that must be parted: pyrite from the value minerals, then copper, lead and zinc from each other. That is the whole of the complex-ore module later in the course, and the reason the exam keeps asking for the flotation conditions of copper, lead and zinc. The "chalcopyrite disease" of the lecture, tiny chalcopyrite blebs inside sphalerite, is a liberation problem no grind can fully solve.

8. SEDEX and the sandstone lead of Laisvall

Sedimentary-exhalative deposits form when the hydrothermal fluid vents into a basin and the sulphides settle as a chemical sediment: blanket-shaped, often laminated and fine-grained bodies of pyrite, pyrrhotite, sphalerite and galena, with a minor stockwork in the footwall and bedded barite or chert; hosts are shale, siltstone, sandstone and carbonate. Zinkgruvan is the Swedish example: a Zn-Pb-Ag SEDEX deposit in production since 1858 at 8 % Zn, 3.5 % Pb and 70 g/t Ag in sphalerite, galena and pyrrhotite, more than 50 Mt of pre-mining resources. The Laisvall type puts galena into Lower Cambrian sandstone below the Caledonian nappes: Laisvall carried 5.4 Mt of lead metal, Dorotea 3.1 Mt, Vassbo 0.2 Mt.

The Caledonian orogen supplies the rest of the lecture's catalogue: Cyprus-type VMS in ophiolites (Løkken, 24 Mt at 2.1 % Cu and 1.9 % Zn), Kuroko-type in island arcs (Stekenjokk), Besshi-type (Sulitelma), Ni-Cu in mafic intrusions (Råna), lead-silver veins (Nasafjäll), banded iron formation and SEDEX in Vendian clastic units (Bleikvassli), niobium in carbonatite (Fen).

For the concentrator: fine lamination means fine grinding and slimes; the Zinkgruvan products balance of the 2014 exam (lead cake, zinc cake, silver in both) is this ore in numbers.

9. Gold: free, or locked in the sulphides

Gold arrives in two very different packages. Orogenic lode gold sits in structurally controlled quartz or quartz-carbonate veins, usually as native gold with low sulphide content, sometimes enriched in W, Ag, As, Mo or Sb. Björkdal near Skellefteå is the example: quartz veins in a quartz-diorite along a regional shear zone, 25 t of gold produced, a combined open pit and underground mine feeding 1.2 to 1.3 Mt a year through a jaw crusher, cone crushers, rod and ball mills, a gravity circuit with a Knelson concentrator and a flotation circuit for the gold-bearing pyrite; recoveries near 90 %. The other package is refractory gold: at Agnico Eagle's Kittilä mine in Lapland (33 Mt at 4.5 g/t Au along a shear zone in altered graphitic schist), 71 % of the gold is locked inside arsenopyrite and 22 % inside pyrite, only about 2 % is free and very fine, so the plant grinds, floats the sulphides, oxidises them under pressure and leaches the gold with cyanide in a carbon-in-leach circuit; expected recovery above 83 %.

For the concentrator: the gold question of every exam turns on this split. Free, coarse gold goes to gravity; gold in pyrite or arsenopyrite goes with the sulphide float and then needs the sulphide destroyed before cyanide can reach it. The lecture also notes the platinum-metal potential of the 2.4 Ga layered intrusions of northern Finland (17 Moz of low-grade reef and contact-type resources, palladium four times platinum).

10. Resources, reserves and the words on a mine's slide

Every company slide in the lecture uses the same vocabulary, and the exam borrows it. A mineral resource is the tonnage and grade that exists with reasonable geological confidence, classed as measured, indicated or inferred by how densely it has been drilled. A mineral reserve is the part of the measured and indicated resource that can be mined at a profit with a stated plan, classed as proved or probable. Kevitsa's slide reads: 160.6 Mt of total reserves (84.7 proved, 75.9 probable) at 0.30 % Ni and 0.40 % Cu, 480 Mt of waste, a strip ratio of 3.0 to 1, inside 274.8 Mt of resources at a 0.1 % Ni cut-off. Rönnbäcken's three pits quote strip ratios of 0.39, 0.89 and 0.54 with 30 Mt a year of ore. The strip ratio, waste tonnes per ore tonne, is the number that decides whether a low-grade disseminated ore is mined at all.

Worked example

From texture and grade to kilograms of concentrate (the lecture's own two ores, with the arithmetic filled in).

Massive iron ore, 65 % Fe. Mining mixes in 10 % waste, so the mill sees 0.9×65=58.5, the lecture's 59 % Fe. Magnetite, Fe3O4, is 72.4 % Fe, so the ore is 58.5/72.4=81% magnetite by mass, 810 kg in every tonne. A magnetic separator that recovers 95 % of it into a concentrate at about 70 % Fe (some silicate locked in) yields

0.95×810kg0.966≈800to850kgofconcentratepertonne

which is the lecture's 850 kg: the plant is a mass-moving operation, and the tailing is a small fraction of the feed.

Disseminated copper ore, 0.3 % Cu. After the same 10 % dilution the mill sees 0.27 % Cu. Chalcopyrite, CuFeS2, is 34.6 % Cu, so the ore holds 0.27/0.346=0.78% chalcopyrite, 7.8 kg per tonne. Floating 90 % of the copper into a concentrate at 25 % Cu gives

0.90×2.7kgCu0.25=9.7kgofconcentratepertonne

the lecture's 10 kg. Here 99 % of the feed is tailing, the concentrator is a tailing-moving operation, and the grind must free grains that make up less than 1 % of the rock: the reason autogenous mills, large flotation cells and tailings dams dominate the copper module.

How the exam asks it

The written exam has asked this module's geology only through its consequences and, twice, through mining methods (topic T8): describe sub-level caving and its prerequisites (2014) and the fills used in cut-and-fill and what is demanded of them (2015). Those two answers sit in the exam bank with the method drawn out; note that the mining methods themselves are not in this lecture's slides, so the bank's answers lean on the course's mining lectures and on standard practice, and say so. What the geology does for the rest of the paper is supply the reasons: why pyrrhotite must be handled in a nickel circuit (idea 4), why copper, lead and zinc must be separated one after another (idea 7), why a gold ore may need oxidation before cyanide (idea 9), and why an iron ore and a copper ore have concentrators of such different shape (idea 3 and the worked example). A six-point answer names the deposit type, its minerals and texture, and then draws the process consequence in one sentence each.
