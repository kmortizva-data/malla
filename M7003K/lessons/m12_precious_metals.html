Precious metals: where the gold sits decides how you get it out

Why this module exists

Gold, silver and the platinum metals are the noble metals: they hardly react, they are dense, and they are worth so much per gram that an ore at 3 g/t, three parts in a million, pays for a mine. Those three facts make their processing unlike any other module. Nobility means the ordinary collector chemistry barely applies and the metal is dissolved instead, by cyanide; density means gravity comes back as a first-line method; and grams per tonne mean that where the metal sits in the rock, free or locked inside a pyrite grain a few microns across, decides everything, before any flowsheet is drawn.

The reading covers the metals and their minerals, the gold ore types, the five ways of winning gold and how mineralogy chooses between them (with the ore microscopy of Gasparrini's paper as the guide), the plants the course uses as examples (Björkdal, Kittilä), and then silver and the platinum metals, which mostly ride along with other concentrates. The written exam asks about gold every year (topic T5).

The ideas

1. Noble, dense, soft: what the metals are like

Gold is one of the first metals mankind produced; four fifths of all the gold ever mined is still around, mostly as bars. Its density is 19.3 to 19.6 g/cm³, it is so malleable that it can be beaten to leaf 0.1 µm thick, and that malleability has a processing side effect: native gold grains in a grinding circuit smear into slightly hydrophobic flakes that float on the pulp surface of gravity equipment and are, in the best case, caught by flotation. Gold reacts with almost nothing. The compendium's short list is the whole of gold hydrometallurgy: aqua regia dissolves it (the electro-refining route); cyanide complexes it, as it does silver and copper, and is the leaching reagent; bromine forms halides and has been proposed; thiosulphate and sulphide complex it under certain conditions; and mercury alloys with it, the old amalgamation. Silver is a fine conductor, white, soft, blackened by sulphur gases ("oxidised silver" is silver sulphide), resistant to dilute acids except nitric. The platinum metals occur together as an alloy, native platinum, 60 to 80 % Pt with 1 to 10 % Os and Ir, 1 to 2 % Pd, 0.5 to 2 % Ru and Rh and 5 to 10 % Fe; osmium is the densest metal there is, 22.5 g/cm³, hard enough to scratch glass.

The noble metals also hide in solid solution: silver in copper and lead sulphides and sulphosalts, gold and copper in tellurides and selenides. And the most important gold "mineral" of a sulphide ore is not a mineral at all: gold as microscopic inclusions or rims on the surfaces and in the cracks of a host mineral, normally pyrite or arsenopyrite. Gasparrini's paper, the module's reading on gold mineralogy, sets the scale: economic gold grades are parts per million, the grains are often below ten microns and sometimes a fraction of a micron, they cluster in a few places in the deposit, and finding them under the microscope in a polished mount takes an expert a week. Her classification of how the gold sits is the key to the whole module:

Boyle's explanation, quoted in the paper, is worth one sentence in an exam: early, high-temperature pyrite and arsenopyrite took gold and silver into solid solution or as atomic layers on their growing faces; as the crystals cooled and recrystallised, the foreign atoms distorted the lattice and migrated to the low-energy sites, fractures and grain boundaries, where they gathered as native gold. Unfractured, unrecrystallised pyrite keeps its invisible gold; reworked pyrite shows it as grains.

3. Gold ore types

The compendium follows Evans. Vein (lode) ores form next to intrusions: quartz mobilised in cracks carries gold and some pyrite and arsenopyrite; the intrusion, leached of its small gold content, is the source, not the quartz, the sulphides or the alkaline host. Veins formed deep and hot (Kalgoorlie) or shallow and cool (Björkdal, whose gold minerals are native gold and tellurides). Metasomatic deposits in hard rock are the complex sulphide ores of the copper and lead-zinc modules; today most of them should be regarded as precious-metal ores, and in the Skellefte field (Renström, Petiknäs) the gold is electrum and gold on the rims of and as inclusions in pyrite and arsenopyrite. Placers are gold released by weathering and enriched where the water slowed, because gold resists chemical and mechanical work and is dense; Alaska, California and Australia began as placers, and the fossil placer of the Witwatersrand, conglomerate beds 0.3 to 3 m thick over a 30 m zone with balls of vein quartz as gangue, is one of the world's great goldfields, with very finely disseminated gold. Carlin ores, discovered only in 1965, are shale-hosted precipitates of gold below one micron, as films on pyrite and inside amorphous graphite, invisible to the eye, which is why they were found so late.

The lecture's tree sorts the same ores by what the plant must do: free-milling ores (oxide or sulphide) whose gold cyanide can reach after grinding; complex ores that consume cyanide (copper minerals), consume oxygen (pyrrhotite) or steal the dissolved gold back onto carbon (preg-robbing graphite); and refractory ores with gold locked in pyrite and arsenopyrite or with dissolvable iron, arsenic, antimony and carbon.

4. Five ways to win gold, and what picks between them

The compendium's list, with the condition that chooses each:

  1. Wet gravity for every ore type except where the gold is in solid solution or too fine. Today gold is enriched down to about 10 µm. The whole history of gravity equipment has been used on it: jigs, pinched sluices, spirals and shaking tables, strakes covered with corduroy (now patterned rubber sheets) whose stripes run across the flow, tilting tables, and lately the centrifuges: the batch-operated Knelson bowl that bleeds gold out of the grinding circuit, and the continuous Kelsey centrifugal jig. Gravity dominates for alluvial deposits and coarse-grained vein gold.
  2. Amalgamation, for alluvial gold with clean surfaces or ground vein gold: mercury partially adsorbs on gold, wets it (gold's surface tension is higher than mercury's, the gangue's is not), and gold sinks in mercury while most other solids float. Plates take 0.5 to 1.5 t of ore per square metre and day, use 30 to 50 g of mercury per tonne, recover 50 to 90 %, and lose the fine gold that is too light to reach the mercury; the amalgam, up to 20 % Au, is squeezed through cloth and retorted. The compendium's verdict is in italics: a method not to be used in modern mineral processing, and the Brazilian jungle's poisoned rivers are its epitaph.
  3. Flotation of the host mineral and smelting when the gold sits in solid solution or as microscopic inclusions in a mineral that is itself worth floating, copper or lead: the gold reports to the concentrate, then to the smelter's anode slime.
  4. Flotation of pyrite and arsenopyrite, then oxidation, then cyanide leaching when those are the hosts: roasting, pressure leaching in an autoclave, or bacterial leaching breaks the sulphide so that cyanide can reach the gold in the residue.
  5. Flotation for gold tellurides and selenides, and for free gold and electrum: their surfaces are non-polar, so gold is naturally floatable and a strong frother alone will catch it; ordinary collectors do not form a gold-collector surface compound because the metal is too noble, and if xanthate helps it is through its non-polar dimer, dixanthogen. Steel-media grinding coats the gold with colloidal iron hydroxides and hides it from every reagent.

Then leaching, with cyanide, bromine or thiosulphate, either directly on free-milling ore or on the oxidised sulphide concentrate of option 4. The cyanide reaction, unchanged since MacArthur and Forrest in 1887, needs oxygen and dilute alkaline solution,

4Au+8NaCN+O2+2H2O→4NaAu(CN)2+4NaOH

and the gold is taken back either by cementation on zinc dust, 2NaAu(CN)2+Zn→Na2Zn(CN)4+2Au, or, in the modern plant, on activated carbon: the lecture's flowsheet grinds with cyanide and lime to 45 to 50 % solids at minus 100 mesh, leaches in a train of agitators, adsorbs on carbon in six stages of carbon-in-pulp agitators with screens between them, strips the loaded carbon, electrowins the gold and reactivates the carbon (carbon-in-leach does the leaching and adsorption in the same tanks). The modern-trends tree of the lecture is the same choice drawn as a diagram: free-milling ore to conventional leaching; refractory ore either to a pre-treatment (roasting, bio-oxidation, or chemical oxidation by oxidative acid, alkaline pressure or acid pressure leaching) followed by cyanide, or to direct routes (carbon-in-leach, non-conventional lixiviants such as thiourea, alkaline cyanidation under oxygen pressure).

Gasparrini's table of what defeats cyanide is the checklist that turns "complex" and "refractory" into actions: gold between 5 and 50 µm leaches well if the host is pyrite, arsenopyrite or quartz, which are stable in cyanide; chalcopyrite, secondary copper minerals and pyrrhotite consume cyanide and oxygen (pyrrhotite is aerated in alkaline solution before cyanidation); silver-rich electrum tarnishes and grows a coating of secondary silver sulphide that encloses it and keeps the cyanide out; tellurides dissolve very slowly or not at all and must be oxidised first; secondary iron oxides and copper minerals form rims on gold grains; graphite adsorbs the dissolved gold; and totally enclosed fine gold in pyrite or arsenopyrite is only reached after roasting (arsenopyrite below 450 °C with limited air, or arsenic oxides coat the gold) or pressure or bacterial oxidation. Uranium hosts are treated in reverse: acid leach for the uranium first, then cyanide.

5. Two plants the course uses

Björkdal, near Skellefteå, is the vein ore: quartz veins in a quartz-diorite along a regional shear zone, native gold with few sulphides, about 25 t of gold produced from open pit and underground, 1.2 to 1.3 Mt a year. The flowsheet is options 1 and 5 in series: jaw and cone crushing, rod and ball milling, a gravity circuit with a Knelson concentrator taking the coarse free gold out of the grinding circuit, and flotation of the gold-bearing pyrite; recoveries near 90 %. Kittilä, Agnico Eagle's mine in Finnish Lapland, is the refractory ore: 33 Mt at 4.5 g/t along a shear zone in altered graphitic schist, with 71 % of the gold locked in arsenopyrite, 22 % in pyrite and about 2 % free and very fine. Its flowsheet is option 4: grinding, flotation of the sulphides, pressure oxidation of the concentrate, and carbon-in-leach cyanidation, with a recovery above 83 %. The two plants are the two ends of Gasparrini's diagram.

6. Silver: mostly a passenger

Only 15 to 25 % of the world's silver comes from primary silver ores; the rest is a by-product won in copper, lead and gold concentrates and parted at the smelter. Its own ores are three kinds of vein (native silver with cobalt and nickel minerals, Cobalt in Ontario; high-temperature veins with tin and tetrahedrite, only in Bolivia; shallow silver-gold veins with zinc and lead, Mexico and the US West), metasomatic lead-zinc deposits in limestone (Leadville), and the fine-grained copper-zinc-sulphur ores of Rio Tinto type (Renström). Its minerals are soft and dense, so they over-grind in hydrocyclone circuits, a warning the compendium repeats for gold. Flotation of silver minerals is no problem in itself, silver xanthates are stable, but the silver usually sits in minerals whose other metal dictates the behaviour: tetrahedrite holds Cu(I) and floats as a copper mineral. The lecture's table from the American plant surveys gives the reagent rules: lime has little effect on argentite, the halides, polybasite and tetrahedrite, but is deleterious to proustite, pyrargyrite and stephanite; talcose slimes lower the grade and starch corrects it; sodium sulphide is very harmful to pyrargyrite. Native silver can be won by gravity as a complement to flotation, and cyanide leaches every silver mineral except the arsenic and antimony ones, proustite, pyrargyrite and tetrahedrite, which is precisely the Skellefte assemblage.

7. The platinum metals: even more of a passenger

Platinum ores are basic rocks with very low grades. The Ural olivine rocks carry grains and patches in chromite at 0.05 to 0.005 g/t Pt, enriched by rivers into chromite concentrates of 5 g/t (up to 275 g/t); the Bushveld in South Africa, a bowl-shaped Precambrian norite intrusion, holds the largest proven deposit, the Merensky Reef, a metre-thick layer at 10 to 30 g/t Pt with 2 to 10 % sulphides that can be followed for 500 km; and the nickel-pyrrhotite ores of the Sudbury type carry about 0.2 g/t Pt that follows the sulphides. The lecture's pie charts say who mines them: South Africa 76 % of the platinum, Russia 44 % and South Africa 37 % of the palladium; the geology lecture added the 2.4 Ga layered intrusions of northern Finland with 17 Moz of low-grade resources at four parts palladium to one of platinum. Processing is gravity where the metals are alluvial with gold (amalgamation does not work unless the mercury is first activated with sodium amalgam), flotation of the nickel minerals where they sit with pyrrhotite and pentlandite, and recovery from the anode slimes of the copper and nickel electrolyses.

Worked example

Kittilä by the numbers. Take the mineralogy of idea 5: 71 % of the gold in arsenopyrite, 22 % in pyrite, 2 % free, 5 % elsewhere. Gravity on the grinding circuit might catch 80 % of the free gold: 0.02×0.80=1.6% of the gold. Sulphide flotation recovering 95 % of the pyrite and arsenopyrite takes 0.93×0.95=88.4% of the gold into the concentrate. Pressure oxidation opens the sulphides and carbon-in-leach cyanidation dissolves, say, 95 % of what is exposed: 88.4×0.95=84.0%. Total, with the other 5 % lost:

R≈1.6+84.0=85.6%

against the plant's expected "above 83 %". Change one assumption, cyanide the ore directly without oxidation, and the recovery collapses to the free gold plus whatever sits in fractures, a few per cent: that is what "refractory" means in numbers.

Why cyanide consumption has nothing to do with gold. The Elsner equation needs two moles of cyanide per mole of gold: 2×49/197=0.50 g of NaCN per gram of gold, or 2.5 g/t for a 5 g/t ore. Plants use hundreds of grams per tonne. The difference is eaten by copper minerals, pyrrhotite and the oxygen demand of the pulp, which is why the lecture's tree sorts ores by what they consume rather than by what they contain.

How the exam asks it

Topic T5 comes every year in one of four shapes. The 2015 paper asks about the Björkdal ore and its process (idea 5: vein gold, gravity plus pyrite flotation, with the numbers of the geology lecture). The 2014 retake asks how gold is floated (idea 4, option 5: non-polar surfaces, frother alone, dixanthogen if xanthate, iron hydroxides from steel media as the enemy, and host-mineral flotation when the gold is locked). The 2015 retake asks for the methods of gold recovery (the five options of idea 4 plus leaching, one condition each). The 2022 paper asks which processing options suit which deposit type (idea 3's tree: free-milling to gravity and cyanide, complex to conditioned cyanidation, refractory to sulphide flotation and oxidation before cyanide). A six-point answer always starts from where the gold sits, names the method that reaches it, and gives one reason the other methods would not.
