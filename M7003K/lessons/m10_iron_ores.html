Iron ores: magnets, mass, and the phosphorus that Kiruna floats away

24 min read · module 10

Why this module exists

Iron is 4.7 % of the crust, and the world digs some 3000 Mt of its ore a year, more than every other metal ore together. The concentrators that treat it look unlike anything in the sulphide modules: they move enormous tonnages, their product is 60 to 70 % of the feed rather than 1 %, and the separation is done mostly by magnets and water rather than by reagents. Sweden's iron ore, and the reason Luleå has a mineral-processing division at all, is the Kiruna-type apatite magnetite of LKAB, whose concentrators at Kiruna, Malmberget and Svappavaara are the working example of every idea below.

The reading covers the iron minerals and the ore types that decide the flowsheet; magnetic separation, from the wet drum that made Swedish iron ore to the pulsating high-gradient machines that upgrade hematite; gravity and the two ways flotation is used (to float silica away from hematite, and to float apatite away from magnetite); the LKAB plants and the pellet; and the iron ore products a blast furnace will accept. The exam's iron questions (topic T6) ask for the ore types, for how hematite ores are beneficiated, and for how the SLon separator works.

The ideas

1. The iron minerals: one that magnets love, one they hardly feel

MineralFormula% FeWhat matters for processing

Maghemiteγ-Fe2O369.9Magnetic hematite of some younger ores, or artificial

SideriteFeCO348.2Formed by weathering under reducing conditions
IlmeniteFeTiO326.8The titanium mineral of titano-iron ores

Magnetite is a spinel with a closely packed oxygen lattice only partly filled with iron, so its iron-to-oxygen ratio, and the share of divalent and trivalent iron, vary. It forms solid solutions with the other spinels at high temperature: Mg, Mn, Zn, Ni and Ti(II) substitute for Fe(II), Al, Ti(III), V and Cr for Fe(III). Sedimentary and skarn magnetites are clean; Kiruna-type magnetites, having seen higher temperatures, carry 0.2 % Mg, 0.1 % Al and Ti, 0.15 % V; magmatic titanomagnetites carry up to 10 % Ti and 0.5 % V, and when they cool the absorbed elements precipitate as lamellae of ilmenite along octahedral planes and of spinel along cubic planes that no grinding can liberate, so the crystals run at about 60 % Fe instead of the theoretical 72. Magnetite is plastic above 600 °C but hard and difficult to crush at ambient temperature, which is why coarse-grained magnetite ores resist grinding down to and below their crystal size.

The gangue is what the customer pays to avoid: quartz, chert and kaolinite (silica and alumina), apatite and its relatives (phosphorus), and the iron sulphides (sulphur).

2. Where the ores come from, read off a Pourbaix diagram

The compendium's three iron-water diagrams are a geology lesson. Hematite ores form under oxidising conditions in shallow lakes and lagoons, where Fe(II)-bearing water is oxidised to ferric hydroxide that ages into limonite, goethite and finally hematite:

Fe3++OH−⇒Fe(OH)3(aq)⇒Fe(OH)3(s)⇒FeO(OH)⋅H2O(s)⇒FeO(OH)(s)⇒12Fe2O3(s)

Magnetite ores are thought to be the product of submarine volcanism at great depth, where the oxygen potential is low, a variation on the process that makes sulphide ores from a mother rock that was extremely sulphur-deficient; for the Kiruna ores, formed at higher temperature, a magmatic intrusion into the side rocks is the rival theory, and the compendium notes that the theories are still disputed. Secondary processes then rework both: chemical weathering under reducing conditions mobilises Fe(II) and makes siderite; metamorphism of iron-bearing limestone makes the skarn ores of central Sweden.

3. The ore types, and what each one asks of the plant

Hematite-dominated ores are the world market. The quartz-banded sedimentary ores (banded iron formation: Minas Gerais, the Hamersley of Western Australia, Krivoi Rog and Kursk, Lake Superior, India, South Africa) have alternating layers of ore mineral and quartz from millimetres to centimetres thick; unmetamorphosed they are seldom economic, metamorphism recrystallises them to about 50 % Fe and makes them easier to concentrate, and leaching of the silica makes the upper parts into direct-shipping ore over a poorer banded ore below. The weathered ores of Lake Superior type formed where weathering removed the finely disseminated quartz, leaving loose limonite and hematite that sometimes hardened into hematite or magnetite; they carry the export trade of Western Australia and Africa. Laterites are tropical weathering crusts of iron and aluminium hydrates, mined for their nickel and aluminium, not their iron.

Magnetite ores are Sweden's. The metamorphic quartz-banded ores (the taconites of the USA, Sydvaranger, Stråssa, Håksberg, Kallak) have very variable grain size and, when fine, many gangue inclusions in the magnetite that limit the enrichment; metamorphism grows the coarse grains and removes the fine ones. The skarn and limestone ores of central Sweden and the Kaunisvaara district (Stora Sahavaara and Tapuli with less than 1 % Mn, Dannemora with more) are as coarse as the apatite ores but with more complicated grain borders, gangue inclusions and iron in the gangue minerals, so their recovery is lower than expected. The Kiruna-type apatite iron ores (Kirunavaara, Luossavaara, Tuollavaara, Svappavaara, Mertainen, Malmberget, Grängesberg) make up the greater part of Sweden's reserves and hardly exist elsewhere: compact bodies with little gangue, magnetite with some hematite, and 1 to 2 % phosphorus in apatite. Compact ore liberates well because the faces to gangue are few, but the fine-grained Kiruna ore needs long grinding for high enrichment, the breccia parts have hard mixed grains, and Malmberget is in a class of its own, with magnetite grains bordering magnetite grains along weak joints, perhaps from slight martitisation, so it liberates remarkably well. Titano-iron ores (Smålands Taberg, Routevare, Ulvön; none worked today) give iron, titanium oxide and vanadium.

The world numbers of the lecture set the scale: about 3000 Mt of ore a year, China 1320, Australia 579, Brazil 398, India 152, Russia 102, Sweden 26; reserves led by Australia (35 000 Mt), Brazil, Russia and China; China importing 820 Mt in 2013; and the high-grade Simandou deposit in Guinea, 2400 Mt of resources, as the next frontier.

4. Magnetic separation: the property that made Swedish iron ore

Magnetite is ferrimagnetic. That single property gave it a special place in beneficiation: concentration to high grades and high recoveries by magnetic attraction was developed in the early 1900s, primarily in Sweden, and it made Swedish iron making. The lecture's table of magnetic susceptibilities is the whole argument:

Low-intensity magnetic separators (LIMS), wet drums with permanent magnets, take magnetite out of a ground pulp cheaply and completely; a magnetite plant is a chain of them. The lecture's three drum flowsheets show the pattern: a primary mill, a cobber drum on its discharge that rejects liberated gangue before any more grinding is spent on it, a cyclone and a secondary mill, more drums, a magnetic thickener (a drum used to dewater), screens, and a finisher drum on the final product; in one variant a magnetic column and a disc scavenger clean the tail, in another a reverse flotation trims the concentrate. Everything the drums reject is tailing; the drums are the plant.

Hematite is only paramagnetic. New separators with high-intensity magnets (HIMS, 1960s) made the enrichment of weathered ores possible for the first time, and the high-gradient magnetic separators (HGMS) developed by Sala Maskinfabrik in the 1980s pushed it to fine and weakly magnetic material; both are expensive to run (electricity and water) and have largely given way to wet gravity, but stay where the separation is difficult, as in re-cleaner circuits. The SLon vertical-ring pulsating high-gradient separator, developed in 1981, is now one of the most popular machines for upgrading oxidised iron ores: it combines a magnetic field, a matrix that concentrates the field into steep gradients, a pulsating fluid that keeps the matrix from clogging and washes entrained gangue out, and gravity, and it is efficient, reliable and cheap to run. The lecture's flowsheets give its performance: a specularite feed at 34.9 % Fe upgraded through SLon-2500 roughing and cleaning to 67.1 % Fe at 74.7 % iron recovery, with the cleaner tail returned to the rougher; and oxidised ores treated by LIMS for the magnetite, SLon for the hematite, and a reverse flotation for the last silica.

5. Gravity, and the dig-and-ship ores

Hematite is dense, and for coarse and medium sizes gravity does what the magnet cannot: the Arrium low-grade plants of South Australia screen the feed, jig the lumps (minus 32 plus 8 mm) and the fines (minus 8 plus 1 mm), and put the minus 1 mm plus 45 µm fraction on spirals after desliming, the slimes below 45 µm being the reject. Dense-medium separation plays the same role in Australia. The richest sedimentary ores need none of it: they are dug in open pits, loaded on rail cars, and at most screened into lumps and sinter fines; the Newman Hub flowsheet is primary and secondary crushing, a coarse ore stockpile, scalping screens, three tertiary crushers and product screens into lumps (minus 40 plus 7 mm) and fines (minus 7 mm). Weathered ores are the difficult ones: clays and colloidal silica, limonite-dominated iron minerals, so the ore is washed, enriched by wet gravity and HIMS together, and sometimes flotation removes the silicates.

6. Flotation: silica away from hematite, apatite away from magnetite

The compendium gives three ways to use flotation on iron ores, and the first is the one not to use. Direct anionic flotation of the iron oxides with carboxylic acids, petroleum sulphonates or hydroxamates works, using the Fe(II) and Fe(III) surface precipitates to hydrophobise the surface, but it needs the particles fine (d80 about 100 µm), so the only agglomeration left is balling, and a hydrophobic magnetite will not hold together in a green pellet: the water-repellent surface destroys the capillary bonds. With the high reagent consumption on top, it is uneconomic today.

Reverse cationic flotation floats the silica away instead and leaves the iron in the cell. Its chemistry is charge: quartz has its point of zero charge at pH 2 to 3.7 and is negatively charged over the whole working range, hematite and magnetite have theirs at pH 6.5 to 6.7, so at pH 10 to 11 an amine cation adsorbs on the quartz and floats it, while starch (0.5 to 1.2 kg/t, amylopectin-rich) covers the iron oxides by hydrogen bonding to their surface hydroxyls and depresses them; ether amines are the collectors, sodium silicate the dispersant. The Tilden mine in Michigan is the classic: a martite-hematite ore with chert, ground autogenously, deslimed after selective flocculation (sodium hydroxide to pH 10.5 to 11, sodium silicate to disperse the silica slimes, starch to flocculate the iron minerals so that the fine silica leaves with the thickener overflow), then amine flotation of the silica in six lines of roughers and four scavenger stages, the froths going to tailings and the cell product to the pellet plant. Its design balance: crude ore at 35.9 % Fe, desliming overflow 20 % of the weight at 12.5 % Fe, flotation tailings 41.6 % of the weight at 19.7 % Fe, and a concentrate of 38.4 % by weight at 65.6 % Fe carrying 70.2 % of the iron. Iron ore frothers are the ordinary ones (pine oil, cresylic acid, alcohols, glycols, MIBC, and fuel oil with slimy ores), and the modifiers table is the flotation lesson's: lime, soda, caustic soda, sulphuric acid as regulators, calcium and magnesium ions as activators of silica for reverse anionic flotation, silicates as dispersants, starch, dextrin, guar, CMC and quebracho as depressants.

Apatite flotation at Kiruna is the third way and the Swedish one. The ore carries 1 to 2 % P in apatite, and a blast-furnace pellet must have almost none, so the magnetic concentrate is floated with modified carboxylates (Atrac, an ethoxylated tall-oil ester of maleic acid) that take apatite and the other calcium and magnesium minerals into the froth. The problem is the one the 2015 retake exam drew on its iron Bjerrum diagram: carboxylic acids have a strong affinity for the iron positions in the mineral surfaces, so the plant must add just enough collector to float the calcium minerals, keep fine magnetite out of the apatite froth, and above all not hydrophobise the magnetite, which would ruin both the recovery and the balling.

7. LKAB: the Kiruna line from sorting plant to pellet

The appendix draws the whole Kiruna system. Ore from the underground mine goes to the sorting plant: fine crushing, screening, dry magnetic cobbing that throws out waste rock (and sends gangue pebbles to the secondary mills), and a homogenising stockpile. The concentrators differ in their grinding: KA1 has three lines at 190 t/h of minus 10 mm ore at about 62 % Fe and 1 % P through a primary ball mill (3.8 by 4.9 m, 885 kW) and a secondary pebble mill (5.9 by 7.7 m, 2700 kW) fed with 3 t/h of pebbles; KA2 and KA3 have two lines at 400 to 500 t/h of fractionated feed under 100 mm at about 60 % Fe through a primary autogenous mill (6.5 by 5.3 m, 2700 kW) whose pebbles feed the secondary pebble mill (6.5 by 8.5 m, 4300 kW), with a spiral classifier on the primary discharge; Svappavaara uses a rod mill and a pebble mill. In every plant the sequence after each mill is the same: a magnetic separator, the pebble mill, a magnetic separator, the apatite flotation, a magnetic finisher, a thickener, and slurry tanks to the pellet plant; the tailings of the drums and the apatite froth go to the thickener and the tailings pond, with a magnetic scavenger on the thickener underflow in KA2.

The pellet plants (KK2, KK3, KK4, Svappavaara) take the filtered concentrate, mix it with a binder (bentonite) and additives (olivine, dolomite, limestone according to the pellet grade), roll it into green balls in balling drums, screen them, and fire them on a grate-kiln: updraught and downdraught drying at 40 to 400 °C, preheating to 1000 to 1200 °C, a rotary kiln at 1250 to 1280 °C, a cooler whose air is recycled to the grate, then rail to the port. LKAB's product codes on the appendix's map read like a product list: blast-furnace pellets (KPBA, KPBO), direct-reduction pellets (KPRS) and fines (KMR).

8. What the blast furnace will accept

Three products leave a mine: lumps of hard iron mineral, 20 to 50 mm, fed straight to the blast furnace; sinter fines, 1 to 3 mm, that must first be sintered with coke into larger aggregates; and pellets, fine mineral agglomerated and fired at the mine. The furnace's demands explain the whole downstream flowsheet: the reduction gas must pass through the bed, so the products must carry as little fines as possible and must not disintegrate or swell during reduction; they must contain no phases that liquefy at furnace temperature (feldspars); and phosphorus and the alkalis (sodium, potassium) are unwanted, which is why a Kiruna concentrate is floated for its apatite before it is ever balled.

Worked example

The Tilden balance, closed with the two-product formula. The design balance of the appendix gives the deslimed flotation feed at 41.8 % Fe, the flotation tailings at 19.7 % Fe and the concentrate at 65.6 % Fe. The share of the flotation feed that reports to the concentrate follows from the formula of the circuit-balancing lesson:

CF=f−tc−t=41.8−19.765.6−19.7=22.145.9=0.481

The deslimed feed is 80 % of the crude ore, so the concentrate is 0.481×80=38.5% of the crude, against the table's 38.4 %; the iron recovery to the concentrate is 38.5×65.6/35.9=70.3%, against the table's 70.2 %. The 20 % of the crude that left as desliming overflow at 12.5 % Fe took 7 % of the iron with it: the price of a fine-grained ore, paid before flotation.

How much apatite Kiruna must float. Fluorapatite, Ca5(PO4)3F, is 18.4 % P, so an ore at 1.0 % P holds 1.0/0.184=5.4% apatite. A pellet specification of 0.025 % P in a concentrate of about 71 % Fe allows 0.025/0.184=0.14% apatite to remain. If the magnetic circuit already rejects most of the liberated apatite with the gangue and the flotation feed still carries, say, 1 % apatite, the flotation must remove (1.0−0.14)/1.0=86% of it, with a collector that would just as happily attach to the magnetite: the balance the compendium calls delicate, in numbers.

How the exam asks it

Topic T6 has four shapes. The 2014 retake asks for the types of iron ores and how each is processed: ideas 3 to 7 in a table, one line per type (banded hematite: crush, screen or gravity, reverse cationic flotation; weathered: wash, gravity, HIMS or SLon; magnetite: grind, LIMS, apatite flotation; titano-iron: magnetic separation and lamellae that do not liberate). The 2015 paper sets true-or-false statements on iron ores, answered with the mineral facts of idea 1 and the separator facts of idea 4. The 2015 retake asks how hematite ores are beneficiated: gravity for the coarse, high-gradient magnetic separation for the fine, reverse cationic flotation with amine and starch for the silica, desliming and selective flocculation before it, and why direct flotation is avoided (idea 6). The 2020 and 2022 papers ask what the SLon separator is and how it works: vertical ring, high-gradient matrix, pulsating fluid and gravity acting together on weakly magnetic hematite and limonite, with the roughing-cleaning numbers of idea 4. In each case a six-point answer names the mineral property used, the machine, and one number.
