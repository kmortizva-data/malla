Lead, zinc and complex ores: one collector, four sulphides, and the order in which they float

Why this module exists

Lead and zinc precipitate under the same conditions, so they come out of the ground together, with about twice as much zinc as lead, and almost always with pyrite, often with pyrrhotite, sometimes with chalcopyrite, silver and gold. Their ores are the most abundant sulphide ores in the world and the source of some 85 % of its silver. A copper plant has one job, copper against iron; a lead-zinc plant has three or four minerals that all react with xanthate and must leave in separate concentrates, one after the other. That sequence, and the chemistry that makes each mineral wait its turn, is what this module teaches, and it is what Zinkgruvan, Garpenberg and the Boliden mills do every day.

The reading covers the minerals and ore types, the surface chemistry of galena and of sphalerite (one floats too easily, the other not at all without help), the two process routes and their reagent schemes, the extension to copper-lead-zinc ores, and the regrinding that fine-grained complex ores demand. The exam returns to it in the lead-zinc flotation conditions of topic T4 and in the worksheets of topic T11.

The ideas

1. The minerals, and the guests they bring

Galena, PbS with 86.6 % Pb, is nearly the only lead mineral mined; silver substitutes into its lattice, and in some deposits sits beside it as argentite (Garpenberg North). Its oxidation products, cerussite PbCO3, anglesite PbSO4 and the very insoluble basic lead sulphate and carbonates, matter twice: as ore minerals in oxidised zones, and as the hydrophilic hide that forms on a galena surface in a flotation pulp. Sphalerite, ZnS with 67.1 % Zn, is the zinc mineral; zinc in its lattice is exchanged for Fe(II), which turns it from colourless through yellow and brown to the black marmatite with 10 to 26 % Fe, and also for mercury and cadmium, which can shut a concentrate out of the smelter market. Iron-rich sphalerite keeps company with pyrrhotite. The oxidised zinc minerals are smithsonite (zinc spar, ZnCO3), hemimorphite (calamine) and willemite; zinc sulphate, unlike lead sulphate, is very soluble.

The guests: pyrite and pyrrhotite in nearly every ore (lead against iron is the classic selectivity problem, usually solved with cyanide); silver, in galena or in its own minerals; and, in weathered ores, secondary copper minerals whose dissolved copper activates the sphalerite before anyone wants it to. The lecture's word for those salts is flotation poisons.

2. Ore types, and what grain size they come in

The compendium sorts lead-zinc ores into vein deposits (silver-rich, with fluorspar, calcite and barite: Harz, Sala; small and mostly historic), sedimentary or metasomatic deposits in hard rock (the complex ores with iron sulphides, the important reserves: Bergslagen, the Skellefte field), hydrothermal precipitations in sandstone (the Buntsandstein deposits, Laisvall and Vassbo), and metasomatic deposits in limestone (the New Lead Belt of the USA, Zinkgruvan). The lecture's tables draw the processing line between two families:

  • Sedimentary (SEDEX-type) ores, Red Dog with 87 Mt at 5.0 % Pb, 16.5 % Zn and 72 g/t Ag, Sullivan, Mount Isa, Broken Hill, Bleikvassli: galena and sphalerite finely disseminated in carbonaceous shale, needing a relatively fine grind for liberation, often with secondary copper minerals that spoil the lead-zinc selectivity.
  • Mississippi Valley (MVT) ores, West Fork at 6.2 % Pb, Polaris, Pine Point, Leadville: galena and sphalerite in dolomite and calcite, coarse, unoxidised, without salts, sphalerite not pre-activated, liberated at a K80 of 100 to 180 µm, treated with simple reagent schemes to high grades. The textbook case.

The massive sulphide ores of the volcanic districts are the third family: relatively coarse pyrite with fine galena and sphalerite of variable iron content, liberation only at K80 of 15 to 40 µm, and the biggest pyrite burden of the three.

3. Before flotation: dense media, jigs and a wash

Three pre-treatments are typical. Vein ores and ores where galena and sphalerite sit as lumps or layers in limestone go through dense-medium separation first, to throw away a large part of the gangue as a coarse product. Where galena is coarsely crystallised, jigs or other wet gravity units inside the grinding circuit take out a coarse galena cake, partly because it sells as bedding for sintering, mainly to stop the "dead grinding" of galena: a heavy, soft mineral that a closed classifier circuit keeps returning to the mill until it is slime. And where weathering has released lead and copper salts, the ore is washed during or between the crushing stages, as it is before amine flotation of oxide zinc, where sulphate ions would steal the reagent.

4. Galena: floats readily, and is hindered by its own oxidation products

Du Rietz's Bjerrum diagram for lead is the map of galena flotation, and the 2014 exam's figure. The critical pH of lead with amyl or ethyl xanthate lies far to the alkaline side, so galena floats over the whole useful range, but pH should normally not exceed about 10, and the compendium's reasons decide the reagent choice: soda is the preferred regulator because it is also a dispersant; sodium hydroxide is the alternative; lime is not liked, because an excess makes local pH above 12 where hydroxide adsorbs on galena almost irreversibly, so lime added to the grinding circuit partly depresses the lead. Weathering products (lead sulphate, lead carbonate, the basic salts) are strongly hydrophilic and compete for the surface; in practice they show as slow kinetics, and an oxidised ore can be reconditioned with sodium sulphide to precipitate a fresh sulphide lattice on the galena. Anglesite itself is not floated by xanthate: long-chained carboxylic acids, or sulphidisation where the anglesite content is low.

Two depressants belong to lead. Sulphite and sulphur dioxide form the fairly insoluble lead sulphite and depress galena for a while; Canadian and Australian plants use it to float chalcopyrite first in an SO2 environment and take the galena when the depression has worn off. Chromate depresses galena fully, through lead chromate, the reaction of the flotation lesson, and it is what parts lead from copper in a bulk concentrate. Pålsson and Forssberg's predominance calculations for the real Zinkgruvan pulp add the qualification that matters at the cleaners: the lead-xanthate field covers the rougher point at about pH 8.5 but not the cleaner point at pH 12 unless the oxidation of thiosulphate is kinetically restricted and the carbon dioxide of the blower air is not unlimited, so lead flotation in the plant lives on kinetics, not on equilibrium.

5. Sphalerite: will not float until it is copper-activated, and then must be stopped again

Both xanthate and dithiophosphate form fairly soluble zinc salts, so sphalerite needs activation by copper sulphate: copper ions exchange with zinc in the lattice, Cu(II) sulphide precipitates on the surface and partly converts to Cu(I) sulphide, and from then on the grain behaves like a copper mineral towards xanthate. The course of the reaction depends on the iron content of the sphalerite and on temperature, enough that some plants warm the pulp in winter. The design of the activation is part of the flowsheet: copper sulphate goes in before the lime, the pH is then raised with milk of lime to 10.5 to 11.5, and about ten minutes of reaction are given by passing the pulp through several conditioning tanks in series, because the iron sulphides can be activated by copper sulphate too. Marmatite needs much more copper sulphate than clean sphalerite, in proportion to its iron content according to surveys of about thirty plants, floats with much slower kinetics, and builds up a recirculating load in the cleaning circuit.

Stopping the sphalerite is the other half. In the lead circuit, sphalerite that copper or lead salts have already activated is depressed with zinc sulphate, often with bisulphite or metabisulphite, which keep the pulp non-oxidising and hinder the release of the activating ions; cyanide removes copper from an activated surface by complexing it, but cyanide also depresses copper and iron minerals, so its addition point in the flowsheet must be chosen with care. Oxide zinc is a separate story: sulphidised smithsonite floats best with amines at about pH 11 with sodium hydroxide, after desliming and dispersion with water glass, because that flotation is sensitive to slimes and to sulphate ions.

6. Two routes for a lead-zinc sulphide ore

Stepwise selective flotation, the standard procedure: galena first, with a short-chained xanthate (usually isopropyl) or a dithiophosphate, in soda-alkaline pulp at about pH 9.5, with cyanide as needed to depress the iron sulphides and zinc sulphate to depress any lead-activated sphalerite; then copper sulphate to activate the sphalerite, lime to about pH 10.5, and the zinc float with xanthate. The coarse MVT plants in the lecture's tables are this route with numbers: zinc sulphate in the primary grind, the collectors in the secondary grind, cyanide to the lead cleaner, copper sulphate and the zinc collector in a conditioner ahead of the zinc rougher; lead concentrates of 73 to 78 % Pb at 88 to 98 % recovery, zinc concentrates of 59 to 63 % Zn at 80 to 94 % recovery.

Collective-selective flotation, possible when the grinding is autogenous and the ore holds few iron sulphides: galena and sphalerite are floated together without deliberate activation, at natural or neutral pH held with sulphuric acid, with a short-chained xanthate (ethyl) in a non-reducing pulp; the bulk concentrate is then split at about pH 12, where sphalerite is depressed and galena floats, or at about pH 10 with a huge addition of metabisulphite, 3 to 5 kg per tonne of concentrate. Very effective, with one structural weakness: the separation circuit has no tailing, so any dirt in the bulk concentrate must be charged to the lead or to the zinc concentrate.

Oxidised lead-zinc ores are activated by sulphidisation and floated with xanthate (galena) or with amines (sphalerite); lead can also be floated directly with carboxylates, which take nearly everything else too; combinations of sulphide and oxide floats exist, and leaching is the alternative the lecture ends with.

7. Massive sulphide ores and the copper-lead-zinc scheme

The massive sulphide ores add the pyrite problem to the sequence. The basic treatment is sequential lead-zinc flotation with a reverse flotation of iron sulphides from the lead concentrate, and, when the zinc concentrate carries too much lead, a de-leading step: at Santa Barbara the zinc concentrate is conditioned with 0.7 kg/t of cyanide, thickened, and floated in a lead rougher and cleaner with more cyanide and zinc sulphate, the lead leaving as the froth and the cleaned zinc concentrate as the tailing of that circuit. The desorption method does the same with sodium sulphide at 3 to 6 kg/t to strip the collector, thickening, soda to pH 10.5 and zinc sulphate at 2 kg/t, then a pyrite-galena rougher, cleaner and recleaner. Where the ore is partly oxidised and pyrrhotite and marcasite make it acid (pH 5.5 to 6.5, Faro), a semi-bulk flowsheet with soda ash, sodium sulphite, lime, sodium sulphide and a modified xanthogen formate does the job.

For copper-lead-zinc ores the compendium's principal scheme is the copper and lead-zinc options in series, and it is the answer to the 2022 exam's question:

The grinding runs in lime-alkaline pulp if pyrite and pyrrhotite are abundant (lime depresses the disulphides best, and xanthate is used in the copper-lead float), or in soda-alkaline pulp with dithiophosphates if the iron sulphides are few or if free-milling gold is present, since lime is believed to coat gold with hydrophilic calcium precipitates (an opinion, the compendium notes, not a proven fact). The bulk float produces a copper-lead concentrate with some sphalerite; silver, if present as its own minerals, mostly goes here, and free gold may float if the surfaces are clean. If the lead content of the bulk concentrate is high enough, a copper-lead separation follows at slightly alkaline pH with dichromate or chromate depressing the galena: a good copper concentrate and a dirty lead concentrate with most of the sphalerite, iron sulphides and gangue. The middlings of the copper-lead float, sphalerite and iron sulphides, are activated with copper sulphate and the pH raised above 11 with lime; the copper-activated sphalerite is floated with isopropyl xanthate, the lime keeping the iron sulphides down. If there is a market for pyrite, it is floated from the zinc middlings by the soda-activation route of the copper lesson. Plant reagent schemes in the lecture (Rosebery: soda ash and sulphur dioxide in the copper circuit at pH 6.8 with twelve minutes of aeration, soda and cyanide in the lead circuit at pH 9.0, lime and 950 g/t of copper sulphate in the zinc circuit) and the flowsheet with aeration, copper rougher-scavenger, regrind, three cleaners per metal, are the same scheme with the plant's numbers.

8. Regrinding: fresh surfaces and unmixed grains

The purpose of grinding in a flotation plant is to liberate the values at minimum cost: the primary grind is set for the best possible recovery, and the regrinding of intermediate concentrates and middlings serves to raise the grade by breaking mixed grains, and to rub the mineral surfaces clean, creating fresh surface for the collector. In the typical layout the rougher concentrate is reground and sent to cleaners and recleaners, with the cleaner tailing returned. Below about 40 µm tumbling mills lose efficiency, and grate-discharge mills cannot run balls under 20 mm, so regrinding is done in attrition (stirred) mills where abrasion does the work: vertical screw mills (Tower mill, VertiMill: up to 2240 kW, feed under 6 mm, grinding to 20 µm at a periphery speed of 3 m/s), vertical pin mills (SAM, Metso SMD: up to 1100 kW, feed 30 to 100 µm, 20 to 60 % solids, 5 to 100 kWh/t at 8 m/s), horizontal disc mills (IsaMill M10000: 2600 kW, 1.5 by 6 m, 20 m/s, feed under 1 mm to about 20 µm with ceramic beads of some 5 mm) and vertical disc mills (Outotec HIGmill: up to 5000 kW, F80 100 to 300 µm to P80 about 20 µm, beads down to 1 to 3 mm). The chemistry pays too: ceramic media instead of steel means no iron in the regrind pulp, which the copper lesson explained.

9. Copper-nickel ores, a preview

The lecture closes with the nickel ores that module 11 treats in full. In most of them pyrrhotite is the most abundant mineral, with chalcopyrite, pentlandite, magnetite and pyrite, and magnetic separation before or after the flotation stages is part of the flowsheet (Inco, Falconbridge, Norilsk). Pentlandite oxidises rather fast during aeration and covers itself with iron oxides and hydroxides that depress it, so the copper float at pH 10.8 to 11.2 with lime and thionocarbamate comes first, then a nickel float at pH 9.2 with sulphuric acid, copper sulphate and amyl xanthate. Pyrrhotite is held down by lime and cyanide with pre-conditioning, or by an hour of oxidation before cyanide, which takes its recovery to the first cleaner from 85 % to about 15 %; the lecture's table of separation methods lists lime with dextrin or starch, sodium hypochlorite with lime and cyanide, activated carbon with lime and cyanide, and lime with sulphur dioxide and starch at pH 5.5, each matched to how much pyrrhotite the bulk concentrate carries.

Worked example

Reading a plant's reagent table. The lecture lists Pend Oreille, an MVT plant, as: lead circuit at pH 8.5 with sodium cyanide 20 g/t, zinc sulphate 100, sodium silicate 300, dithiophosphate R242 15, PAX 10 and MIBC 8; zinc circuit at pH 10.0 with lime 200, copper sulphate 350, sodium silicate 200, PAX 25 and Dow 250 8. Every number is one line of the ideas above:

ReagentWhereWhat it does (idea)
Cyanide 20 g/tPb circuitDepresses pyrite and any copper-activated sphalerite (5)
Zinc sulphate 100 g/tPb circuitKeeps sphalerite from being activated while galena floats (5)
Sodium silicate 300 and 200 g/tbothDisperses the carbonate slimes of a dolomite gangue (3)
R242 15 g/t and PAX 10 g/tPb circuitA selective dithiophosphate first, a little amyl xanthate for the rest (4)
Lime to pH 10.0Zn circuitThe lime-alkaline pulp of the zinc float, keeping the pyrite down (5, 6)
Copper sulphate 350 g/tZn circuitActivates the sphalerite; a clean, low-iron sphalerite, hence a modest dose (5)
PAX 25 g/tZn circuitThe stronger collector, on a copper-covered surface (5)
Dow 250, MIBCbothFrothers, 8 g/t each, the smallest dose that holds a froth

Compare Sweetwater, whose sphalerite is marmatitic: 900 g/t of zinc sulphate in the lead circuit and 180 g/t of copper sulphate with an isopropyl-xanthate mixture in the zinc circuit, or Polaris at 600 and 300 g/t. The copper sulphate dose follows the iron in the sphalerite, and the zinc sulphate dose follows how badly the sphalerite wants to float uninvited.

Where the metabisulphite goes. In the collective-selective route the separation stage uses 3 to 5 kg of metabisulphite per tonne of bulk concentrate. For an ore of 3 % Pb and 8 % Zn making a bulk concentrate of about 15 % of the feed, that is 0.15×3to5=0.45to0.75 kg per tonne of ore, in the same range as the lime dose of a porphyry plant, applied to one seventh of the tonnage. The reagent is expensive per tonne of concentrate and cheap per tonne of ore, which is why the route can compete.

How the exam asks it

Topic T4's lead-zinc half appeared as the flotation conditions of a coarse-grained lead-zinc ore (2020) and of a copper-lead-zinc ore (2022), beside the copper questions. The pattern for a full answer: name the minerals and their guests; give the order (copper and lead first, zinc after activation, pyrite last or to the tailing) with the reason each mineral waits; give for each stage its pH and regulator, its collector, its activator or depressant, with the mechanism in one line (galena floats in soda-alkaline pulp with a short xanthate while zinc sulphate and cyanide hold the others; copper sulphate then lime then xanthate for sphalerite; dichromate for the copper-lead split); and draw the flowsheet with regrind and cleaners. The 2015 question on the flotation conditions of copper and lead-zinc ores is the same answer for both halves. Topic T11's worksheets (Zinkgruvan selective 2014, collective 2015, the column by size fractions 2015 retake) are these flowsheets in tonnes; and idea 6's remark that the collective route has no tailing in the separation is exactly why the 2015 worksheet's separation stage has two products and the bulk float the only tail.
