Autogenous grinding: the ore as its own grinding medium

18 min read · module 2 · King 5.11, 5.13-5.14

Why this module exists

Grinding is where a concentrator spends most of its energy and most of its steel. A conventional circuit crushes the ore in three stages and grinds it with rods and balls that wear away by the kilogram per tonne. Autogenous grinding throws the crushers and the media out: the large lumps of the ore itself, tumbling in a big mill, break the smaller ones. Sweden and Finland built their metal-ore concentrators around this idea for fifty years (Boliden, LKAB, Zinkgruvan, Pyhäsalmi), so the course treats it before flotation, and the written exam asks about it every single year: why it gives a better process result, what a SAG mill is, and what to do about the "critical stones" that refuse to break.

The reading has three parts: what autogenous comminution is and what it buys; how the charge of an autogenous mill works and why it can get stuck; and the circuit options (FAG, SAG, lump milling, pebble crushing) that engineers use to keep it unstuck.

The ideas

1. Autogenous means self-generated

Autogenous comminution is size reduction in which the ore itself supplies the breaking bodies. It comes in two families. Autogenous crushing is rock hitting rock in a vertical-shaft impactor (VSI) or, with a stretch of the word, a high-pressure grinding roll. Autogenous grinding is the tumbling mill family:

VariantWhat it means
FAG, fully autogenous grindingOnly ore in the mill; the coarse lumps are the media
SAG, semi-autogenous grindingOre plus a few per cent by volume of large steel balls
Lump milling, fractionated grindingThe potential critical-stone fraction is crushed before it enters the primary mill
APC, autogenous-pebble-crusherCritical stones are taken out of the primary mill, crushed, and returned
Pebble grindingA secondary mill whose media are pebbles screened out of the primary mill
Jet millingParticle against particle in a gas jet; autogenous by definition

In plain words: the word covers every machine in which the feed does the work of the steel.

2. What it buys: five kinds of advantage

The lecture's balance sheet, with its own numbers, is the answer the 2020 and 2022 exams ask for:

  1. Lower investment, about 25 %. No crushing station: the primary mill takes run-of-mine ore under 300 mm straight from the primary crusher.
  2. Lower operating costs, about 25 %. No crushing station to run, no grinding media to buy; and, if the circuit is well designed, the same energy cost as a conventional circuit.
  3. Better process results, 2 to 8 %. Two reasons, one physical and one chemical, that the 2014 examiner wanted spelled out. Physical: the breakage in an autogenous charge is largely attrition, chipping and self-breakage rather than blunt impact by steel, so grains tend to part along their boundaries: better liberation with less over-grinding of the soft minerals. Chemical: there is no iron from steel media. Grinding with steel puts iron into the pulp; the iron oxidises, consumes oxygen, drags the pulp potential down, and ferric hydroxide slimes coat the mineral surfaces, all of which hurts flotation (the flotation lesson's Petiknäs tests measured exactly this). An autogenous pulp is cleaner and more oxidising, and sulphides float better in it.
  4. Better availability. No crusher station with conveyor belts to rip apart, no stops to fill media, no rod chunks arriving in the pump sumps.
  5. Better working environment. AG mills run quieter, nobody handles grinding media, and there is no crusher station.

The price is in idea 5: the mill depends on the feed for its media, so it is only as good as the ore it gets.

3. Inside an autogenous mill: breakage, classification, recycle

An autogenous mill is a short, wide drum with a grate at the discharge end and pulp lifters behind it. King's picture of it (and the lecture's) is two processes in one shell: the charge breaks the feed by high-energy impact (a lump falling on the toe of the charge), low-energy impact and attrition (lumps rubbing), and the grate classifies: fine material passes through and leaves, coarse material is returned into the charge, a recycle inside the mill. Some mills carry pebble ports in the end grate, larger openings that let 30 to 80 mm stones out; Boliden's design screens them on a cylinder, meters them with a movable gate controlled by the power draft of the secondary mill, and returns the surplus to the primary mill. Those pebbles are the media of the secondary pebble mill, and they are the cheapest grinding medium there is.

Four breakage mechanisms act in the charge and must be modelled separately: attrition (wear, measured by abrasion tests), chipping, impact (breakage-selection functions, as in ball milling), and self-breakage of a lump that falls (the t10 and fracture-energy tests). In a SAG mill a fifth is added, impact by steel balls. King's chapter 5.11 to 5.14 carries the models; the lecture carries the consequence: the mill's product size and throughput depend on how much of each mechanism the ore allows.

4. A charge in balance, and the critical stones

The charge of an autogenous mill is only in balance if the feed keeps supplying it with enough coarse stones to act as media: the lecture's rule of thumb is at least 15 % of the feed coarser than 100 mm. The coarse stones break the feed and slowly wear down; the finest sizes are made by attrition. Between the two lies the trouble. Stones of intermediate size, roughly 20 to 80 mm, are too small to break anything else and too large to be broken by the others. They do not leave through the grate, they do not wear fast, so they build up in the charge, take its volume, and the throughput falls. Those are the critical stones, and the specific rate of breakage against size shows why: it rises with size in the fine region (media-on-media impact), drops to a minimum in the critical-size region, and rises again for large lumps that break by their own fall (self-breakage).

The lecture's list of what can go wrong, with the remedy against each:

  • Lack of coarse stones (less than about 15 % over 100 mm): change the feed, blend ore types, or pre-crush less; in a SAG mill, add balls.
  • Brittle stones that shatter before they have done their work, or strong stones that do not break: use variable-speed mills and tune the speed to the ore.
  • Lack of secondary pebbles: use worn-in pebbles from the primary mill, or reduce the power drawn by the secondary mill.
  • Critical stones: take them out and crush them (idea 6), crush the candidate fraction before the mill (lump milling), or turn the mill into a SAG mill.

Mill speed is a lever of its own. The lecture's factorial tests show the main effects of the per cent of critical speed: faster running gives less fines under 45 µm, lower grindability and a larger maximum deflection of the mill shell (a heavier, higher-thrown charge), so speed is chosen for the ore, not set to a standard.

5. Choosing between AG and SAG, and testing for it

The mill cannot be picked from a catalogue, because the media are the ore. The choice depends on two intrinsic properties of the ore, its competency (how well the lumps survive as media) and its work index, and on the total power and circuit configuration. Smith's rule of thumb, quoted by the lecture: an AG or SAG mill may be considered instead of a conventional ball mill when the unconfined compressive strength is below 180 MPa, the Bond crushing work index below 20 kWh/t, and the rod-mill work index not significantly higher than the ball-mill work index, both not much above 15 kWh/t.

Because the ore body varies, the metallurgy is sensitive to grind size, maintenance must be planned and expansions foreseen, the tests are done at bench and pilot scale on samples of very different size: pilot milling on tonnes at 100 to 150 mm top size; the media competency test on 750 kg of 165 mm rock; the drop-weight and Bond impact tests on single 50 to 75 mm particles; the MacPherson autogenous test on 135 kg at 32 mm; the SAG power index on 10 kg; and the Bond rod and ball mill tests in locked-cycle batches. The lecture's Temora gold-ore data show what the choice does:

6. The circuits

The lecture sets three conventional circuits against three autogenous ones, and each is a complete answer to "what circuit and why".

  • Conventional 1: three stages of crushing and a ball mill closed with a hydrocyclone at circulating loads up to 400 %; for easily ground ores; simple; high energy and media costs.
  • Conventional 2: crushing to 25 mm, a rod mill as the tertiary crusher, then a ball mill: the "fool-proof" circuit for any new deposit and for very tough ores, at high operating cost.
  • Conventional 3: screened-out pebbles of 30 to 50 mm from the crushing plant as the media of the secondary mill: high rod-mill throughput, cheaper secondary media, a cheap 10 to 15 % capacity increase, nearly iron-free grinding, and the nuisance of handling the pebbles separately.
  • FAG: mills that used to be under 6.5 to 6.7 m in diameter with a length-to-diameter ratio of 1 to 1.5 (up to 2), feed under 300 mm, the critical stones of the primary mill used as pebbles in the secondary mill, and a circulating load of only 5 to 15 % over the primary screw classifier.
  • SAG: length-to-diameter ratio 0.5, diameters of 38 to 44 ft (11.5 to 13.5 m), 5 to 8 % by volume of 80 to 120 mm balls; the secondary mill must be a ball mill, often two in parallel; the modern "fool-proof" circuit.
  • Lump milling (fractionated grinding): the 10 to 100 mm fraction, the future critical stones, is crushed before it enters the primary mill; less sensitive to changes in ore toughness, but the crusher is back. Pyhäsalmi and LKAB's KA2 and KA3.
  • APC, autogenous-pebble-crusher: the critical stones are extracted after the primary mill, crushed in a cone crusher and returned; the crusher is back, but inside the grinding circuit. Zinkgruvan.

Worked example

What the Temora numbers say when the products are made comparable. The autogenous run made a 750 µm product at 36.7 kWh/t; the 6 % SAG run made a 5330 µm product at 7.1 kWh/t. To compare them the SAG product must be ground down to 750 µm in a ball mill. Bond's law with a ball-mill work index of 15 kWh/t (a typical value, not in the slides) gives the extra energy:

E=10Wi(1P80−1F80)=10×15(1750−15330)=150×(0.0365−0.0137)=3.4kWh/t

So SAG plus ball mill reaches the same product for about 7.1+3.4=10.5 kWh/t against 36.7 for the fully autogenous laboratory run, and at four times the throughput. Two lessons: the laboratory AG mill was starved of competent media for this ore, so the "no media" advantage of idea 2 was lost in energy; and the comparison of circuits is always made at equal product size, never on the primary mill alone.

Is this ore an AG candidate? Take an ore with an unconfined compressive strength of 150 MPa, a crushing work index of 12 kWh/t, a rod-mill work index of 16 and a ball-mill work index of 14 kWh/t. Against Smith's rule: 150 < 180, 12 < 20, and 16 is not significantly above 14, though both sit near the 15 kWh/t line. Verdict: worth a media-competency and a pilot test; the decision is not taken from the table but from whether the coarse lumps survive as media.

How the exam asks it

Topic T3 appears in every paper. The 2014 question asks for the physical and chemical factors that make fully autogenous grinding deliver a better process result: the two reasons of idea 2, liberation by attrition and no iron from steel, each with the mechanism behind it. The 2014 and 2015 retakes ask what process options eliminate or diminish the critical-stone problem: pebble extraction and crushing (APC), lump milling of the 10 to 100 mm fraction, SAG balls, variable speed, feed blending, and pebble use in the secondary mill, each with one line on why it works (idea 4 and 6). The 2015 paper asks what semi-autogenous grinding is, what characterises it, when it is used and its advantages and disadvantages: the SAG line of idea 6 plus the Temora trade of idea 5. The 2020 and 2022 papers ask for the advantages of AG mills against conventional grinding: the five points of idea 2, with numbers. A six-point answer names three mechanisms and says for each what happens in the mill and what it does to the product.

Words to know

TermIn plain words
AutogenousThe ore breaks itself; the lumps are the grinding media
FAG, SAGFully autogenous (ore only) and semi-autogenous (ore plus a few per cent of large balls)
Grate discharge, pulp liftersThe screen at the mill outlet that keeps the coarse in, and the vanes that lift the fines out
Pebbles, pebble portsIntermediate stones bled from the primary mill through large grate openings, used as media in the secondary mill
Critical stonesSizes too small to break others and too large to be broken; they build up in the charge
Attrition, chipping, self-breakageRubbing, edge-breaking, and a lump breaking by its own fall
CompetencyHow well the ore's lumps survive as grinding media
Work indexThe Bond measure of how much energy a rock takes to grind
Lump millingCrushing the would-be critical fraction before the primary mill
APCAutogenous-pebble-crusher: crushing the critical stones after the primary mill and returning them
Per cent of critical speedMill speed as a share of the speed at which the charge would centrifuge
Circulating loadSolids returned to the mill as a percentage of the fresh feed
