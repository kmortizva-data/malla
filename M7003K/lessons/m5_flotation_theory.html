Flotation theory: wettability, collectors, and the pH that decides who floats

Why this module exists

A ground ore holds about 1014 particles per cubic metre, most of them finer than 0.1 mm. No sieve, no jig, no magnet can sort that many grains by what mineral they are. Air bubbles can: a bubble rising through a pulp picks up the particles whose surface repels water and leaves the rest behind, and the chemistry of the pulp decides which surfaces repel water. That is froth flotation, the most generally applicable concentration method there is, and the one the rest of this course keeps coming back to for copper, lead, zinc, gold, iron sulphides, apatite and the industrial minerals of period two.

The module has two layers. The physics of a bubble on a mineral surface (contact angle, work of adhesion, bubble size) says what a floatable particle is. The chemistry of the pulp (collectors, frothers, pH, activators and depressants, read on a Bjerrum diagram) says how to make one mineral floatable and its neighbour not. Two exam topics live here: T1, the flotation chemistry with its Bjerrum diagrams and critical pH, and T2, the reagents and the froth.

The ideas

1. A particle floats when water lets go of it

Put an air bubble on a mineral surface under water. Three surface tensions pull at the line where air, water and solid meet: solid-air γS/A, solid-water γS/W and water-air γW/A. At equilibrium they balance along the surface, and the angle θ that the bubble makes with the solid, measured through the water, is the contact angle:

γS/A=γS/W+γW/Acos⁡θ

The force needed to tear the bubble off is the work of adhesion, and inserting the balance turns it into two measurable quantities:

WS/A=γW/A+γS/W−γS/A=γW/A(1−cos⁡θ)

The water-air surface tension is set by the frother; the contact angle is set by the collector and its dosage. So the equation is the whole reagent scheme in one line: a weak collector (small θ) or too much frother (small γW/A) both make the bubble easy to dislodge. A bubble that sticks also has a maximum size that grows steeply with the contact angle, which is why the largest floatable particle depends on the mineral and the collector: sulphides with xanthate float up to about 0.2 mm, hematite with fatty acid to about 0.4 mm, coal to 1.5 mm. At the fine end the recovery of chalcopyrite holds down to about 2 µm; below 10 to 20 µm the particles follow the water round the bubbles and attach mainly by bubbles that precipitate on their surfaces from air dissolved in the pulp, rather than by collision.

In plain words: flotation sorts by wettability, and wettability is a number, the contact angle, that the collector raises and the pulp chemistry controls.

2. Collectors: a tail that hates water and a head that grabs the mineral

Most minerals are hydrophilic: their broken lattice binds an ordered layer of water. A collector is a hetero-polar organic molecule, a hydrocarbon chain (the non-polar tail) with a chemically active polar group, that anchors on the mineral and turns the layer of ordered water into a surface with low energy. It does not coat the grain: an incomplete surface precipitate at energy-rich sites (edges, corners, steps, dislocations) covering about 15 % of the surface is enough for flotation. Adsorption is in most cases chemisorption, a surface chemical product with an interaction energy of 1 to 10 eV, against 1 to 100 meV for physical adsorption.

  • Xanthates (dithiocarbonates, ROCS2−Na+ or the potassium salt) are the sulphide collectors. Ethyl, isopropyl, butyl, amyl and hexyl xanthate form a series of increasing chain length; ethyl gives a contact angle of about 60°, amyl 85 to 90°; beyond hexyl the salts are too insoluble and too prone to oxidation to be practical. The sulphide surface must first be oxidised (2MeS+2O2+H2O⇌2Me2++2OH−+S2O32−) so that a metal ion is loosened from the lattice and can form the very insoluble metal xanthate (heavy-metal xanthates have solubility products well below 10−10; zinc and iron xanthates are much more soluble, which is why sphalerite needs activation). Xanthic acid has pKa=2.3 and decomposes, so xanthate flotation is not possible below about pH 4. Consumption runs from 10 g/t to a few hundred g/t: more for short chains, more when other ions compete at the working pH, more with a finer grind and more slime.
  • Dithiophosphates (Aerofloats) carry two chains; their heavy-metal salts are slightly more soluble than the xanthates, so they are weaker but more selective: in alkaline pulp they do not hydrophobise pyrite or arsenopyrite, xanthates do. They are often added first, to collect the well-liberated, well-activated grains, with xanthate later in the scavengers for the rest.
  • Fatty acids and soaps (oleic acid, tall oil, alkyl sulphates and sulphonates) are the collectors of non-sulphide minerals: apatite, scheelite, fluorspar, barite, hematite. Their minerals have oxygen in the lattice and bind water strongly, so chains longer than nine carbons and dosages of 100 to 1000 g/t are needed; above the critical micelle concentration the reagent forms micelles that adsorb unselectively on edges and corners, mostly on fines. Unsaturated acids (one or more double bonds) float best; the pulp is kept between low pH and about pH 10 depending on the mineral, and it is the insolubility of the calcium or iron soap that decides the adsorption and the optimum pH.
  • Cationic collectors, the amines (dodecylamine and its salts, chains of 8 to 18 carbons), float silicates, potash salts and oxidised zinc minerals, largely by charge: the amine cation meets a negatively charged silicate surface. With them came reverse flotation, where the gangue floats and the value stays in the pulp.

In plain words: short thio-chains for sulphides because the bond is chemical and strong, long fatty chains for oxides because the bond is weak and the water clings, amines for silicates because the surface is charged.

3. The Bjerrum diagram and the critical pH

Carl du Rietz measured the solubility products of the metal-collector salts by potentiometric titration and plotted them in a log-log diagram of the metal-ion activity (pMe, upward is less metal in solution) against pH. That is the Bjerrum diagram, and it is the single most useful picture in sulphide flotation. Every solid is a straight line: a metal-collector salt MeX2 that releases neither H+ nor OH− is a horizontal line at

pMe=pKs−2pX({Me2+}{X−}2=Ks)

and the metal hydroxide Me(OH)2 is a slanted line, two units of pMe per unit of pH, because {OH−} rises tenfold for every pH unit:

pMe=pKshydroxide−2pOH=pKshydroxide−28+2pH

Reading it is the whole skill. To the left of a crossover (lower pH) the metal-collector salt is the less soluble solid: it forms on the mineral surface and the mineral is hydrophobic. To the right (higher pH) the hydroxide is the more stable solid, it wins the surface and the mineral is hydrophilic. The crossover is the critical pH for that mineral with that collector, and it is mineral-specific: flotation is made selective by holding the pulp below the critical pH of the mineral you want and above the critical pH of the ones you want to depress. The compendium's own example: with a metal hydroxide and 30 micromolar collectors, dithiophosphate crosses at pH 10.0 and ethyl xanthate at pH 12.5, so the xanthate keeps floating two and a half pH units longer than the dithiophosphate, which is exactly the "stronger but less selective" of idea 2.

Two consequences the exam likes. First, the closer to the critical pH you work, the better the selectivity and the lower the reagent consumption, because fewer unwanted minerals adsorb collector there. Second, to keep the metal-collector salt in equilibrium after a tenfold rise in {OH−}, one pH unit, the collector concentration must also rise tenfold: the critical-contact curves of galena with xanthate and dithiophosphate are straight lines of log⁡C against pH. Over-dosing the pH regulator therefore overshoots the critical pH and depresses the mineral; under-dosing it costs selectivity.

In plain words: a Bjerrum diagram is a map of which solid wins the surface at each pH; the mineral floats where the collector salt wins.

4. pH regulators, and what else they do

On the alkaline side the plant uses slaked lime, soda ash or sodium hydroxide; on the acid side, sulphuric acid. Each has side effects that a good answer mentions. Lime (CaO+H2O→Ca(OH)2⇌Ca2++2OH−) is cheap, its calcium ion is a flocculant, and calcium ions or calcium hydroxy complexes adsorb on the disulphides, so lime depresses pyrite. Soda ash (Na2CO3) works through the carbonate and bicarbonate equilibrium, disperses the pulp and reduces the entrainment of hydrophilic slimes into the froth. Sodium hydroxide gives hydroxide with no calcium. The acid side is limited by the collectors themselves: xanthate decomposes below pH 4 and fatty acids hydrolyse quickly in acid pulp. By floating in stages at decreasing pH it is in principle possible to float one mineral after another (chalcopyrite from pyrite by the acid route is done in some plants).

5. Activators: giving the surface a new face

An activator either gives the mineral a surface the collector can react with, or removes something that blocks it. The classic case is copper sulphate on sphalerite: zinc xanthate is too soluble to float sphalerite under most conditions, so copper ions are added and exchange with zinc at the surface,

Cu2++ZnS(s)→CuS(s)+Zn2+,CuS(s)→12Cu2S(s)+12S0

and the surface now behaves like a copper mineral towards xanthate (which is why, in the DIDA diagram of the 2014 retake, an activated sphalerite is read on the copper lines). Over-dosage activates pyrite too; under-dosage leaves the reaction incomplete. Sodium sulphide (Na2S⇌2Na++S2−, S2−+Me(OH)2⇌MeS+2OH−) turns the surface of oxidised minerals (malachite, lead and copper oxide ores) into a sulphide: the contact angle of malachite swings from wetting to strongly hydrophobic as the sulphide concentration passes a few tens of millimolar. Sulphide ions also strip old xanthate layers, because most heavy-metal sulphides are less soluble than the xanthates. Over-dosage makes the pulp strongly alkaline and depresses zinc minerals; a severe overdose drops the redox potential and depresses most sulphides; under-dosage does nothing at all.

6. Depressants: covering the surface, or stealing the activator

A depressant makes a mineral hydrophilic on purpose, by covering its surface, by removing the collector or the activator from it, or by complexing the activating ions in solution. The compendium's shelf:

  • Cyanide (NaCN⇌Na++CN−; Cu2++3CN−→Cu(CN)2−+12C2N2): forms insoluble solids and strong complexes with heavy metals. Its normal jobs are to depress copper in the separation of a bulk lead-copper concentrate, and, added to the grinding mill, to depress sphalerite and pyrite in galena flotation; it also depresses free gold and silver and is said to clean mineral surfaces. The pulp must stay above pH 7 so that no HCN gas forms.
  • Zinc sulphate with sodium bisulphite: keeps the pulp non-oxidising, hinders the release of zinc ions from the mineral surface and so blocks the self-activation of sphalerite in lead flotation.
  • Sodium dichromate (Cr2O72−+2OH−⇌2CrO42−+H2O; CrO42−+PbX2(s)→PbCrO4(s)+2X−): lead chromate is less soluble than lead xanthate, so chromate takes the galena surface, which is the point of question b) in the 2014 lead diagram (chromate beats amyl xanthate but not hexyl).
  • Starch, dextrin, CMC, guar gum ((C6H10O5)n): adsorb by hydrophobic attraction between non-polar crystal planes and their hydrocarbon chains, increase the negative charge and the wettability of the gangue; dextrin keeps talc down in sulphide flotation; over-dosage depresses fine sulphides.
  • Water glass (sodium silicate): a strong and selective depressant in non-sulphide flotation, a dispersant that peptises slimes, an activator of calcium minerals at low dose, and a reagent that makes the froth brittle.
  • Lime on the disulphides (idea 4); tannic acid and quebracho on calcite in fluorspar flotation.

The compendium warns that "activating" and "depressing" are not fixed properties: the same reagent can do either, depending on the conditions, and some reported effects are of "homeopathic" value.

7. Frothers and the froth

The frother stabilises the froth on the pulp surface, and in doing so it lowers the water surface tension, which makes the bubbles smaller and more suitable for flotation, strengthens the bubbles against coalescence, and shortens the induction time for a stable particle-bubble contact. It also has some collecting influence of its own, unselective and unwanted, which is why the plant uses the least frother and the purest frothing action it can, 10 to 50 g/t in sulphide flotation; fatty-acid collectors froth by themselves. Frothers are hetero-polar too: the polar group sits in the water, the branched hydrocarbon part points into the air, and a branched, unsymmetrical chain gives an elastic, easily broken froth with a short relaxation time. Pine oil (about 60 % terpineol) and cresylic acid are the old natural frothers; the aromatic dithiophosphates contain cresylic acid and so collect and froth at once; the synthetic ones are aliphatic alcohols of six to ten carbons (MIBC, TEB) and the polypropylene glycol ethers (DowFroth 250). Over-dosage gives a lathery, unselective froth; under-dosage a mushy froth layer.

The froth itself is a concentrator. Particles arm the bubbles, so the first cells of a bank carry a thick, rich froth with little air, while the last cells need lots of air for a thin, contaminated froth. Frother concentration rises up the froth as it decays, which keeps it stable, and the water drains down: entrained gangue drains back with it. This is the secondary concentration: in an apatite froth the P2O5 grade rose from about 30 % at the bottom to 38 % at the top over some 300 mm of froth. Froth height therefore controls selectivity and concentrate purity, and column flotation takes it to the limit: a deep froth washed from above with water, a collection zone below it, wash water widening the froth lamellae so that hydrophilic particles are rinsed back into the pulp, wherever a clean concentrate from fine material is wanted (the graphite tables of the lecture show columns matching four or five stages of mechanical cells in one or two).

8. The pulp is a reactor: potential, iron and grinding

Sulphide surfaces are not stable in aerated water: they oxidise, unevenly and in patches, and both oxidation and collector adsorption favour faults and lattice disruptions. That makes the pulp potential a flotation variable. The Petiknäs tests in the 1994 paper of the module ground the same complex ore in mild steel and in stainless steel: residual iron from mild-steel grinding dominated the result, slowing the kinetics and pushing more sulphur species (thiosulphate, sulphite) into solution by galvanic interaction, and only oxidising pre-conditioning with air brought the copper flotation back, at the price of kinetics; copper showed a marked potential dependence with a flotation "edge", and the minerals kept a "memory" of the pre-conditioning. Zinc sulphate, bisulphite, sulphur dioxide and cyanide all work partly by creating a non-oxidising environment. The Eh-pH diagram of the lecture makes the same point for chalcopyrite and ethyl xanthate: the stable species on the surface (CuEtX, dixanthogen, hydroxides) depend on potential as much as on pH.

9. Hunger flotation and the reagent regime

Reagents are added in a fixed order with conditioning time between them: pH regulator first, then activators and depressants, then the collector, then the frother, then air. Each mineral has its optimum at a specific pH, and at that pH it reacts selectively with the collector if the collector is scarce; add too much at once and the competing minerals take reagent and selectivity vanishes. Hunger flotation (the Swedish name for the technique) distributes the total collector in small portions along the bank under close pH control. The Boliden example of the compendium: pyrite and arsenopyrite are both disulphides and float alike, so with all the xanthate added at once the pyrite concentrate carried 50 % of the arsenic; spreading the same 190 g/t over the flotation time cut the arsenopyrite recovery to 20 % at almost the same sulphur recovery. Experienced flotation people prefer this to heavy doses of depressants and activators.

Worked example

The critical pH by calculation (the 2015 exam, question 1). Galena is to be floated with isopropyl xanthate at {iPX−}=10−5 M; the solubility products are Ks(Pb(iPX)2)=10−17.8 and Ks(Pb(OH)2)=10−14.37.

  1. The lead-ion activity the collector salt allows:
{Pb2+}=Ks{iPX−}2=10−17.810−10=10−7.8M⇒pPb=7.8
  1. The pH at which lead hydroxide allows the same lead-ion activity:
{OH−}2=10−14.3710−7.8=10−6.57⇒pOH=3.29⇒pH=10.7
  1. What it means: below pH 10.7 the xanthate line is the lower one in the diagram, lead xanthate forms on the galena and it floats; above 10.7 lead hydroxide is the more insoluble solid and galena is depressed. The same arithmetic, with the numbers read off the figure instead of given, is what the 2014 paper asks with its lead diagram, and the redrawn diagram in the exam bank shows each step in colour.

A line of the diagram from a solubility product. Lead ethyl xanthate at 3⋅10−5 M collector, Ks≈10−16.8: pPb=16.8−2×4.52=7.8, a horizontal line at pPb=7.8, which sits where the 2014 figure draws the ethyl xanthate line (about 7.6). That is how every horizontal line of a Bjerrum diagram is made, and why the lines of a homologous series step downward with chain length: the longer the chain, the smaller the solubility product, the larger pMe, the higher the critical pH.

How the exam asks it

T1, flotation chemistry. Five variants in six papers. Three give a Bjerrum diagram (lead with the xanthate series and the depressants, 2014; copper, zinc and manganese with DIDA, 2014 retake; iron with carboxylates for apatite flotation, 2015 retake) and ask where a mineral floats, which chain length a depressant can still beat, whether an ion depresses on its own, or what pH keeps a collector off magnetite. One gives the solubility products and asks for the critical pH by calculation (2015). The 2020 and 2022 papers ask true-or-false statements about xanthates. Every one of them is idea 3 plus one line of idea 2 or 6: name the two lines, say which solid wins below and above the crossover, and give the number.

T2, reagents and the froth. Secondary concentration and how it is influenced (2014: idea 7), the two uses of cyanide (2014 retake: idea 6), the purpose of the frother with the effects of under- and over-dosage and common examples (2015 retake: idea 7). A six-point answer names the mechanism, the two failure modes (too little, too much) and two named reagents.

The rule for all of them: the examiner marks the reasoning (the solubility argument, the reaction on the surface, the dosage effect), not the vocabulary; a sentence that says why beats three that say what.

Words to know

TermIn plain words
Hydrophilic, hydrophobicWetted by water, not wetted by water
Contact angleThe angle a bubble makes with a surface, through the water; zero means no attachment
Work of adhesionThe energy needed to pull the bubble off; γW/A(1−cos⁡θ)
CollectorHetero-polar molecule whose polar group anchors on the mineral and whose chain repels water
Xanthate, dithiophosphateThe short-chain thio-collectors of sulphide flotation; the second is weaker and more selective
ChemisorptionAdsorption by forming a surface chemical compound (1 to 10 eV), the usual case for collectors
Bjerrum diagramPlot of pMe against pH with one straight line per solid; horizontal for collector salts, slanted for hydroxides
Critical pHWhere a collector line meets the hydroxide line; the mineral floats below it, not above
ModifierA pH regulator, activator or depressant: anything that changes which surfaces the collector reacts with
ActivatorGives the mineral a surface the collector can bond to (copper on sphalerite, sulphide on oxides)
DepressantMakes a mineral hydrophilic: covers it, strips the collector, or complexes the activating ion
FrotherLowers the water surface tension, makes small tough bubbles, shortens the induction time
Secondary concentrationThe grade rise up through the froth as water and entrained gangue drain back
Hunger flotationCollector added in small portions along the bank under close pH control, for selectivity
Pulp potentialThe redox state of the pulp; it decides which surface species exist and whether the sulphide floats
Zeta potential, isoelectric pointThe charge of the particle at its slipping plane, and the pH where it is zero
