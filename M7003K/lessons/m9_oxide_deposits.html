Oxide deposits: layered intrusions, apatite iron ores, and the Nordic mines built on them

Why this module exists

The sulphide lecture explained the ores that float; this one explains the ores that are pulled out with magnets and sorted by density: the oxides of iron, chromium, titanium, vanadium and uranium. Nearly all of them formed in one of two ways, either by crystallising out of a mafic magma as layers and segregations, or, in the Swedish case that matters most to this course, as the apatite iron ores of Kiruna type whose origin geologists still argue about. The lecture (Olof Martinsson, 2013) is a tour of the Nordic oxide mines, and every stop on it is a plant the processing lessons have already used: Kemi, Tellnes, Kiruna, Malmberget, Kaunisvaara, Sydvaranger, Dannemora.

The reading keeps to what the process engineer needs: which mineral carries the metal, what it is intergrown with, how coarse the grains are, and what the deposit's numbers (tonnes, grade, phosphorus, sulphur) mean for the concentrator. The exam asks this module through the iron-ore questions of topic T6 (the ore types and their processing) and through the true-or-false statements about iron ores.

The ideas

1. Orthomagmatic oxide deposits: crystals that settled in a magma chamber

An orthomagmatic deposit crystallises its ore minerals directly from a magmatic melt together with the other magmatic minerals, and the ore occurs disseminated, as cumulate layers, or as segregations. Mafic and ultramafic magmas give nickel, copper, PGE, chromium, titanium, vanadium and iron; felsic magmas give niobium, tantalum, uranium, thorium, lithium, tin and beryllium (mostly in pegmatites); alkaline magmas give zirconium, REE, niobium, phosphorus and diamonds. The oxide minerals of the lecture's first slide are the module's cast: magnetite, chromite (Fe,Mg)(Cr,Fe)2O4, uraninite UO2, hematite, ilmenite FeTiO3 and rutile TiO2.

The type example is the Bushveld complex of South Africa, 2060 Ma, a layered intrusion with chromitite layers and the Merensky reef holding the world's largest resources of chromium (23 000 Mt of ore), PGE (8000 Mt) and vanadium (1030 Mt). The Nordic counterparts are the Karelian mafic layered intrusions of 2505 to 2428 Ma along the Finnish-Russian border (Mount Generalskaya, Fedorova, Monchegorsk, Koillismaa, Penikat, Kemi and others), the source of chromium, titanium-vanadium, PGE and copper-nickel.

2. Kemi: a chromite ore treated by density and by high gradients

Kemi, discovered in 1959, in production since 1966 and underground since 2003, is Europe's largest chromium mine: disseminated to massive chromite in serpentine-altered dunite at the base of a 2 km thick layered intrusion of 2433 Ma; 41 Mt produced to 2012 at 25 % Cr2O3, 1.25 Mt a year, 90.8 Mt of resources at 29 %. Its processing is the alloying-metals lesson's flowsheet in the geologist's words: the 12 to 100 mm fraction of the crushed ore goes to a heavy-media separation plant that has recovered a lumpy concentrate since 1984, replacing screening; the minus 12 mm goes to the concentrating plant, is milled and treated on Reichert cones that recover about 90 % of the chromite from the classifier feed, and the slimes go to the Sala high-gradient magnetic separation circuits adopted in 1995. Total chromite recovery is about 80 %. No flotation anywhere: chromite is dense and weakly magnetic, and that is enough.

3. Titanium and vanadium ores: ilmenite, and the vanadium locked in magnetite

The Rogaland anorthosite province of Norway, 931 Ma, holds the Tellnes ilmenite deposit: discovered in 1954, in production since 1960, reserves above 380 Mt at 18 % TiO2, the host an ilmenite norite of andesine, ilmenite, bronzite, olivine, biotite, apatite, magnetite and the sulphides chalcopyrite, millerite, pentlandite, pyrite and pyrrhotite. Titania A/S mines 3.44 Mt of ore for 0.83 Mt of ilmenite concentrate (2012): after the primary crusher the ore goes by tunnel conveyor to fine crushing and wet ball-mill grinding to below 0.5 mm, the coarse ilmenite is separated by gravity, the finer ilmenite by high-intensity magnetic separation, and a flotation step removes the pyrrhotite from the magnetic concentrate; only about 20 % of the ore is floated. The magnetite of the ore is rich in titanium and vanadium, the lecture's scatter plots showing how V2O3 in the magnetite tracks the titanium.

The titanomagnetite deposits are where vanadium lives. Ruoutevare at Kvikkjokk, a magnetite-rich anorthosite drilled by SGU in the 1970s: an inferred resource of 140 Mt at 39.1 % Fe, 5.7 % Ti and 0.2 % V at a 30 % Fe cut-off, in a titanomagnetite Fe(Fe,Ti)2O4 with ilmenite lamellae. LKAB's test of 1973 took the 38 % Fe feed to a 65 % Fe magnetic concentrate at 65 % recovery and a 44 % TiO2 ilmenite concentrate; the 2010 tests ground to P80 150 µm, took a 62.4 % Fe concentrate in three stages of wet magnetic separation, reground it to P80 45 µm and took a 68 % Fe pellet feed at 84 % overall recovery. The mine plan is a 1500 m pit at a strip ratio of 1.5, 10 Mt a year for 4.8 Mt of concentrate. Bricka in Hälsingland, a titanomagnetite body of 800 by 100 m in a gabbro, 12 Mt at 0.23 % V, 25 % magnetite carrying 1 % V, planned as three-stage crushing, rod mill, magnetic separation, regrind in two ball mills, final magnetic separation and dewatering, to a concentrate at 64 % Fe and 0.92 % V. Mustavaara in the 2.45 Ga Koillismaa intrusion, a layer of magnetite gabbro from the last phase of the chamber's crystallisation: reserves of 99 Mt at 14 % magnetite with 0.91 % V in the magnetite concentrate; the plan is 450 000 t of concentrate a year at 0.91 % V and 62 % Fe, concentrated electromagnetically, pre-reduced and smelted to 350 000 t of pig iron and 4900 t of vanadium in ferrovanadium. Engebøfjellet is the rutile end of the titanium story: 400 Mt of rutile-bearing eclogite at 4 % rutile from 15 000 m of drilling. And Rönnbäcken's nickel ore holds a little magnetite that magnetic separation will recover at 84 % into a 66 % Fe concentrate, 1.54 Mt a year at full production, whose assay the lecture prints: 0.077 % P, 2.9 % SiO2, 3.9 % MgO, 2.2 % Cr, 0.58 % Ni.

For the concentrator: a vanadium ore is a magnetite ore whose concentrate is sold for what sits inside the magnetite lattice; the plant is the iron lesson's LIMS chain, and the ilmenite lamellae are the part no grind liberates.

4. Apatite iron ores: the Kiruna type and the argument about its birth

The genetic classification of apatite iron ores, the lecture's tree, has two branches: a magmatic melt origin (intrusive as stocks, sills, dykes and breccias, extrusive as lavas and pyroclastics; Geijer 1910, Park 1961, Nyström and Henriquez 1994), and a hydrothermal origin (replacement, breccia, veins, exhalative; Parák 1975, Hitzman 1992, Rhodes and Oreskes 1999, Sillitoe and Burrows 2002). Iron deposits of Kiruna type are uncommon: the type deposits are Kiirunavaara itself and El Laco in Chile, with a few others in the Gawler craton, Iran and elsewhere. The characteristic features, in the lecture's list: magnetite, hematite and apatite; massive, breccia and banded textures; often a high content of phosphorus; a low content of titanium but a high content of vanadium; enriched in REE; hosted by metavolcanic rocks (porphyries). Loberg and Horndahl's plot of vanadium against titanium in magnetite separates the three families at a glance: apatite iron ores high in V and low in Ti, titaniferous iron ores high in both, banded iron formations low in both.

Kiirunavaara produced 1400 Mt between 1900 and 2012 and holds 700 Mt of reserves. Its massive magnetite ore comes in types the miners named in Swedish: B-malm, low in phosphorus, and D-malm, high in phosphorus with apatite bands, so that the distribution of P in the ore body is bimodal; around it the breccia ore, diluted by wall rock. The lecture's scatter plot of iron against phosphorus is a mixing line: every sample lies between pure magnetite and apatite, so the phosphorus grade is a direct measure of how much apatite the flotation must remove.

The other Kiruna-type bodies of Norrbotten fill in the range. Mertainen, breccia-style in a Svecofennian trachyandesite, 166 Mt at 35 % Fe and 0.005 % P, with very low phosphorus and sulphur, lenses of massive ore at 60 to 70 % Fe inside breccia at 20 to 30 %. Gruvberget, iron and copper, mined for copper in 1657 to 1684 and for magnetite since 2010, 64 Mt at 56.9 % Fe and 0.87 % P. Malmberget, 16 Mt a year, reserves 271 Mt at 41.9 % Fe, several ore bodies with hematite-magnetite in the west and magnetite in the east, recrystallised and deformed in upper amphibolite facies so that the ore and its host grew coarse, crystalloblastic to granoblastic textures with well developed triple-junction grain boundaries, which is the geological reason for the "exceptional liberation" the iron lesson noted. Grängesberg in Bergslagen, apatite iron ore in dacitic volcanics, 150 Mt produced, closed in 1992, reserves of 82.4 Mt at 37.2 % Fe and resources of 148 Mt, and a candidate for reopening.

5. Skarn iron ores: the Kaunisvaara district

Skarn iron ores are partly banded, with magnetite, magnesium silicates, calcium-magnesium silicates, pyrite, pyrrhotite and sometimes chalcopyrite; the lecture's ternary plots of six deposits (Sahavaara, Suksivuoma, Vieto, Masugnsbyn, Vathanvaara, Tapuli) show how alike they are: 1 to 5 % S, 0.01 to 0.1 % Cu, less than 0.1 g/t Au, with pyroxene-amphibole skarn between the bands. The numbers: Sahavaara 75 Mt at 41 % Fe (test mining 2007), Tapuli 101 Mt at 26 % Fe, Masugnsbyn 112 Mt at 28.6 % Fe, Hannukainen across the border in Kolari, 171 Mt at 35.3 % Fe with 0.18 % Cu and 0.09 g/t Au in a magnetite-pyrrhotite-pyrite-chalcopyrite ore. The Kaunisvaara project's 12 Mt a year plant, under construction when the lecture was given, made a concentrate at about 69 % Fe with less than 0.05 % S, less than 1 % SiO2 but 2 to 3 % MgO, high against the 0.02 to 0.48 % of other concentrates on the market: the magnesium silicates of the skarn follow the magnetite into the concentrate. The plant flowsheet in the lecture's rendering is the iron lesson's chain, primary grinding in autogenous mills, secondary grinding in vertical mills, magnetic separation, thickening, filtering, and trucks 150 km to Svappavaara for the railway to Narvik; the 2013 quarterly report gives 1.5 Mt of ore at 18.7 % Fe for 0.33 Mt of concentrate at 69.6 % Fe.

For the concentrator: skarn ores carry sulphur that the drums do not remove (pyrrhotite is magnetic) and magnesia in silicates that lock with the magnetite; a skarn concentrate is sold on its sulphur and MgO as much as on its iron.

6. Banded iron formations, Bergslagen, and the uranium shale

The Nordic banded iron formations are the metamorphic quartz-banded ores of the iron lesson: Sydvaranger at Kirkenes, a strongly folded Archean BIF with several ore lenses, 204 Mt produced from 1910 to 1997, restarted in 2009 for 7 Mt of ore and 2.9 Mt of concentrate a year, reserves and resources of 570 Mt at 31 % Fe, a concentrate of 62 to 65 % Fe with 6 to 12 % silica; Kallak near Jokkmokk, an SGU discovery of 131.6 Mt at 28 % Fe with 0.04 % P and 0.6 % S in amphibole, biotite, pyroxene, serpentine and carbonate, its magnetite commonly hematite-altered, planned as a 10 Mt a year open pit for 4.8 Mt of concentrate; Rakkurijokki near Kiruna, 74.5 Mt at 39.7 % Fe. Bergslagen's iron ores formed in the caldera structures of the felsic volcanoes that also made its sulphide ores: Dannemora, a skarn iron ore high in manganese, mined for at least 500 years and closed in 1992, reopened in 2012 with 33.6 Mt at 38.4 % Fe and 2.0 % Mn, its magnetite ore crushed and separated by dry magnetic separation in two stages into two products, Dannemora fines of 0 to 5 mm at 55 % Fe and Dannemora lump of 5 to 16 mm at 50 % Fe; and the Nordic Iron Ore project of Blötberget, Håksberg and Väsman, 600 to 650 Mt at 29 % Fe. The lecture ends with uranium: the Viken project in the Cambrian alum shale of Jämtland, Europe's largest known uranium resource, 2800 Mt at 0.017 % U with 0.27 % V, 0.024 % Mo and 0.032 % Ni, 1.5 km wide, 2 km long and open at length, planned as two open pits, 40 000 t a day and a bioheap leach, the Talvivaara model applied to a shale.

Worked example

Reading a deposit's numbers as a flowsheet. Take the lecture's three Norrbotten bodies and ask what each one's grade and phosphorus mean for the plant:

DepositFePWhat the mill does with it
Kiirunavaara D-oreabout 60 %1 to 2 %LIMS chain, then apatite flotation to reach 0.025 % P in the pellet feed: 5 to 11 % apatite must leave
Mertainen35 % (lenses 60 to 70 %)0.005 %No phosphorus problem: dry cobbing to reject breccia wall rock, then LIMS; the grade, not the chemistry, is the issue
Tapuli skarn26 %lowLong LIMS chain at a mass pull of about a third; sulphur and MgO, not phosphorus, decide the concentrate's price

The apatite arithmetic for Kiirunavaara, with fluorapatite at 18.4 % P: 1 % P is 5.4 % apatite, 2 % P is 10.9 %; a 0.025 % P concentrate may hold 0.14 % apatite. The magnetic drums reject most of the liberated apatite with the gangue, and the flotation removes what is left, which is why the lecture's Fe-P mixing line is the map of the plant's whole reagent problem.

Why Malmberget liberates so well. Metamorphism in the upper amphibolite facies recrystallised the ore into coarse grains with triple-junction boundaries between magnetite crystals: the grain boundaries are clean and few, so the ore breaks along them and the magnetite frees at a coarse grind, where Kiirunavaara's finer, compact ore needs long grinding. Same mineral, same company, a different concentrator.

How the exam asks it

No exam question is set on oxide geology alone. The module supplies the reasons behind topic T6: what an apatite iron ore is and why Kiruna floats apatite (idea 4), what a skarn ore carries and why its concentrate is judged on sulphur and MgO (idea 5), why a banded iron formation is fine-grained and needs the taconite route (idea 6), and why a titanomagnetite is mined for vanadium and cannot be liberated from its ilmenite lamellae (idea 3). When the 2014 retake asks for the types of iron ores and their processing, this lecture gives the Swedish names and numbers to put beside each type; when the 2015 paper sets true-or-false statements, the facts about Kiruna type (high V, low Ti, apatite, magnetite and hematite) are the ones to know. A good answer names the deposit type, one Nordic example with its grade, and the process consequence.

Words to know

TermIn plain words
OrthomagmaticOre minerals crystallised straight from the magma, as layers, segregations or disseminations
Cumulate, chromititeCrystals that settled and accumulated in a magma chamber; a rock made mostly of chromite
Layered intrusionA large mafic body that cooled in layers of different minerals: Bushveld, Kemi, Koillismaa
TitanomagnetiteMagnetite with titanium and vanadium in its lattice and ilmenite lamellae; mined for V and Fe
Apatite iron ore, Kiruna typeMagnetite-hematite ore with apatite, high V and low Ti, in metavolcanic rocks; origin disputed

Banded iron formationLayered sedimentary iron ore; folded and metamorphosed in the Nordic shield
