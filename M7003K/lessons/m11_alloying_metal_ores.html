Alloying metal ores: nickel two ways, tungsten two minerals, and the heavy oxides that gravity sorts

Why this module exists

Stainless steel is iron with 18 % chromium and 8 % nickel; tool steel carries tungsten, molybdenum and vanadium; every jet engine and every mobile phone holds titanium, niobium and tantalum. The metals of this module are mined for what they do to other metals, and they arrive in ores that look nothing like each other: sulphides that float like copper minerals, laterites that cannot be concentrated at all and go straight to a furnace or an autoclave, and dense oxides (cassiterite, chromite, wolframite, ilmenite, columbite) that gravity has sorted for a hundred years and magnets and flotation now finish. The module is the course's tour of how the same handful of unit operations is rearranged for each of them.

The reading takes nickel and cobalt first, because they carry the two big ideas: the sulphide route through flotation and the laterite route through pyrometallurgy or pressure leaching. Then chromium, manganese, molybdenum, tungsten, vanadium, titanium, tin and the coltan pair, each in a few paragraphs built around its minerals and the one property that separates them. Two of the exam's questions live here (topic T7): how tungsten ores are beneficiated, and the nickel routes.

The ideas

1. Nickel: two families of ore, two industries

Nickel is the 24th element of the crust at 0.008 %, but it concentrates in the mafic and ultramafic rocks (peridotite 0.20 % Ni, gabbro 0.016 %, granite 0.0002 %), and nature enriched it in two unrelated ways. Sulphide ores formed when a sulphide melt separated from a cooling mafic magma, scavenged the nickel and copper, and settled (the geology lesson's story): pentlandite (Ni,Fe)9S8 with 34 % Ni is the mineral of about 65 % of the world's nickel, always intergrown with far more pyrrhotite, plus chalcopyrite and magnetite; they hold about 35 % of the reserves but gave most of the production for a century, from Sudbury, Norilsk, Kambalda, Thompson, the Finnish mines and Pechenga. Laterite ores formed by tropical weathering of ultramafic rock: rain leached silica and magnesium out, iron and nickel stayed behind, and the nickel re-precipitated deeper in the profile, in two horizons: limonite on top (goethite with 1 to 1.5 % Ni and 45 % Fe) and saprolite below (nickel silicates, garnierite and nickeliferous serpentine, 1.5 to 3 % Ni, high magnesia). Laterites hold about 70 % of the resources, in New Caledonia, Cuba, Indonesia, the Philippines and Australia, and they are what nickel's future is made of.

The lecture's tree draws the consequence in one picture: laterite ore, 70 % of the resources and 40 % of the production, splits into saprolite (drying, calcining, smelting to ferronickel or matte) and limonite (high-temperature acid leaching, refining to nickel); sulphide ore, 30 % of the resources and 60 % of the production, goes concentrating, smelting, refining. Cobalt, in almost every case, rides along: as a minor constituent of pentlandite in the sulphides, of the goethite in the limonites, and it is separated at the refinery, not the mill.

2. The sulphide route: a copper plant with a pyrrhotite problem

A sulphide nickel concentrator is a copper concentrator whose main enemy is an iron sulphide that outweighs the value minerals ten to one. The lecture's plant tables give the shape: crushing in gyratories (Sudbury, Thompson) or a jaw (Kambalda), grinding in semi-autogenous mills of 10 by 4 m at 8200 kW (Sudbury) or a fully autogenous mill at Kambalda closed with cyclones and flash flotation of the cyclone underflow, then rougher-scavenger flotation with regrinding, column cleaners and recleaners to an 18 % Ni concentrate; Kambalda floats 1.5 Mt a year of 2.3 % Ni ore to a 13 % concentrate at 90 % recovery, Raglan 2.4 % ore to 18 % at 87 %, Thompson 2.4 % to 12 % at 90 %. The Finnish mills (Kotalahti, Hitura, Vuonos) ground in a rod mill and pebble mills and floated in acid circuits at pH 5.5, with sulphuric acid, sodium amyl xanthate, and sulphuric acid or CMC to depress the easily floated silicates, edenite and anthophyllite at Kotalahti, talc at Hitura, whose flowsheets are the lecture's; the concentrates go to the Harjavalta flash smelter, whose high-grade matte (60 % Ni, 30 % Cu, 7 % S) is leached and electrowon.

Two things make the reagent scheme. First, pentlandite oxidises fast: on aeration it covers itself with iron oxides and hydroxides that depress it, so the copper is often floated first (pH 10.8 to 11.2 with lime and a thionocarbamate) and the nickel after, at pH 9.2 with sulphuric acid, copper sulphate to activate the tarnished pentlandite and amyl xanthate, the scheme the lead-zinc lesson previewed. Second, pyrrhotite: its monoclinic form is removed by wet low-intensity magnetic separators before or after flotation (Inco's pyrrhotite rejection cut the Sudbury smelter's sulphur dioxide by a quarter), and the rest is depressed with lime and cyanide after pre-conditioning, or with an hour of oxidation before cyanide, which took its recovery to the first cleaner from 85 % to 15 % in the lecture's table; the plant survey lists collectors (sodium ethyl, potassium amyl and isobutyl xanthates, Flex 31), frothers and, for each plant, how much pyrrhotite and gangue it manages to reject (Clarabelle 82 % pyrrhotite to waste, Thompson 55 %). Talc, the floatable magnesium silicate of ultramafic hosts, is depressed with guar gum or CMC (Kambalda, Thompson). The concentrate specifications of the smelters (5 to 15 % Ni, 0.3 to 12 % Cu, 0.1 to 0.5 % Co in the lecture's table) are the target.

Rönnbäcken, the geology lesson's Nickel Mountain, is the exception that proves the rule: a low-grade ultramafic ore whose nickel sits in heazlewoodite and millerite with no iron sulphide at all, so the concentrate can run at 25 to 30 % Ni, and magnetite and magnesia are by-products. Talvivaara is the other end: a black-schist sulphide ore too lean for any concentrator, bioleached on heaps, with copper, zinc and uranium as by-products.

3. The laterite route: no mill, only a furnace or an autoclave

A laterite cannot be concentrated: the nickel is not in discrete mineral grains that can be liberated, it sits in the lattice of goethite or of the serpentine-group silicates. The only physical upgrading is to reject selected coarse fractions during ore preparation: the New Caledonian Tritout screen, a heated revolving screen that scrubs and sorts the sticky ore, lifting the nickel grade of the undersize from 1.65 to 2.70 % at 82 % recovery; the Ravensthorpe flowsheet of the lecture (scrubbers, screens, hydrocyclones, spiral classifiers) that takes 1 % Ni ore to 2 % on the rule that small, soft, low-density particles are richer in nickel than large dense ones. After that the ore goes to metallurgy at almost run-of-mine grade, and the moisture (25 to 40 %) and the high magnesia decide which:

  • Saprolite (low iron, high MgO) goes the pyrometallurgical way: drying in a rotary kiln, reduction with coal in a rotary kiln (the RKEF route, rotary kiln and electric furnace), electric-furnace smelting to ferronickel (15 to 45 % Ni, the rest iron) or, if sulphur is added, to a nickel matte, then converting; Société Le Nickel's Doniambo flowsheets in the compendium, with the sintering grates, blast furnaces and Bessemer converters of the older practice, and the nickel oxide "rondelles" reduced in vertical retorts at Le Havre.
  • Limonite (high iron, low MgO) goes the hydrometallurgical way: high-pressure acid leaching (HPAL) in titanium-lined autoclaves at about 255 °C and 44 bar with 400 kg of sulphuric acid per tonne of ore, counter-current decantation washing, neutralisation, precipitation of a mixed nickel-cobalt sulphide with hydrogen sulphide at pH 2.5, oxygen re-leach of that sulphide, solvent extraction to part cobalt from nickel (Cyanex 272), and hydrogen reduction of the ammoniacal solutions to nickel and cobalt powders and briquettes: the Sherritt process as run at Murrin Murrin and Moa Bay, and the lecture's process chain. Ammonium sulphate is the by-product. Heap leaching and atmospheric leaching of laterites are the newer, less proven options.

The compendium's older text and the 2009 paper agree on the economics: pyrometallurgy of laterites is energy-hungry (drying 30 % moisture, no exothermic sulphide reactions to help), which is why the oil shock of the 1970s pushed the industry back to sulphides, and why HPAL, with its two to three billion dollar plants and its teething troubles, is only now the main growth route.

4. Chromium: one mineral, dense medium and gravity

Chromium has one commercial mineral, chromite FeCr2O4, 46.5 % Cr, density 4.5 to 5.1, and Europe's largest chromium mine is Kemi in Finland, the source of the ferrochrome for Outokumpu's stainless steel. The Kemi flowsheet in the lecture is a gravity plant from end to end: crushing, dense-medium separation that makes a lumpy product and rejects the light rock, then a concentrating section of rod and ball mills, screens, cyclones, and banks of spirals with high-gradient magnetic separators, thickeners and drum filters producing a metallurgical-grade concentrate. Chromite's density contrast against silicate gangue and its weak magnetism make it a gravity and high-intensity magnetic problem, never a flotation one.

5. Manganese and molybdenum: one dug and washed, the other floats itself

Manganese has three major minerals, pyrolusite MnO2 (63.2 % Mn, density 4.4 to 5.0), manganite MnO(OH) (62.5 %) and rhodochrosite MnCO3 (47.8 %, density 3.7), in sedimentary ores (Russia), weathered ores (South Africa, Brazil) and, one day, sea-floor nodules. The ICOMI flowsheet of the lecture is the weathered-ore plant: jaw and cone crushing, screening into lump and blast-furnace sizes at 48 % Mn, rotary scrubbing to wash the clay off, rake classifiers and cyclones for the sands, Humphrey's spirals and a Dyna-Whirlpool dense-medium separator for the fines, and a pelletising plant for the minus 20 mesh product at 55 % Mn. Molybdenum has one mineral, molybdenite MoS2, 59.9 % Mo, density 5.5, a layered sulphide whose cleavage faces are non-polar: it is naturally hydrophobic and floats with frother and a little fuel oil, which the copper lesson used for the copper-molybdenum separation. Pure molybdenite ores in quartz veins exist (the Hörken mine near Ludvika) but most molybdenum is a by-product of porphyry copper.

6. Tungsten: two minerals, two methods

Tungsten comes in two (really four) minerals, and the exam's question turns on the difference:

MineralFormulaDensity% WSeparated by
ScheeliteCaWO45.9 to 6.163.9 (80.5 % WO3)Gravity and flotation (a calcium mineral, fatty-acid collector)
Wolframite: ferberiteFeWO47.4 to 7.560.5Gravity and wet high-intensity magnetic separation
Wolframite: hübneriteMnWO47.1 to 7.260.7Same; paramagnetic through its iron and manganese

Wolframite is very dense and weakly magnetic, so it is concentrated by gravity (jigs, tables, spirals) and finished by wet high-intensity magnetic separation (WHIMS), which parts it from the equally dense but non-magnetic cassiterite it often comes with. Scheelite is a calcium tungstate, not magnetic, dense enough for gravity in the coarse sizes but usually fine-grained, so it is floated like the other calcium minerals, with fatty acids or soaps at about pH 9.5 after the sulphides have been floated off, with sodium silicate to depress the calcium gangue (calcite, fluorite, apatite) and the Petrov process (a hot conditioning with a large silicate dose) to clean the rough concentrate to more than 65 % WO3. The lecture's flowsheets show both: a scheelite plant that floats the sulphides first with soda, xanthate and sodium silicate, splits them into copper and molybdenum concentrates, then floats scheelite with sodium silicate and soap and finishes it chemically (autoclave digestion with soda, synthetic scheelite precipitated with calcium chloride, and an ammonium paratungstate plant); and a mixed ore in which scheelite, wolframite and cassiterite are floated together at pH 9.5 with 400 g/t of collector and then parted by gravity into a tungsten concentrate over 60 % WO3 and a fluorite by-product over 93 % CaF2, with the scheelite-wolframite split done by high-gradient magnetic separation. The lecture's table of newer collectors, hydroxamic acids and amine mixtures, is the research front.

In plain words: if the tungsten mineral has iron in it, use a magnet; if it is calcium tungstate, float it as a calcium mineral and keep the other calcium minerals down with silicate.

7. Vanadium and titanium: metals that live inside other minerals

Vanadium has minerals of its own (patronite, coulsonite, montroseite, and the uranium mineral carnotite at 11 % V) but is mostly won from what it substitutes into: the magnetite and ilmenite of the titano-iron ores (the iron lesson's lamellae) and from fly ash; once a steel additive, now a battery metal. Titanium has two minerals, rutile TiO2 (59.9 % Ti, density 4.25) and ilmenite FeO⋅TiO2 (31.6 % Ti, density 4.72), in placer deposits (the mineral sands), in eclogite hard rock (rutile at Engebøfjellet) and in hard-rock ilmenite (Titania A/S in Norway). The mineral-sands flowsheet of the lecture is gravity from the mining unit onward: hydrocyclones to reject the clay fines, a constant-density tank, rougher, cleaner, recleaner and scavenger spirals with middlings recycled, an upgrade spiral, and a heavy-mineral concentrate to a dry mill where magnetic and high-tension (electrostatic) separators part ilmenite (magnetic, conducting), rutile (non-magnetic, conducting), zircon and monazite (non-conducting).

8. Tin: the classic gravity ore, and the modern ways to save its fines

Tin's minerals are cassiterite SnO2, 78.7 % Sn, density 6.8 to 7.0, and the sulphide stannite Cu2FeSnS4 (27.6 % Sn); its ores are placers (Malaysia, Indonesia, mined by dredging, the lecture's photographs) and granite-related lode ores (Cornwall, Bolivia, Australia). Cassiterite is dense, non-magnetic, non-conducting and brittle, so tin dressing is gravity with a slimes problem. The placer plant of the lecture is a ladder of Reichert cones (roughers, cleaners, scavengers, recleaners, then spirals), and the dredge concentrate is finished in a dry mill of magnetic and high-tension separators like the mineral sands. The lode plants (Wheal Jane, Renison) add what gravity cannot do: sulphide flotation to remove pyrite and arsenopyrite, magnetic separation of the iron-bearing minerals, and cassiterite flotation of the slimes with the special collectors of the lecture's table (iso-hexyl phosphonic acid, p-tolyl arsonic acid, sulphosuccinamates at pH 2.5 to 5), because cassiterite ground below about 20 µm is lost from every gravity device. The South African Rooiberg flowsheet strings them together: crushing and screening, heavy-medium separation, a rod mill, classification, gravity concentrators for the sands (jigs, spirals, tables) and, for the deslimed fines, magnetic separation, pyrite flotation and cassiterite flotation. World production is 230 kt a year, China 100 kt, Indonesia 41, Peru 29, Bolivia 20; reserves 4.9 Mt, a third of them Chinese.

9. Niobium and tantalum: coltan, and the flotation of pyrochlore

Niobium (once "columbium") and tantalum sit together in columbite-tantalite, (Fe,Mn)(Nb,Ta)2O6, the "coltan" of the Congo and Rwanda, a conflict mineral, and in pyrochlore, the niobium mineral of the carbonatites that supply 91 % of the world's niobium from Brazil (Araxá, Catalão) and 8 % from Canada (Niobec). Coltan is dense and is won by wet gravity from placers and pegmatites, then refined by hydro- and pyrometallurgy. Pyrochlore is floated: the lecture's table of plant practice shows amine collectors (120 to 530 g/t), fluosilicate or hydrofluoric acid as activators, oxalic acid or hydrochloric acid as pH modifiers to pH 2.5 to 4 in the cleaners, and silicate depressants, to concentrates of 50 to 60 % Nb2O5; the Araxá and Niobec flowsheets add desliming, carbonate flotation, magnetic separation and a leach before the final niobium cleaners.

Worked example

Choosing the nickel route from the assay. A laterite profile gives a limonite sample at 1.3 % Ni, 45 % Fe, 0.5 % MgO, and a saprolite sample at 2.2 % Ni, 12 % Fe, 25 % MgO. Neither can be concentrated (no nickel mineral to liberate, idea 3). The saprolite's magnesia would consume acid by the tonne, so it goes to the kiln and the electric furnace: with the lecture's typical reduction, its 2.2 % Ni ends up in a ferronickel at, say, 25 % Ni, so one tonne of dry ore yields about 22/0.25=88 kg of ferronickel, and drying 30 % moisture first costs the fuel that makes laterite smelting the most energy-intensive step in nickel. The limonite's iron is goethite that sulphuric acid hardly touches at 255 °C, so it goes to HPAL: 400 kg of acid per tonne of ore for 13 kg of nickel, or 31 kg of acid per kg of nickel, which is why the acid plant burning imported sulphur is the biggest unit on a laterite site and why the sulphide route, whose concentrate carries its own fuel into the flash furnace, kept the industry for a century.

Why wolframite needs a magnet and scheelite does not. Wolframite at density 7.4 and cassiterite at 7.0 cannot be parted by gravity at any practical size, and both are non-conducting; only wolframite's iron and manganese give it a magnetic susceptibility that a wet high-intensity separator can use. Scheelite at 5.9 could be parted from cassiterite by gravity in coarse sizes, but its fine intergrowth and its calcium chemistry make flotation the practical route, and calcite (2.7), fluorite (3.2) and apatite (3.2) float with the same fatty acid, hence the silicate depressant and the Petrov cleaning.

How the exam asks it

Topic T7 collects the questions on the metals outside copper, lead-zinc, iron and gold. The 2014 paper asked how tungsten ores are beneficiated: idea 6 in full, two minerals, two properties, the flowsheet with the sulphide pre-float and the silicate depressant. The 2015 retake asked about nickel: the sulphide and laterite families with their minerals, why the laterites cannot be concentrated, and the two metallurgical routes (ideas 1 to 3), with Talvivaara's bioleaching as the newer option. The 2014 paper's question on pyrrhotite against the disulphides is answered in the copper lesson. The 2020 and 2022 papers asked true-or-false statements about cassiterite and tantalum: the density, magnetism and conductivity facts of ideas 8 and 9 (cassiterite is dense, non-magnetic and non-conducting; its slimes need flotation; coltan is won by gravity). A six-point answer names the mineral, the property that separates it, the machine that uses the property, and one plant.
