What the exam wants · T6 · asked in 2014, 2015, 2020, 2022

Iron ores

  1. Two ore types dominate: magnetite (strongly magnetic, low-intensity magnetic separation, wet LIMS drums) and hematite (weakly magnetic, needs high-intensity separation, gravity or flotation).
  2. Magnetite flowsheets: AG grinding, cobbing with LIMS, several LIMS stages, sometimes apatite flotation to remove phosphorus (Kiruna).
  3. Hematite flowsheets: gravity (spirals, jigs), WHIMS or SLon pulsating high-gradient magnetic separators, reverse cationic flotation of quartz.
  4. Slon: a vertical-ring pulsating high-gradient wet magnetic separator; the pulsation keeps the matrix from clogging, so it treats fine weakly magnetic minerals (hematite, limonite, ilmenite) with good recovery.
  5. True/false items test these facts: hematite versus magnetite responses to gravity, LIMS and flotation, and where sinter fines and pellet feed come from.

2014-12-22 · question 4

Asks for the major iron ore types mined today, their process mineralogy and the prevalent concentration method of each.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Three parts: the ore types, what their mineralogy means for processing, and the method each gets. One row per type is the clearest answer.

Ore typeProcess mineralogyPrevalent concentration method
Magnetite ores (Kiruna, Malmberget, Kaunisvaara, Sydvaranger)Magnetite, strongly ferromagnetic, 72 % Fe when pure; gangue of quartz, apatite (phosphorus), silicates; often fine-grained, needs grinding to 40 to 100 µm for liberationWet low-intensity magnetic separation (LIMS drums) in several stages, after autogenous grinding; dry LIMS cobbing on coarse material; apatite removed by flotation where phosphorus is high
Hematite ores (Hamersley and the other Australian and Brazilian ores)Hematite, weakly magnetic (antiferromagnetic), 70 % Fe when pure; often coarse and high grade; gangue quartz, claysHigh-grade ores: crushing and screening only (direct-shipping lump and fines). Lower grades: gravity (spirals, jigs, dense media), high-intensity magnetic separation (WHIMS, SLon), reverse cationic flotation of quartz
Banded iron formations / itabirites (fine hematite and magnetite in quartz bands)Fine-grained, needs fine grinding, mixed magnetite and hematiteGrinding, desliming, flotation (reverse cationic with amine, starch depressing the iron oxides) or LIMS plus WHIMS in series
Goethite and limonite ores (hydrated oxides, laterites, "brown ores")Porous, low iron content and high loss on ignition, weakly magneticWashing and desliming, gravity, high-intensity magnetic separation; often blended rather than concentrated
Titanomagnetite and other special oresMagnetite with titanium in the lattice or as ilmeniteLIMS for the magnetite, then separate treatment of the ilmenite

The two words that organise everything: magnetite → low-intensity magnetic; hematite → gravity, high-intensity magnetic or flotation. Grain size decides the grind and whether flotation is needed; the gangue decides the extra step (apatite flotation for phosphorus, desliming for clays).

Exam Sp 1, 2015-10-26 · Ex. 5 · 6 p · T6 · magnetite, hematite, Slon · module 10

2015-10-26 · question 5

Six true-or-false statements about iron ores: hematite and magnetite in gravity separation, oleate flotation of hematite, autogenous crushing of fine-grained ores, wet LIMS for hematite, electrostatic separation of hematite, and where sinter fines come from.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Six statements, one point each, and the reason is what makes the point safe.

StatementAnswerWhy
aHematite may be separated from magnetite by wet gravity separation.FalseTheir densities are almost the same (hematite 5.26, magnetite 5.18 g/cm³): no density contrast to work with. They are separated magnetically: low-intensity separation takes the magnetite, hematite stays.
bHematite can be floated with oleate.TrueDirect anionic flotation of hematite with fatty acids (oleate, tall oil) is a classic route; the alternative is reverse cationic flotation, where quartz is floated with an amine and hematite depressed with starch.
cAutogenous crushing is suitable for fine-grained iron ores with strong grain boundaries.FalseAutogenous breakage works by abrasion along grain boundaries; strong boundaries and fine grains give little liberation that way, and the ore behaves like a homogeneous rock. Such ores need fine grinding with media or a different route.
dWet low-intensity magnetic separators are used for concentration of hematite.FalseLIMS works on ferromagnetic magnetite. Hematite is only weakly magnetic and needs high-intensity or high-gradient separators (WHIMS, SLon), gravity or flotation.
eElectrodynamic/electrostatic separators have been used to separate hematite from gangue.TrueHematite is a semiconductor and quartz an insulator, so high-tension electrostatic separation of dry, sized hematite ores has been practised (and is used for ilmenite-rutile-zircon sands on the same principle).
fSinter fines are won in the last stage of the concentration process.FalseSinter fines are the fine fraction (below about 6 to 8 mm) from crushing and screening of direct-shipping ore or coarse concentrates, sold as a product for the sinter plant. The last stage of a concentration plant gives the fine pellet-feed concentrate, not sinter fines.
Why

The theme behind the six: hematite and magnetite look alike on a density basis but not on a magnetic or surface-chemical basis, so the separation methods differ. Module 10 (SC-Iron ores.pdf and OH_IronOres.pdf) is the source; the 2014 retake (variant A of this topic) is the long-form version of the same knowledge.

Retake Sp 1, 2015-12-22 · Ex. 6 · 6 p · T6 · magnetite, hematite, Slon · module 10

2015-12-22 · question 6

Asks for methods to concentrate iron ores in which hematite is the main iron mineral.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

Hematite is weakly magnetic, so the magnetite tricks do not work. Three families of methods, plus the two that prepare the ore.

Preparation. Crushing and screening: high-grade hematite ore is sold as lump (6 to 30 mm) and fines directly, without concentration. Washing and desliming with hydrocyclones removes clays and ultrafines that would spoil every later step. Grinding only to the liberation size, which for coarse hematite can be several hundred micrometres.

1. Gravity separation. Hematite (5.26 g/cm³) against quartz (2.65): a large density contrast. Jigs for the coarse fractions, spirals for 0.1 to 1 mm sand-sized material (the workhorse of Australian and Brazilian plants), shaking tables and Reichert cones for fine sand, dense-medium separation for lump. Cheap, no reagents; loses the fines.

2. High-intensity and high-gradient magnetic separation. Wet high-intensity magnetic separators (WHIMS) and the pulsating high-gradient SLon machines create fields and gradients strong enough to pull weakly magnetic hematite (and goethite) out of the gangue, down to a few tens of micrometres. Used as a stage after gravity or after desliming to recover the fines.

3. Flotation. For the fine fraction and for itabirite-type ores: reverse cationic flotation (an amine floats the quartz, starch depresses the iron oxides, pH around 10) is the industrial standard; direct anionic flotation with fatty acids (oleate) floats the hematite itself, less selective in the presence of slimes.

4. Magnetising roasting. Reduce the hematite to magnetite in a roaster (or by a reducing gas), then use the cheap low-intensity magnetic separation. Energy-intensive, so used only where the other routes fail.

The typical plant stacks them: crushing, screening, desliming, spirals for the sand, WHIMS or SLon and reverse flotation for the fines, with the slimes to tailings.

Why

Compare with a magnetite plant (Kiruna: autogenous grinding and LIMS drums, one method for everything). Hematite needs the combination because no single property separates it cheaply at all sizes: density works coarse, magnetism medium, flotation fine. Module 10, especially the Hamersley and Kaunisvaara appendices, shows the real flowsheets; the Slon question (variants D and E) is the magnetic part in detail.

Written exam, 2020-10-26 · Ex. 4 · 6 p · T6 · magnetite, hematite, Slon · module 10

2020-10-26 · question 4

Asks what a Slon separator is and what its advantages are.

The paper's own wording is on the PC copy of Malla; the exam papers belong to LTU.

Answer, step by step (Malla)

What SLon is. A vertical-ring, pulsating, high-gradient wet magnetic separator, developed in China (Ganzhou) and now standard for fine, weakly magnetic minerals. Its parts:

  • A vertical ring that carries a matrix of magnetic rods (or expanded metal) through a strong electromagnet's field (up to about 1 to 1.3 T at the poles, with very high field gradients at the rod surfaces).
  • The pulp is fed from above into the matrix inside the magnetic zone; weakly magnetic particles (hematite, limonite, ilmenite, chromite, wolframite) are trapped on the rods, non-magnetics pass through and leave as tailings at the bottom.
  • A pulsating mechanism (a diaphragm) makes the pulp in the matrix oscillate up and down, so the trapped bed is loosened continuously and the mechanically entrapped gangue is washed out while the magnetics stay held.
  • As the ring rotates out of the field, the magnetic product is flushed off with water from the top into a launder, and the clean matrix rotates back into the feed zone.

Its advantages.

  1. High recovery of fine weakly magnetic minerals (down to 10 to 20 µm) thanks to the high gradient at the rods.
  2. High concentrate grade, because the pulsation and the top-flush remove the gangue that a static matrix would hold by entrapment.
  3. The matrix does not clog: vertical ring, feed from the top, flushing in the opposite direction, pulsation; no blocking by coarse particles or slimes, which was the weakness of the older horizontal-ring WHIMS.
  4. Low operating cost and simple operation: low water and energy per tonne, few moving parts, long matrix life, continuous operation.
  5. Large capacity in one unit and a wide feed size range (about 1 mm to 10 µm).

Where it is used: hematite and limonite ores (upgrading the fines after spirals), ilmenite from beach sands, removing iron from feldspar and quartz, wolframite and chromite fines.

Why

Why the examiner likes Slon: it solved the one thing that kept fine hematite in the tailings, entrapment and clogging in high-gradient matrices. Say "pulsation plus vertical ring" and what each does; that is the essence. Module 10, SC-Iron ores.pdf and OH_IronOres.pdf, is the source; the same question appears in the 2022 paper.
