What this session is

Friday 4 September, 13:00 to 14:30, room F433. TimeEdit calls it a Lesson; the study plan lists it as the third session of week 36: PC-lesson, King chapter 5.1 to 5.6, crushing and crusher models. The chapter is worked through together with MODSIM, so bring the laptop with MODSIM running (decimal point set, work folder set) or be ready to pair up.

The Canvas page that backs it is 2.4 Crushing: breakage and crusher models. The lecture slides Crushing (PDF) live on that page.

Three ideas to arrive with

  1. Small particles are relatively stronger. Brittle rock fails in tension, and cracks are where stress concentrates. A small particle contains fewer cracks, so the energy needed to break it rises steeply as size falls. King's Table 5.1 gives median fracture energies E50 per ore type; the relation has a floor E∞ and grows as (dp − dp,min) shrinks. Consequence: fines are expensive to make, and crushing stops making sense where grinding starts.
  1. The breakage function describes the children, not the parent. B(x; y) is the cumulative size distribution of fragments from a parent of size y. Three forms appear in the course: the Austin form (a fine part plus a coarse part), truncated distributions for crushers (nothing can be coarser than the parent), and the t10 family, where one number anchors the whole fragment curve. Rapid strikes give a few large fragments (cleavage); slow compression gives many small ones; attrition only chips surfaces.
  1. A crusher is a cycle, not a single break. Primary crushers (jaw, gyratory) set their product mainly by the open-side setting, OSS. Secondary and tertiary cone crushers run a crushing cycle: a fraction of the material is selected for breakage (the internal classification function), broken by the breakage function, and re-enters the cycle until it escapes through the closed-side setting, CSS. That selection-then-breakage structure is exactly MODSIM's standard crusher model (CRSH), and it comes back in the grinding module.

Two questions to keep in mind

  • In Exercise 3 you raise the feed rate by 50 % and watch the product. Which crusher models react to throughput at all? (Hint: the lecture's last slide says JAW1, JAW2 and GYRA depend on OSS and material type only, and none of the crusher models carries a capacity term.)
  • Energy: Rittinger, Bond and Kick are the same differential relation with different exponents. Which one does Exercise 4 ask you to use, and what does its work index Wi = 12 kWh/t buy you?

Five terms

TermIn one line
OSS / CSSOpen- and closed-side setting: the widest and narrowest gap of the crushing chamber; primary product follows OSS, secondary product follows CSS
Breakage function B(x; y)Cumulative fragment size distribution from a parent of size y
t10Percent of fragments finer than one tenth of the parent size; one number that anchors the fragment curve
Internal classificationThe selection step of the crushing cycle: which particles get broken in each pass
Work index WiBond's material constant, kWh/t, the workhorse for first energy estimates

Where it lands in the assignment

Assignment 1 (crusher station, 25 pts, released week 37, seminar Friday 18 September) has two parts: simple crusher models first, then advanced models with crusher sizes chosen from King's Tables 5.4 to 5.7, checking that each machine can take its feed within the manufacturer's capacity and power. Friday's lesson is where those advanced models get names: JAW1, JAW2, GYRA, EMJC, CRS1, CRSH, SHHD.

Before Friday, if you have ten more minutes: open BreakageModels.xls from page 2.4 and move the t10 slider. Watching the fragment curve respond once is worth a page of reading.
