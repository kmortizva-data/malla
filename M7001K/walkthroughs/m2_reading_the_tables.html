What the table gives you

Every crusher and screen in this course is checked against a table that somebody else measured. The tables look dense because they answer three questions at once, and the trick is to ask them one at a time. This page is the slow version of "How to check overloading" in the Assignment 1 guide: which table, which number on the left, which column, which cell, and what to do with it.

The four crusher tables of King, chapter 5 (Canvas: Tables5.4-5.7.pdf; legibly, pages 152 to 155 of the e-book on the PC copy):

Two things the tables do not say and you must know: a jaw or a gyratory only swallows lumps up to about 80 % of its gape (King, section 5.6.1), and a blank cell means the machine is not offered at that setting, not that its capacity is zero.

Cone tables: what fine, medium, coarse and extra coarse mean

A cone crusher is a steel cone, the mantle, that wobbles inside a steel bowl. Rock enters at the top, between the two, and is squeezed as it slides down; the gap at the bottom when the mantle is closest to the bowl is the closed-side setting (CSS). The cavity is the shape of that space, and it comes from the pair of wear liners bolted inside. The same machine, same size and same motor, is sold with different liners:

  • Fine: a narrow mouth at the top, so only small lumps get in, but it can close to a small setting.
  • Extra coarse: a wide mouth, so big lumps get in, but it cannot close as far, so its first columns are blank.
  • Medium and coarse: in between.

That is why each size has several rows, why the feed opening grows down each block, and why the coarse rows start further to the right. The feed opening is measured on the closed side with the machine at its smallest setting: a lump wider than it does not get in. The 3 ft, whose three cavities share one 56 kW motor:

3 ft (56 kW)Feed opening, mmCSS 9CSS 13CSS 19CSS 25
Fine83455981blank

So a row is a size and a cavity, and the cavity is chosen by the largest lump before any capacity is read. With 80 t/h of lumps up to 100 mm at CSS 19, the Fine row shows 81 t/h, more than enough, but its 83 mm mouth cannot take a 100 mm lump: the machine is the 3 ft Coarse, 91 t/h.

The mesh table (Canvas, module 2: ScreenMesh.pdf, "Dimensions of standard wire mesh screens"): the left number is the opening in inches; there are four column groups for the weight of the wire (medium light, medium, medium heavy, heavy), each giving the wire diameter (one page in inches, one in mm) and the per cent open area. It is where the Karra screen model's wire diameter comes from.

Step by step

For a crusher, with the three numbers of its fly-out in front of you: tonnage in t/h, largest size in the feed in mm, and the setting you typed (OSS for a jaw or a gyratory, CSS for a cone).

  1. Open the right table. Jaw → 5.4. Gyratory → 5.5. Standard cone (a secondary) → 5.6. Short head (a tertiary) → 5.7. If you are not sure which cone you meant, the model tells you: CRS1 and CRSH are standard cones, SHHD is a short head.
  2. Cross out the rows that cannot swallow the feed. Jaw or gyratory: multiply the gape (the first of the two left numbers, in inches × 25.4 for mm) by 0.8 and compare with the largest lump; if the lump is bigger, the row is out. Cone: compare the feed opening on the closed side with the largest lump; if the lump is bigger, the row is out. Do this before reading any capacity, because a machine that chokes on its feed has no capacity.
  3. Find the column of your setting. The column headers are the settings in mm. If your setting is not a column, take the nearer column on the small side (a smaller setting gives a smaller capacity, so the check stays on the safe side) and say so in the report. For a cone, also look at the minimum CSS of the row: if your setting is below it, the row is out. Table 5.7 prints that minimum in its own column; Table 5.6 does not, and there the minimum of a row is its first column with a number.
  4. Go down the column. Among the rows that survived step 2, take the first one whose number is at least your tonnage. That row is your machine: write down its size, its cavity, its motor power and the capacity you read. A blank cell in your column means that row does not run at your setting: skip it.
  5. Check the power. The unit report of JAW1, CRSH or SHHD estimates kilowatts from the work index. Compare with the motor of the row. A model estimate above the motor means the machine is too small for the duty, or the work index is too high for this rock; either way it is a sentence for the report.
  6. If no row reaches your tonnage, the crusher is overloaded. Three honest answers: open the setting (and accept a coarser product), send it less (the splitters and the screen apertures upstream), or put two machines in parallel behind a splitter. Never a bigger number in the fly-out.
  7. If the first row that reaches you is far bigger than you need, say so: a smaller machine at a wider setting may be the better design, and the seminar likes that sentence.

For a screen deck, with its aperture in mm:

  1. Convert the aperture to inches: mm ÷ 25.4. The rows of the mesh table are in inches.
  2. Find the nearest row on the left. 10 mm is 0.39 in: the 3/8 in row (9.5 mm) is nearer than 7/16 in (11.1 mm).
  3. Pick the wire weight. Medium unless the sheet or the group decides otherwise: a lighter wire gives more open area and less life, a heavier one the reverse. Write the choice in the assumption list.
  4. Read the two numbers of the cell: wire diameter (use the mm page) and per cent open area.
  5. Type them in SCR2 or DSC2: mesh size h is the aperture in metres (0.0095 for the table's own opening, 0.01 if you keep the round number), wire diameter d in metres (0.0038). Karra computes the open area from these two, so the table's percentage is your check, not an input.

Worked readings

Three readings drawn out, with the row and the column marked, and after them three readings of Table 5.6 to practise on your own. The numbers of the examples (45 t/h, 250 t/h, 10 mm, and the three practice duties) are illustrative; yours come from your own fly-outs.

Table 5.6, three readings to practise

Each title below is a duty: tonnes per hour, the largest lump and the setting. Find the machine yourself first, in the order of the steps above (mouth, column, first row that covers the tonnage, power), on the full table: the fold just below on the PC copy, or Tables5.4-5.7.pdf in Canvas. Then open the fold and compare. In the drawings, grey rows are the ones the lump rules out.

Practice 1: 150 t/h, largest lump 170 mm, CSS 25 mm. Which standard cone?

Above the picture, the 2 ft and 3 ft rows are out at step 1: their widest opening is 163 mm. Power closes the check: 93 kW is the motor of every 4 ft, whatever the cavity, and it is the number to compare with the kilowatts the CRSH report estimates.

Practice 2: 100 t/h, largest lump 90 mm, CSS 13 mm. Which standard cone?

Above the picture, the 2 ft Extra coarse (opening 100 mm) fits the lump but gives 23 t/h at 13 mm. The lesson of this reading: a small setting pushes the answer to a fine cavity of a bigger size, because the coarse cavities stop further to the right. The motor to compare is 112 kW.

Practice 3: 400 t/h, largest lump 230 mm, CSS 38 mm. Which standard cone?

Other honest answers from the same column: the 5½ ft Extra coarse, 431 t/h and loaded at 93 %, or two 4½ ft Extra coarse in parallel behind a splitter, 2 × 303 = 606 t/h and 66 % each. Which one is a design decision; the report says which and why. The 7 ft motor is 224 kW, and 260 kW in the extra heavy duty (EHD) version.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the biggest number in the row instead of the number in the column of your setting: the biggest number is the capacity at the widest setting, which makes a product you did not design for.
  • Mixing the two settings: jaw and gyratory tables are by open-side setting and cone tables by closed-side setting. The cycle models CRSH and SHHD ask for the closed side on a cone, but CRSH reads the same field as the open side when it runs on a jaw or a gyratory, even though its window still calls it closed-side.
  • Inches and millimetres in the same breath: the sizes on the left are inches (and feet for cones), the settings and the feed openings are millimetres. Convert before comparing with your fly-out.
  • Skipping the feed check: a row that cannot swallow the largest lump has no capacity, whatever its column says. Jaws and gyratories take about 0.8 × gape; cones what the feed-opening column says.
  • Reading a blank cell as zero, or as "any setting is fine". Blank means the machine is not offered at that setting; for cones the minimum CSS says why (a column of its own in Table 5.7, the first filled column of the row in Table 5.6).
  • Taking a row because its number covers the tonnage before checking its mouth. In Table 5.6 a fine cavity often shows a big enough number at your setting; if the largest lump is wider than its feed opening, the lump does not get in and the number does not count.
  • Reading the cavities as different machines. Fine, medium, coarse and extra coarse are the same size and the same motor with different liners: the kilowatts belong to the size, and the cavity is chosen by the lump.
  • Taking short tons for tonnes: King's tables are in tonnes per hour, which is what MODSIM's fly-outs show when the units are set to t/h.
  • Choosing a machine three or five times larger than the tonnage without saying so, when a smaller one at a wider setting would do; and the reverse, a machine that "just" makes it with no margin for the surges of a real plant.
  • For screens, typing the table's per cent open area somewhere: Karra wants the aperture and the wire diameter; the open area is what you check against.
  • Comparing the wrong opening. The crusher sheet asks for the open-side feed opening; Table 5.7 gives both sides for the short heads (use the open one), Table 5.6 gives only the closed side for the standard cones, which is the safe side of the comparison.
  • Forgetting that the setting must have a machine: a column with a blank cell for every size of the right class means the setting is not recommended; change the setting or the machine class, not the number in the fly-out.
