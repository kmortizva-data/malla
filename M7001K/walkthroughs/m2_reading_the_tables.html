What the table gives you

Every crusher and screen in this course is checked against a table that somebody else measured. The tables look dense because they answer three questions at once, and the trick is to ask them one at a time. This page is the slow version of "How to check overloading" in the Assignment 1 guide: which table, which number on the left, which column, which cell, and what to do with it.

The four crusher tables of King, chapter 5 (Canvas: Tables5.4-5.7.pdf; legibly, pages 152 to 155 of the e-book on the PC copy):

TableMachine, and the number on the leftColumnsThe cell
5.4Jaw crushers. The left number is the feed opening, gape × length in inches (30 × 42 is a jaw whose mouth is 30 inches deep and 42 wide); the motor kW is next to itOpen-side setting, OSS, in mmCapacity in tonnes per hour
5.5Gyratory crushers. The left number is gape × lower mantle diameter in inches; motor kW next to itOpen-side setting, mmTonnes per hour
5.6Standard cone crushers. The left number is the head diameter in feet with the maximum power in brackets, then the cavity (fine, medium, coarse, extra coarse), then the feed opening on the closed side in mm with the minimum CSSClosed-side setting, CSS, in mmTonnes per hour
5.7Short-head cone crushers. Same as 5.6, with a column for the recommended minimum CSS and the feed opening given for both the closed and the open sideClosed-side setting, mmTonnes per hour

Two things the tables do not say and you must know: a jaw or a gyratory only swallows lumps up to about 80 % of its gape (King, section 5.6.1), and a blank cell means the machine is not offered at that setting, not that its capacity is zero.

The mesh table (Canvas, module 2: ScreenMesh.pdf, "Dimensions of standard wire mesh screens"): the left number is the opening in inches; there are four column groups for the weight of the wire (medium light, medium, medium heavy, heavy), each giving the wire diameter (one page in inches, one in mm) and the per cent open area. It is where the Karra screen model's wire diameter comes from.

Step by step

For a crusher, with the three numbers of its fly-out in front of you: tonnage in t/h, largest size in the feed in mm, and the setting you typed (OSS for a jaw or a gyratory, CSS for a cone).

  1. Open the right table. Jaw → 5.4. Gyratory → 5.5. Standard cone (a secondary) → 5.6. Short head (a tertiary) → 5.7. If you are not sure which cone you meant, the model tells you: CRS1 and CRSH are standard cones, SHHD is a short head.
  2. Cross out the rows that cannot swallow the feed. Jaw or gyratory: multiply the gape (the first of the two left numbers, in inches × 25.4 for mm) by 0.8 and compare with the largest lump; if the lump is bigger, the row is out. Cone: compare the feed opening on the closed side with the largest lump; if the lump is bigger, the row is out. Do this before reading any capacity, because a machine that chokes on its feed has no capacity.
  3. Find the column of your setting. The column headers are the settings in mm. If your setting is not a column, take the nearer column on the small side (a smaller setting gives a smaller capacity, so the check stays on the safe side) and say so in the report. For a cone, also look at the minimum CSS of the row: if your setting is below it, the row is out.
  4. Go down the column. Among the rows that survived step 2, take the first one whose number is at least your tonnage. That row is your machine: write down its size, its cavity, its motor power and the capacity you read. A blank cell in your column means that row does not run at your setting: skip it.
  5. Check the power. The unit report of JAW1, CRSH or SHHD estimates kilowatts from the work index. Compare with the motor of the row. A model estimate above the motor means the machine is too small for the duty, or the work index is too high for this rock; either way it is a sentence for the report.
  6. If no row reaches your tonnage, the crusher is overloaded. Three honest answers: open the setting (and accept a coarser product), send it less (the splitters and the screen apertures upstream), or put two machines in parallel behind a splitter. Never a bigger number in the fly-out.
  7. If the first row that reaches you is far bigger than you need, say so: a smaller machine at a wider setting may be the better design, and the seminar likes that sentence.

For a screen deck, with its aperture in mm:

  1. Convert the aperture to inches: mm ÷ 25.4. The rows of the mesh table are in inches.
  2. Find the nearest row on the left. 10 mm is 0.39 in: the 3/8 in row (9.5 mm) is nearer than 7/16 in (11.1 mm).
  3. Pick the wire weight. Medium unless the sheet or the group decides otherwise: a lighter wire gives more open area and less life, a heavier one the reverse. Write the choice in the assumption list.
  4. Read the two numbers of the cell: wire diameter (use the mm page) and per cent open area.
  5. Type them in SCR2 or DSC2: mesh size h is the aperture in metres (0.0095 for the table's own opening, 0.01 if you keep the round number), wire diameter d in metres (0.0038). Karra computes the open area from these two, so the table's percentage is your check, not an input.

Worked readings

Three readings drawn out, with the row and the column marked. The numbers of the examples (45 t/h, 250 t/h, 10 mm) are illustrative; yours come from your own fly-outs.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the biggest number in the row instead of the number in the column of your setting: the biggest number is the capacity at the widest setting, which makes a product you did not design for.
  • Mixing the two settings: jaw and gyratory tables are by open-side setting, cone tables by closed-side setting, and the cycle models CRSH and SHHD ask for the closed side.
  • Inches and millimetres in the same breath: the sizes on the left are inches (and feet for cones), the settings and the feed openings are millimetres. Convert before comparing with your fly-out.
  • Skipping the feed check: a row that cannot swallow the largest lump has no capacity, whatever its column says. Jaws and gyratories take about 0.8 × gape; cones what the feed-opening column says.
  • Reading a blank cell as zero, or as "any setting is fine". Blank means the machine is not offered at that setting; for cones the minimum CSS column says why.
  • Taking short tons for tonnes: King's tables are in tonnes per hour, which is what MODSIM's fly-outs show when the units are set to t/h.
  • Choosing a machine three or five times larger than the tonnage without saying so, when a smaller one at a wider setting would do; and the reverse, a machine that "just" makes it with no margin for the surges of a real plant.
  • For screens, typing the table's per cent open area somewhere: Karra wants the aperture and the wire diameter; the open area is what you check against.
