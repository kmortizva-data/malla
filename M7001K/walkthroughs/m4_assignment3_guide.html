Assignment 3, step by step: the wet gravity circuit

What they ask

The sheet is Assignment_3.pdf (Bertil Pålsson, 2010-11-03, two pages). It is a comparing study: a circuit designed for one ore is run on another, and the two materials are, on purpose, described in two different ways. Point by point:

1. The magnetite ore (section 2.1). Pilot tests with Reichert cones on an African iron ore of magnetite and silicates. Table 1: magnetite 5.2, hematite 5.1, gangue 2.7 g/cm³. Table 2, the liberation data: five density classes (float at 2.70; 2.70 to 3.17; 3.17 to 3.94; 3.94 to 5.2; sink at 5.2) with their magnetite content (0, 16.3, 49.8, 85.1, 100 %) and the mass distribution of each class in three size ranges (under 1.07 mm, 1.07 to 2.15 mm, over 2.15 mm). Table 3: the size distribution from 4.29 mm (99 % passing) to 0.27 mm (1.8 %).

2. The hematite ore (section 2.2). Data from test runs on a Canadian banded iron ore reported in Richards, MacHunter, Gates and Palmer, Gravity separation of ultra-fine (−0.1 mm) minerals using spiral separators, Minerals Engineering 13 (1), 2000, pp. 65-77: the sizes and analyses by fraction are to be deduced from Figure 10 of that article; all iron is hematite, the rest gangue.

3. The exercises (section 3). 3.1 build a cone circuit of three elements, rougher cone(s), scavenger cone and cleaner cone, each able to recirculate its middling to a mixer in front of it; feed 60 t/h of the magnetite ore at 60 weight per cent solids; (1) design it to make a concentrate of about 60 % Fe at 75 % recovery and motivate the design; (2) document, especially the material definition. 3.2 rename the job (Edit, Change job name); (3) change the material to the hematite ore, same 60 t/h at 60 % solids; (4) run: is the result realistic, and why this result? Document. 3.3 (5) improve the hematite flowsheet as you find appropriate; document.

4. The report (section 4). For each simulation: the material definitions; the flowsheet with solids and water flows, weight per cent solids and an analysis; a table of model and machine parameters; a discussion of the results.

Note

The course's rule 0.8 applies to the report: language help is allowed and must be declared; the design, the results and the discussion are the group's own. This guide explains the task and how to organise it; the circuit you design is yours.

How the work flows

The sheet calls itself a comparing study, and that is the shape of the work: three loops, three files, two comparisons. A design loop on the magnetite ore, a single run of the frozen circuit on the hematite ore that you judge rather than tune, and an improvement loop on the hematite flowsheet. Four things have different roles:

  • Fixed for every run: the two ore descriptions (Tables 1 to 3 for the magnetite ore, the deduction from Figure 10 for the hematite ore), the feed of 60 t/h at 60 % solids, and the topology of 3.1: rougher, scavenger and cleaner cones, each with its middling recycled to a mixer in front of it.
  • Chosen once: how you read Table 2 into the wizard's liberation classes, what you assume about hematite in the magnetite ore, where the cleaner tail and the scavenger concentrate go (the sheet does not say), and how you convert Figure 10 into size classes and grades.
  • Turned in 3.1: the cut density of each cone, its imperfection, and the middling splits. You stop at about 60 % Fe with 75 % Fe recovery, and the report says why that combination of cut points and recycles gets there.
  • Frozen in 3.2: everything except the material. The job is renamed first, the hematite ore replaces the magnetite ore, the circuit runs once, and you judge the result: realistic or not, and why. There is no tuning here; what the frozen circuit does to fine hematite is the finding.
  • Changed in 3.3: the flowsheet, one thing per copy, each change following from the judgement of 3.2. The comparison at the end runs both ways: the same circuit with two ores (3.1 against 3.2), and the same ore with two circuits (3.2 against 3.3).

In the sheet's words, "design" is the loop of 3.1, "is the result realistic" is a judgement, not a run to repeat, and "improvements that you find appropriate" are judged on whether they follow from that judgement. The two material definitions, side by side, are what explain every difference; that is why item (2) says "document, especially the definition of materials".

What you touch, read and wait for changes from one kind of run to the next:

How to check the target and the realism

  • The target is a grade-recovery point. Read the concentrate's iron grade from the fly-out and compute the iron recovery as iron in the concentrate over iron in the feed (both from the fly-outs, both as Fe, not as magnetite: 60 % Fe is about 83 % magnetite). Log both after every run; the design is the path through those points.
  • What a cone can do is fixed by the liberation table. With magnetite at 5.2 and gangue at 2.7, a density class of 3.94 to 5.2 is about 85 % magnetite: a 60 % Fe concentrate must be made of the two densest classes and little else. If your cut points let the 3.17 to 3.94 class in, the grade cannot reach 60 %; if they keep the 3.94 to 5.2 class out, the recovery cannot reach 75 %. That reasoning is the motivation the sheet asks for.
  • "Realistic" means: would a cone do this to this feed? The article the hematite data come from is about spirals for material below 0.1 mm, because conventional wet gravity units lose their efficiency at fine sizes. If the cone model in MODSIM separates by a density cut point and an imperfection with no size effect, it will separate a fine feed on paper as well as a coarse one. A good simulated result for fine hematite is therefore the thing to explain, not to celebrate: look at the model's parameters and at the size distribution you deduced from Figure 10.
  • An improvement is a machine, not a number. Tightening an imperfection parameter improves every result and proves nothing; the seminar will ask what equipment that parameter stands for. A spiral, a size split ahead of the cones or a desliming stage are improvements that can be defended.

Key points

  1. Design, swap, improve: three loops, three files. The magnetite file must survive untouched (that is why 3.2 starts with Edit, Change job name), the hematite run is judged rather than tuned, and every 3.3 change is a copy with a name. A report with one file and one final number has thrown away two of the three comparisons.
  2. This assignment is about the material definition. Table 2 is a liberation table: for each size range, how the mass splits over density classes, and what each class is made of. MODSIM's Material Definition wizard has a Liberation tab for exactly this (the Exercise 1 guide shows the tab you skipped then). The sheet says "document, especially the definition of materials" because that is where the marks and the mistakes are.
  3. Grade and density are the same thing here. A particle's density fixes its magnetite content: with magnetite at 5.2 and gangue at 2.7, a 3.94 to 5.2 class is about 85 % magnetite, which is the table's number. Iron follows: magnetite is 72.4 % Fe, so a 60 % Fe concentrate is about 83 % magnetite, which tells you which density classes must report to it and which must not.
  4. A Reichert cone is a density separator with a cut point and an imperfection. Rougher, scavenger and cleaner are the same unit with different cut points; the middlings recycle to a mixer in front of the cone is the sheet's way of making you decide what to do with the near-density material. The target, 60 % Fe at 75 % recovery, is a grade-recovery point that the cut points and the recycles must reach together.
  5. The second ore is fine, and cones are not. The article the hematite data come from is about spirals for material below 0.1 mm, because the conventional wet gravity units lose their efficiency at fine sizes. The sheet asks whether the result is realistic and why: the answer is in the size distribution you deduce from Figure 10 and in what a cone does to fine particles.
  6. The rubric. Problem definition and assumptions (how you read Table 2 and Figure 10, where the undecided streams go); flowsheet and material definition (the liberation classes); model selection and parameters (the cone cut points, the middlings recycle); simulation results; discussion (why the hematite result is what it is, and what the improvement did).

How to start, step by step

1

Read Table 2 as MODSIM will need it

ClickRedraw Table 2 on paper: rows are density classes, columns are size ranges, cells are mass fractions within each size range (check that each column sums to 1). Next to each density class write its magnetite content from the table and the iron grade it implies (magnetite × 72.4 %).
ExpectA liberation table you understand: for the −1.07 mm range, for instance, 60 % of the mass is barren gangue and 10 % is pure magnetite.
WhyCriterion 2 is scored on the material definition, and the wizard will ask for these numbers class by class.
The columns are size ranges, not sieve sizes; and the size distribution of Table 3 is a separate input.
2

Define the magnetite ore in the wizard, liberation included

ClickNew project A3_magnetite; size classes from the 4.29 mm top down (Table 3 has nine points); minerals magnetite, hematite and gangue with Table 1 densities; feed 60 t/h at 60 % solids; PSD from Table 3; then the Liberation tab: the grade (density) classes of Table 2 and their mass distribution per size range.
ExpectThe Review tab clean, and a feed whose average iron grade you have computed by hand and can compare with the fly-out.
WhySheet 3.1 (2): document especially the definition of materials.
Hematite is in Table 1 but Table 2 speaks only of magnetite; state what you assume about hematite (zero, or part of the dense classes) and why.

The wizard is the same one as in Assignment 1 (its tabs are shown in the folds, from that assignment's screenshots). This is the assignment where the Grades tab finally does something: Table 2's density classes go there, and the sheet's point is that the second ore is described without them. Open a fold, fill the tab, close it.

Components tab: magnetite and gangue for the first ore; hematite and gangue for the second
  • Material type = Mineral.
  • Magnetite ore (sheet 2.1): two rows, magnetite 5.2 g/cm³ and gangue 2.7 g/cm³ (Table 1). Hematite is in Table 1 too but not in this ore.
  • Hematite ore (sheet 2.2), later, in the renamed job: hematite 5.1 and gangue 2.7; "all iron is hematite, the rest gangue".
  • Magnetic susceptibility κ: leave it. Gravity separation only.
See this window in the GUI
Grades tab: the five density classes of Table 2, with the specific gravity of each class
  • Basis = Mass. The column "Sp. gr. of class" is the reason this tab exists for a gravity circuit: the Reichert cone separates by particle density, and the density of a class follows from its magnetite content.
  • Five classes, from Table 2: float at 2.70; 2.70 to 3.17; 3.17 to 3.94; 3.94 to 5.2; sink at 5.2, with magnetite contents 0, 16.3, 49.8, 85.1 and 100 %. Each row: the magnetite fraction (the gangue is the rest), then Normalize rows. The specific gravity of a class comes from its composition: 1 / (x/5.2 + (1 − x)/2.7) with x the magnetite mass fraction; check that it lands inside the class's density limits (a class of 49.8 % magnetite gives about 3.5, inside 3.17 to 3.94).
  • How much of the feed sits in each class, by size, is not typed here: that is the feed stream's grade distribution (the Streams fold), where Table 2's three size ranges go.
  • The hematite ore has no such table: in the renamed job the grade classes are the two minerals as free particles, one class each, because Figure 10 only gives an iron assay per size fraction. That is the "described in two different ways" of the sheet, and the reason its question 3.2 (4) has the answer it has.
See this window in the GUI
Properties, Liberation and Washability tabs: nothing here
  • Use S-classes: off.
  • Liberation (Andrews-Mika): off. The liberation of this ore is already in the density classes; the Andrews-Mika model is for liberation created by breakage, and nothing is crushed or ground in this circuit.
  • Washability: nothing. The tab is for coal, even though a float-and-sink description is exactly what Table 2 is; for a mineral material that description lives in the grade classes.
See this window in the GUI
Streams tab and the Feed Stream dialog: 60 t/h at 60 % solids, Table 3 as sieve points, Table 2 as grade distribution by size
  • Feed: 60 t/h, percent solids 60 (sheet 3.1: 60 t/h of the magnetite ore at 60 weight per cent solids). The same two numbers for the hematite ore later.
  • Define PSD → sieve points from Table 3: from 4.29 mm at 99 % passing down to 0.27 mm at 1.8 %, typed in micrometres (4.29 mm is 4290 µm).
  • Specify grade distributions: on. For each of Table 2's three size ranges (under 1.07 mm, 1.07 to 2.15 mm, over 2.15 mm) the mass distribution over the five density classes. The wizard asks per size class of your mesh, so every size class inside a range gets that range's distribution; step 1 of this guide prepared the table in that form.
  • Water additions for the cone circuit (wash water, dilution) as water feeds with solids 0, if the cone model does not take its water in the unit window.
  • Decimal separator: type numbers with the separator the field shows and read the row back.
See this window in the GUI
Size_Conv. tab: a fine mesh with a small top size
  • Largest particle size = 0.006 m. Table 3 tops out at 4.29 mm (99 % passing); 6 mm leaves room above it.
  • Number of size classes = 12 to 15. From 6 mm to about 0.1 mm is a factor 60, six √2 steps; twelve classes give a finer picture of the three size ranges of Table 2 without empty classes at the ends.
  • Convergence: Modified Newton, 0.1 %, maximum iterations 60. Every cone recirculates its middling to a mixer in front of it: three loops the solver must iterate.

Review tab: Errors 0, and the summary that tells you the classes are in
  • Minerals 2, grade classes 5 (magnetite ore) or 2 (hematite ore), size classes 12 to 15, largest particle size 0.006 m, S-classes 0.
  • The feed row with data and a grade distribution, no duplicate stream names.
  • Save, and save again under the new name before touching the material for the hematite ore (sheet 3.2: Edit, Change job name).

The sheet describes the circuit in one sentence and leaves two streams undecided. Here is that sentence drawn, with the two open destinations dashed: they go into your assumption list, not into a guess.

3

Sketch the three-cone circuit before building it

ClickCopy the drawing above onto paper and fill the two dashed destinations: cleaner tail to the rougher mixer or to the scavenger, scavenger concentrate to the rougher mixer or to the cleaner mixer. Write the reason for each choice beside it; name every stream.
ExpectA sketch that becomes the report's flowsheet, with the two decisions and their reasons written on it.
Why"Each cone shall have the possibility to re-circulate middling, preferably to a mixer point in front of the cone in question" is the sheet's own topology; the two destinations are its silence, so they are criterion 1 material.
A middling recycled to the same cone builds up a circulating load; watch the mixer's flow in the fly-outs.
4

Build it in MODSIM with the cone model and first-guess cut points

ClickPlace the mixers and the three cone units (the Reichert cone model of MODSIM's list, manual chapter 7); set a cut density for each cone: rougher near the gangue-rich classes, cleaner higher, scavenger lower. Run once and log grade and recovery before touching anything.
ExpectA first concentrate grade and recovery, probably not yet 60 % Fe at 75 %. That pair is the first line of the design log.
WhyYou need a baseline before you can motivate a design.
The cone's imperfection parameter decides how much near-density material is misplaced; a perfect separation is not a realistic one.
5

Reach 60 % Fe at 75 % recovery, and write down why

ClickMove the cut points one at a time and log grade and recovery after each run, as "How to check the target and the realism" describes; use the middlings recycles to trade grade for recovery. Stop at about 60 % Fe with about 75 % Fe recovery and record the settings.
ExpectThe target point, and a paragraph on why this combination of cut points and recycles reaches it (which density classes go where).
WhySheet 3.1 (1): design the circuit and motivate the choice.
Recovery here is iron recovery; check that the fly-out's analysis is Fe and not magnetite.
6

Document the magnetite case and save it twice

ClickFly-outs with solids, water, % solids and Fe on every stream; the parameter table; save as A3_magnetite.PAK, then Edit, Change job name to A3_hematite and save again.
ExpectTwo identical projects with different names.
WhySheet 3.2 starts with the rename; the magnetite result must not be overwritten.
Rename before you touch the material; an "undo" does not exist in the wizard.
7

Deduce the hematite ore from Figure 10 of the article

ClickOpen the article (module 4 material). Read Figure 10: the size fractions and their iron assays (or Fe distribution). Build the size distribution and the Fe grade per size class; assume all Fe as hematite (69.9 % Fe) and the rest gangue; convert to a liberation description consistent with how you did the magnetite ore.
ExpectA table of size classes with per cent passing and % hematite per class, and a note on every reading you had to estimate from the figure.
WhyThe sheet says the data "may be deduced" from the figure: the deduction, and its stated uncertainty, is part of criterion 1.
The article's material is below 0.1 mm; make sure your size classes reach down to a few microns, or the fines vanish.
8

Run the hematite ore through the magnetite circuit, once

ClickIn A3_hematite, replace the material definition with step 7's (60 t/h, 60 % solids), keep every cone setting, run once. Tabulate grade, recovery and the size distribution of every stream next to the magnetite run.
ExpectA result you must judge, not tune: is the grade-recovery point realistic for a cone circuit fed with sub-0.1 mm hematite, and what in the size distribution explains it?
WhySheet 3.2 (4) asks exactly those two questions; the comparison of the same circuit on two ores is the first of the study's two comparisons.
If the simulation gives a fine result, ask whether the cone model knows the particles are fine; a density model without a size effect will separate anything, and that is a discussion point, not a success.
9

Improve the hematite flowsheet (sheet 3.3)

ClickChange one thing at a time from the diagnosis, in a copy with its own name: a unit suited to fine material (the article's spirals, for instance), a size split ahead of the cones, a desliming, a different recycle. Compare each copy with the 3.2 run; keep the one you can defend.
ExpectA documented improved circuit and the before-and-after comparison: the same ore with two circuits.
WhySheet 3.3 (5): improvements "that you find appropriate", judged on whether they follow from part 4.
An improvement that only tightens the imperfection parameter is a fudge; the examiner will ask what machine that parameter stands for.
10

Write the report from the sheet's list

ClickFor each of the three simulations: the material definition (tables and the liberation description), the flowsheet with solids, water, % solids and Fe, the parameter table, the discussion. Add the AI-use declaration.
ExpectA report where the two material definitions sit side by side and the discussion explains the difference in results from them.
WhyThe sheet's report list is the marking scheme.
The seminar question will be "why did the hematite run give that result?": two group members should be able to answer with the size distribution in hand.

Deliverables checklist

Common mistakes

  • Tuning the hematite run until it looks good: 3.2 asks for a judgement of the frozen circuit, and a tuned run hides the finding.
  • Reading Table 2 as sieve data, or summing the rows instead of the columns.
  • Defining the magnetite ore without the Liberation tab: the cones then see fully liberated particles and the design is meaningless.
  • Confusing % Fe with % magnetite in the target; 60 % Fe is 83 % magnetite.
  • Forgetting the rename, so the hematite run overwrites the magnetite result.
  • Size classes for the hematite ore that stop at 0.1 mm: the whole ore then sits in one class.
  • Calling a good simulated result for fine hematite realistic because the model produced it.
  • Leaving the cleaner tail and the scavenger concentrate wherever MODSIM's default put them, without a line in the assumption list.
