Exercise 8: investigation of gravimetric models

What they ask

The sheet is Ex_8.pdf (Bertil Pålsson, 2010-11-03, one page). Professor King worked for a long time on coal cleaning by density, so MODSIM carries a very large number of gravity machines, most of them rarely heard of, and many models. Most of those models belong to one of three families, which the sheet names:

  1. Lynch's equation, for normal gravity and coarse material:
Rc(x)=eαx−1eαx+eα−2
  1. Gottfried and Jacobsen's model, with a known cut density, for fine material or increased gravity.
  2. King and Jukes' model, for fine material or increased gravity, where the density of the medium is the input.

The models of a machine may combine these, although it is not common for one machine to be computed with both Gottfried and Jacobsen and King and Jukes. The sheet warns that the sheer number of machines and models makes this part of the course hard to take in, and the purpose of the exercise is to find out which machine and model combinations exist, and what limits and prerequisites they have.

The exercise (2). In MODSIM, take machines from the group "Gravity Separator" and arrange them in groups, each on a flowsheet of its own. The suggested groups are bath and static units, normal gravity equipment, high gravity equipment and wet gravity (mechanical) equipment. Then:

  1. Place the machines on the flowsheet with ample distances between them.
  2. Put the name of each machine underneath it and save the flowsheet, ignoring the program's complaints.
  3. Open the unit model parameters and find out, for each machine, which models it offers, which family each model belongs to, and whether it has limits or prerequisites.
  4. Sum it up in a table for each group.

How the work flows

This exercise has no feed, no material and no run. It is a survey, and the deliverable is four tables. Each group goes through the same two passes. The first pass draws the group: the machines on a flowsheet of their own, each with its name underneath, saved to a file. The second pass reads every machine of that file: its list of models, and each model's form with its fields and defaults. What you write down for each model is its family (Lynch, Gottfried and Jacobsen, King and Jukes, stratification, or something else), what it needs from you, and what material it was made for.

  • Fixed: the four groups of the sheet, and the fact that nothing is run.
  • Chosen once: which machine goes in which group. The suggested solution's grouping is the one used here.
  • Read, machine by machine: the models the machine offers, with the default first; for each model its fields and their defaults, and the family it belongs to.
  • Compared at the end: the four tables, and what has changed since the course's table of 2010.

How to check the tables

  • Every machine has at least one model. The puddle pan is the exception: it offers PAN1, and GUI 0.9.12 has no form for it, which is what the 2010 table already said ("Model missing!").
  • The default model is listed first, and it is the one a machine takes when you place it. Exercises 9 and 10 showed why that matters: the Batac jig opens with BATJ, not the stratification model.
  • Machine and model are not the same thing. One machine can offer many models (the dense medium vessel offers eleven), and one model can serve several machines (SJIG is offered on both jigs and on the vessel).

Why (the engineering)

Three kinds of gravity separator. King (chapter 7) sorts them by what makes the separating environment. In manufactured-medium separators, such as the dense medium drum and the dense medium cyclone, the particles float or sink in a suspension made to a chosen density. In autogenous-medium separators, such as the jig, the sluice, the Reichert cone and the water-only cyclone, the bed of particles itself creates the environment by stratifying. A third group, the spiral and the shaking table, relies on more complex flows.

The partition curve is the common language. Whatever the machine, a model says, for each particle density, what share goes to the heavy product. That curve has a cut density ρ50 where the share is one half, a steepness measured by the ecart probable EPM (half the density band between the 25 % and 75 % points), and in real machines some short circuit of feed straight to one product (King, sections 7.2.1 to 7.2.3). The families differ in how they build that curve.

  • Lynch: a curve of fixed shape around a cut density, with one sharpness number built in. Variants keep the normalised cut-point shift and the imperfection constant, or let the shift change with particle size. Made for coarse coal at normal gravity.
  • Gottfried and Jacobsen: a target density, with a cut that moves with particle size, from coal washing data. Made for fine material and for centrifugal machines.
  • King and Jukes: the medium density as the input, and the cut computed from it with the cut-point shift of a cyclone. Also for fine material and centrifugal machines.
  • Stratification: the jig and cone model of Exercises 10 and 11, which computes a bed rather than applying a curve.
  • Others: an elutriator is really a classifier by settling velocity, and the Knelson concentrator is a regression model for free gold.

Why so many coal models, and why they take one number. Coal is where most gravity data came from, and in GUI 0.9.12 almost every coal washer (CHAN, SLIP, TESK, DREW, NORW, DYNA, BAUJ, WASH and WEMC) asks for a single number, a cut density or a medium density, with a default between 1.4 and 1.5, which is a density for coal. The sharpness of the curve is built into each model and cannot be set in its window. So a coal model on a mineral ore needs at least its cut density moved to the mineral's range.

Before MODSIM: numbers by hand

Lynch's curve with toy numbers. Take a cut density ρ50 = 1.5 and a sharpness α = 30, and compute the share to the sinks at a few densities with the sheet's equation, where x=ρ/ρ50:

At x=1 the equation always gives exactly one half, whatever α is, so ρ50 is the cut. For a large α the curve reaches 0.75 at about x=1+1.1/α and 0.25 at 1−1.1/α, here at densities of 1.555 and 1.445, so

EPM=1.555−1.4452=0.055Ic=EPMρ50=0.0551.5=0.037

A larger α gives a steeper curve and a smaller EPM. That is the only thing the family's single sharpness number does.

The cut-point shift with toy numbers. A dense medium cyclone runs with a medium of 1.40 and cuts at 1.47. Its normalised cut-point shift is

NCPS=ρ50−ρmρm=1.47−1.401.40=0.05

A model that keeps the NCPS constant cuts fine and coarse particles at the same density; one where it grows as the particles get smaller cuts the fine particles higher, which is what King and Jukes' model does, and why in Exercise 9 the same 2.8 on the cyclone gave a cut near 3.04.

Common mistakes

  • Looking for a Run. There is no feed and no material; the flowsheets exist to be read, and the program's complaints are expected.
  • Mixing up machine and model. A machine is a drawing with ports; a model is the calculation behind it. The table needs both columns.
  • Recording only the default. A machine's list can hold several models; the default is just the first.
  • Taking a coal default for a mineral value. The Lynch-type washers open at a cut of 1.4 to 1.5; on a mineral ore that number has to move (Exercises 9 and 10 show what happens when it does not).
  • Expecting the 2010 table to match. Models have been added and removed since; the table of this exercise is the GUI's, with the 2010 row beside it.

Sensitivity analysis

Nothing is run in this exercise, so there is nothing to vary. Two questions test the tables instead:

  • What happens to fine particles on each family? On a Lynch model with a constant NCPS they are cut at the same density as the coarse ones; on SLIP or DREW, where the NCPS changes with size, they are cut at a different density; on Gottfried and Jacobsen and on King and Jukes the cut moves with size by design.
  • What does the same number mean on different models? On DMCY with the selector at 1 the number is the cut; at 2 it is the medium, and the cut lands above it. Exercise 9 runs exactly that comparison.
