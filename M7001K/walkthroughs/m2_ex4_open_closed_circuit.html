Exercise 4: open and closed tertiary circuit

module 2 · King 5.1-5.6 (Table 5.7), 4 (Karra)

What they ask

The sheet is Ex_4.pdf (Bertil Pålsson, 2010-09-22, one page). Secondary-crushed Kiruna D-ore, ρs 4.0 t/m³, 600 t/h, size distribution RRS with d63.2 = 15 mm and n = 0.9 in 20 fractions. Work index for crushing and screening Wi = 12 kWh/t. Model names annotated beside each machine; fly-outs on feed and product discharges.

  1. Open circuit (2.2): a tertiary crushing stage that delivers a feed for primary grinding with d80 5 mm and/or d99 10 mm, without any risk of choked crushing. Crusher and model are your choice; the screen must be a Karra model.
  2. Closed circuit (2.3): the same target, with the crusher discharge screened and the oversize returned.
  3. Analysis (2.4): plot the two feeds to primary grinding; compute the total energy in kWh/t including a primary mill that discharges at d80 0.1 mm; ask whether the machines you chose exist in reality.

How the work flows

The sheet is one page and reads like two designs and a calculation. It is one design loop, run on two circuits, then one analysis that compares them. The loop is always the same: set the crusher, run, read the mill feed, adjust. What differs between the two circuits is only what fixes the top size, and that difference is the whole exercise. Four things have different roles:

  • Fixed for every run: the material (600 t/h, RRS d63.2 15 mm and n 0.9, ρs 4.0, 20 fractions), the target for the mill feed (d80 5 mm and/or d99 10 mm), the Karra screen model, Wi 12 kWh/t, and the primary mill's discharge d80 of 0.1 mm for the energy.
  • Chosen once: the crusher type and model (the sheet leaves them free; a short-head cone, SHHD in MODSIM, is the natural tertiary machine), the screen aperture (10 mm if the screen is to hold the d99), and the size classes (down to 0.05 mm so that a d80 of 5 mm can be read).
  • Turned in the open circuit: the crusher's closed-side setting, one step per run. You read the d80 and d99 of the mill feed and the crusher's feed tonnage, and you stop when the target you can reach is met and that tonnage sits inside a row of King's Table 5.7 at that setting.
  • Turned in the closed circuit: the setting again, and the aperture if needed, never both in one run. The second screen sends its oversize back to the crusher, so the loop must converge before you read anything; you stop when the target holds, the circulating load has settled and the crusher's converged tonnage sits inside a table row.
  • Compared at the end: the two mill-feed curves on one plot, the total energy per tonne of circuit feed for both circuits, and the machine sizes behind them.

In the sheet's words, "design" is the loop on the setting; "without any risk of choked crushing" is a capacity check that MODSIM does not do for you (Exercise 3 showed that its crusher models have no capacity term), so you do it against King's table with the crusher's actual tonnage; and the "and/or" in the target is the sheet being honest: in the open circuit the setting fixes the d80 but the d99 is fixed by the crusher's coarse tail, which goes straight to the mill, so 10 mm is hard to hold; in the closed circuit the second screen fixes the d99 at its aperture by construction, and the setting only has to bring the d80 down. Everything the analysis then says about energy and circulating load follows from that one sentence.

How to check the target, the choke and the energy

  • The target is read on the mill-feed stream. Open its stream report or plot: d80 is the size at 80 % passing, d99 at 99 %. In open circuit say which of the two you met; the d99 is set by the crusher's tail and no setting holds it at 10 mm without pushing the d80 far below 5 mm. In closed circuit the d99 sits at the aperture (a Karra screen lets a little near-size through, so read it, do not assume it) and the setting is judged on the d80 alone.
  • The choke is a table row. Read the crusher's feed tonnage from its fly-out, after convergence in the closed case, and your setting. In King's Table 5.7 (short-head cones) or 5.6 (standard cones) take the column of your setting and go down to the first row whose capacity covers the tonnage: that row is the machine, with its size and power. If no row reaches the tonnage, two crushers in parallel or a more open setting; if the setting is below the row's recommended minimum, the machine cannot run there.
  • Energy on one basis. Bond's law, with the sizes in micrometres, gives the specific energy of a stage in kWh per tonne of the material that passes it:
W=10Wi(1P80−1F80)

The mill term goes from the mill feed d80 to 100 µm at Wi 12 and applies to all 600 t/h. The crusher term comes from the unit report's power divided by the circuit feed of 600 t/h, or from Bond on the tonnes the crusher actually crushes, scaled by that tonnage over 600. Total = crusher + mill, both per tonne of circuit feed; the sheet's "total energy demand in kWh/tonne" is that sum, and mixing kWh per crushed tonne with kWh per tonne of feed in one table is the classic slip.

  • What the crusher product should look like. Rules of thumb, not in the sheet: a cone's product is governed by its setting. With the cycle model, everything coarser than α2⋅CSS (2 × CSS for SHHD) is always broken and everything finer than α1⋅CSS passes untouched, so at a 10 mm setting expect a crusher product almost entirely below 20 mm, and a mixed mill feed whose d80 lands near 5 mm. If the fly-out shows something very different, the setting is in the wrong unit (metres) or the size classes stop too coarse.

How many crushers, and which: reading the uploaded tables

The feed is "secondarily crushed", so the primary and the secondary stages already happened: the sheet asks for one tertiary stage, and the course's suggested solution builds it with a single SHHD in both circuits. One stage can still hold several machines in parallel (a splitter, two or three identical crushers, a mixer) when no single machine takes the tonnage at your setting; machines in series would be new stages, which the sheet does not ask for and which over-crush. The reduction the sheet wants, 25.5 mm to 5 mm, is about 5:1, inside the 4:1 to 6:1 that King gives for tertiary cone crushing (section 5.6.2); three crushers in series would do 1.7:1 each.

The tables on Canvas (Tables5.4-5.7.pdf, the same as King's pages 152 to 155) answer "which machine" in three questions, always in this order:

  1. Does it swallow the feed? For cones, the column "feed opening on the closed side" must exceed the largest lump you send it. For primaries, the largest lump should be at most about 80 % of the gape (King, section 5.6.1).
  2. Does it allow your setting? Table 5.7 has a "recommended minimum CSS" per cavity; a machine cannot run below it. For jaws and gyratories your open-side setting must be a column where the row carries a number.
  3. Does it take your tonnage? Go to the column of your setting and down to the first row whose capacity covers the tonnage the fly-out shows (after convergence in the closed circuit). That row is the machine; its kW is the motor to compare with MODSIM's report.

Worked for this exercise, with the crusher fed by the +10 mm oversize: about 315 t/h in open circuit, about 383 t/h in closed circuit, lumps up to about 82 mm, setting 10 mm wanted. Table 5.7, short-head Symons cones:

Three 4½ ft extra-coarse cones in parallel (98 mm opening, minimum CSS 13, 172 t/h each at 13 mm) are also a valid one-stage answer; three cones one after the other are not.

The sieve tables are the Karra inputs

ScreenMesh.pdf on Canvas lists standard wire mesh: opening, wire diameter and per cent open area for four wire weights. The Karra model (SCR2) asks for exactly those: for a 10 mm cut take the 3/8" row (9.5 mm opening; medium wire 3.76 mm, 51.4 % open area) or the 7/16" row (11.1 mm; 4.12 mm, 53.2 %) and say which. Screens.xls and ScreenFormulas.pdf give the basic capacity by hand, so you can check the deck area MODSIM reports as utilisation.

Why (the engineering)

Open or closed is a question about what the mill downstream can tolerate. A rod mill or an undemanding grind accepts the tail of an open circuit; a primary ball mill wants a controlled top size, so the crusher discharge is screened and the oversize goes round again. The closed circuit buys a guaranteed d99 at the price of a recirculating load: the crusher and the screen must both carry the fresh feed plus what comes back.

Energy is the second axis, and the sheet states the rule of thumb: crush as far as you can, because a millimetre removed in a crusher costs far less than the same millimetre removed in a mill. You will see it in your own numbers: the mill term of the Bond calculation dominates the total, and every reduction of the mill feed d80 takes a visible bite out of it. Throughput and equipment size then decide what is practical, which is why the last question is whether your crusher exists in a catalogue.

"No risk of choked crushing" means the crusher must be able to pass what you feed it at that setting: check the capacity for your setting in King's Table 5.7 (short-head cones) or 5.6 (standard cones) against the tonnage the flowsheet sends it, including the recirculation in the closed case.

Half of the feed is already finer than 10 mm and a third finer than 5 mm. That is the whole design idea in one line: screen first, so that only the coarse half goes to the crusher. The feed d80 is 25.5 mm, the d99 about 82 mm.

Flows if the screen were perfect. A 10 mm cut sends about 300 t/h straight to the mill feed and about 300 t/h to the crusher. A real Karra screen at 10 mm passes a little less (near-size particles stay on the deck), so expect roughly 285 t/h undersize and 315 t/h to the crusher in the open case.

The mill is fifteen to thirty times the crusher; that ratio is the engineering answer to "why crush as far as possible", and it is why a tighter setting pays in the total even though the crusher works harder.

Why

Circulating load. In the closed circuit the crusher feed is the fresh oversize plus the recirculated oversize of the second screen. If the crusher discharge has, say, 20 % coarser than the aperture, the recirculation settles around 20 % of the fresh oversize and the crusher runs at about 1.2 times the open-circuit tonnage. Put that tonnage, not the fresh one, against the capacity table.

Common mistakes

Watch out

Bond with millimetres. The 10 in the Bond formula belongs to sizes in micrometres. With millimetres the mill energy comes out 30 times too large. Convert first: 5 mm = 5000 um, 0.1 mm = 100 um.

Watch out

Choke checked against the fresh feed. In closed circuit the crusher sees the recirculation as well; read the crusher fly-out after the loop converged and check THAT tonnage against Table 5.7.

Also: three crushers in series because the target looked far away (the feed is already secondary-crushed; a stage is one machine or several in parallel); a machine picked for its capacity without checking that its feed opening takes the 82 mm lumps; reading the d99 of the open circuit as if a screen guaranteed it (it is the crusher's tail); treating the first run as the design instead of the first line of the log; an aperture larger than the d99 target (the mill then receives what the screen was meant to hold back); the recycle drawn to the crusher directly instead of through a mixer; forgetting the second screen's model annotation; a size mesh whose finest class is too coarse to resolve d80 = 5 mm; reporting kWh per tonne of crusher feed and per tonne of circuit feed in the same table without saying which.
